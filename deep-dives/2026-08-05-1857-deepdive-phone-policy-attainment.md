# The Lockable-Pouch Study: What Banning Phones Actually Moves, What It Doesn't, and the Outcome to Measure Instead of Test Scores

_Filed 5 August 2026._

> **Thesis:** The paper behind the recent Axios coverage is the best-identified evidence yet on school phone restrictions — six senior economists, 4,607 schools, GPS pings from 40,000 more, a staggered difference-in-differences design, pre-registered. It finds that lockable pouches do exactly one thing cleanly: they cut in-school phone use, hard and durably. Everything downstream is smaller and more interesting. Average test-score effects are close to zero and *precisely* zero — the authors can rule out a gain bigger than about 0.008 of a student-level standard deviation, and their own extrapolation says even a hypothetical perfectly-enforced ban would bound the academic payoff at a rounding error. Well-being drops in the first year and then rebounds to a modest positive by year two, but that recovery is partly confounded with which schools happen to be observed that long. Discipline incidents spike ~16% at adoption and fade. Attendance, self-reported attention and perceived online bullying barely move. The wider literature is genuinely split on scores and more consistent on mental health, and the single most-quoted "phones hurt achievement" study — Beland & Murphy's England paper — is a decade old, measures a pre-TikTok device, and did not replicate in Sweden. The operational reading for anyone setting a school phone boundary: this is a distraction-removal and climate intervention, not a learning intervention. Measure it as such. Reduced in-class use and sustained, gender-disaggregated well-being over *multiple years* — not test scores, and not a reduced-use dashboard read as if it were the outcome.

## 1. What the study actually is

The paper is Hunt Allcott, E. Jason Baron, Thomas Dee, Angela L. Duckworth, Matthew Gentzkow and Brian Jacob, "The Effects of School Phone Bans: National Evidence from Lockable Pouches," NBER Working Paper 35132, April 2026 ([nber.org/papers/w35132](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132)). That author list matters. Allcott and Gentzkow (Stanford) ran the canonical Facebook-deactivation experiment; Dee (Stanford) and Jacob (Michigan) are among the most cited empirical economists of education alive; Duckworth (Penn) is the "grit" psychologist. This is not a graduate-student first pass. It is also not peer-reviewed yet — NBER working papers are circulated for comment — so read it at the strength a careful pre-print earns, which here is considerable.

The intervention is a single, well-defined one: **Yondr lockable pouches**. Students seal their phone in a magnetically locked fabric case on arrival and can't open it until dismissal. Nearly 5,000 US schools use them. The design is a staggered difference-in-differences using the Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) doubly-robust estimator, comparing schools that adopt Yondr to observationally similar never-adopters, with treatment timing taken from Yondr's own administrative sales records for 4,607 schools. Because adopters are visibly different from non-adopters — larger, more urban, more Black and Hispanic, lower baseline scores, poorer neighbourhoods (Table 1) — the whole exercise leans on reweighting to a matched control group and on the absence of differential pre-trends, both of which the paper demonstrates at length. The pre-analysis plan is public ([osf.io/8twh9](https://osf.io/8twh9/overview)).

One disclosure deserves to be stated plainly, because no press write-up will: **Yondr is both the vendor and the data partner.** The authors "thank Yondr for their partnership and for providing access to administrative data," and the paper notes that "at least one co-author has disclosed additional relationships of potential relevance for this research." That is not disqualifying — the outcome data (test scores, GPS, surveys) come from independent sources, and a vendor-funded null result on the vendor's core selling point is close to the opposite of a captured finding. But it belongs in any honest citation.

The outcome architecture is unusually broad, and it is worth naming because it is the part a school can steal directly. Phone use: GPS "pings" from Advan Research (35 million devices; a school-hours-minus-weekend net measure) plus a 108,000-response teacher survey the team fielded themselves. Academic: standardised math and ELA scores harmonised across states. Climate: attendance and disciplinary records. And — the part administrative data can't reach — subjective well-being, classroom attention and perceived online bullying, taken from Panorama Education student surveys. Everything is measured at the school level and reported in school-level standard deviations, then converted to the more familiar student-level units (roughly a third of the school-level number for survey outcomes, half for test scores).

## 2. The one clean result: use falls, a lot

Start with what is not in doubt. Pouch adoption drives a large, persistent fall in in-school phone activity. The GPS proxy shows net phone visits per student during class hours dropping about 0.17 log points in the adoption year and deepening to roughly 0.30 — near a 30% decline — by the second year after ([Figure 4](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132)). The teacher survey is blunter: the share of students reported using phones in class for personal reasons falls **from 61% to 13%** after adoption, an ~80% drop, and teacher satisfaction with the school's phone policy climbs from about 26% to 75%. Both measures are imperfect — GPS can't tell a student's phone from a teacher's, and teacher reports are teacher reports — but they point the same way and the authors treat the GPS figure explicitly as a conservative lower bound.

This is the finding that survives every robustness check, and it is the one a pouch is actually engineered to produce. It is also, as the rest of the paper shows, the least interesting thing you can know about a phone policy — because reduced use is the input, not the outcome.

## 3. The test-score result is a precisely-measured nothing

Here is the number the debate keeps wanting to be non-zero, and isn't. In the pooled sample of middle and high schools, the effect on the combined test-score index is **−0.004 school-level standard deviations, with a standard error of 0.010** (Table 3, Panel A) — statistically indistinguishable from zero and tightly estimated around it. In student-level units that is about −0.002 SD, and the precision lets the authors "rule out improvements larger than approximately 0.008 student-level standard deviations." For scale, Chetty, Friedman and Rockoff's benchmark is that a one-SD better teacher raises scores about 0.14 SD in math; the phone-policy ceiling here is a twentieth of that.

The pooled zero does hide a real split, and it is the honest part of the story rather than a rescue:

| Sample | Combined | Math | ELA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pooled | −0.004 (0.010) | −0.003 (0.012) | −0.002 (0.012) |
| **High school** | 0.025 (0.017) | **0.048*** (0.018)** | 0.011 (0.022) |
| **Middle school** | −0.024** (0.012) | −0.027* (0.014) | −0.017 (0.013) |

_School-level SD; standard errors in parentheses; \*\*\* p<0.01, \*\* p<0.05, \* p<0.10. From Table 3._

High schools get a genuine, statistically significant bump in math — 0.048 school-level SD, about 0.024 student-level, which the authors gloss as roughly a 0.9-percentile-point move or "about one-fifth of the gain associated with a higher value-added teacher." Middle schools go the *other* way, negative and about half the size. The proposed mechanism is substitution: phone use falls more in high schools (where distraction was worse — 72% of high-school teachers call it a major problem versus 33% of middle-school teachers), while younger students with less impulse control may swap phones for other disruptive behaviour, so middle schools "bear the costs of introducing and enforcing the restriction while generating smaller benefits." Suggestive, not settled.

Then the calculation that should end a certain kind of staffroom argument. The authors ask what a *stronger* intervention would buy, assuming effects scale with the size of the phone-use reduction. If pouches cut use by 80% (the survey figure), a hypothetical policy that drove use to zero would still bound the test-score gain at about 0.01 student-level SD. Even under the most conservative reading of the GPS data — a 20% reduction — the bound is 0.04 SD. Their own summary: "even under conservative assumptions about the intensity of the treatment, the potential academic gains from restricting phone use during the school day may be modest." Translated: **there is no version of a phone ban, however total, that shows up meaningfully in aggregate test scores.** If test scores are your success metric, you have chosen a metric the best available evidence says will read as failure no matter how well the policy works.

## 4. The duration finding, pinned — and its confound

The line that travelled from the Axios coverage is that well-being "improves the longer a policy stays in place." It is in the paper, it is the most policy-relevant dynamic result, and it needs both halves of its caveat.

The shape is real and sharp. Averaged across all post-adoption years the well-being effect is negative and insignificant (−0.305 school-level SD, SE 0.201), but that average buries a reversal. In the implementation year, subjective well-being "declines sharply by approximately 0.603 school-level standard deviations and then recovers, becoming positive and statistically significant by t + 2 (approximately 0.485 standard deviations)" — about −0.199 then +0.16 in student-level units ([Figure 6, Panel C](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132)). For context, the same team's Facebook-deactivation experiment moved well-being +0.09 SD; the year-two pouch effect is nearly double that. Discipline follows a matching arc: a ~16% spike in suspensions at adoption that fades toward zero (and turns imprecisely negative) by year two. The authors' conceptual story is adjustment cost then adaptation — early frustration, lost autonomy and altered peer dynamics giving way to more in-person interaction and less always-on social comparison.

Now the confound, which the authors flag themselves and which most coverage will not. On a panel this short, "longer in place" and "different schools" are hard to separate. The early-adopting 2023 cohort — the only one observed out to year two — supplies the recovery; the late-adopting 2025 cohort — observed only at adoption — supplies the sharp initial drop. So the beautiful up-slope could be a genuine dynamic (the same schools feeling better over time) or it could be that the schools which adopted early are simply different from the schools that adopted late. In the paper's own words: "the initial decline in SWB is driven primarily by later-adopting cohorts, while the subsequent recovery reflects earlier-adopting cohorts... the relatively short panel and staggered adoption make it difficult to fully disentangle dynamic treatment effects from cross-cohort heterogeneity." The horizon is also hard-capped: **at most three years of post-adoption data, and well-being observed only to year two.** The duration finding is the right thing to watch and the wrong thing to bank. It is a plausible, partially-supported, actively-caveated dynamic — not an established law that year-two happiness is coming.

Two more climate outcomes came back essentially empty, and the nulls are informative. Self-reported classroom attention: −0.088 SD (SE 0.085), no effect, and with pre-trend problems that make even that untrustworthy. Perceived online bullying: −0.039 SD (SE 0.092), nothing. That second null is not a failure of the policy so much as a category error in expecting it — online bullying does not observe school hours, and a pouch that is unlocked at 3pm cannot touch a device that does its harm at 9pm. Attendance is the same story: a −0.14-percentage-point non-effect against a 93% baseline, with a tiny disruption blip in year one that reverses. This is the cleanest disproof in the paper of the Florida result it most resembles, to which I'll return.

## 5. The wider literature: convergence, contradiction, and one contested classic

A dig that only confirms its own peg is a press release with footnotes. The honest map is messier and more useful, and it splits by *which outcome* you look at.

**On test scores, the literature genuinely contradicts itself — and the contradictions are legible.** The most-cited "phones hurt achievement" finding is Beland & Murphy's "Ill Communication" (Labour Economics, 2016; [LSE CEP DP1350](https://cep.lse.ac.uk/pubs/download/dp1350.pdf)). Using bans across schools in Birmingham, London, Leicester and Manchester and National Pupil Database records back to 2001, they found that "following a ban on phone use, student test scores improve by 6.41% of a standard deviation," with the effect "driven by the most disadvantaged and underachieving pupils" — "students in the lowest quartile of prior achievement gain 14.23% of a standard deviation, whilst, students in the top quartile are neither positively nor negatively affected." That is a real, careful paper and its equity framing (bans as gap-narrowing) is why it shaped a decade of policy. But three things temper it. Kessel, Hardardottir and Tyrefors (2020) ran the same design in Sweden and found **no academic effect**. The NBER team's national US estimate is a precise zero. And — the point nobody makes — Beland & Murphy's data run 2001–2013, when the "phone" in a British teenager's pocket was mostly a texting-and-Snake feature phone, not an algorithmic short-video machine. The device that paper studied and the device a 2026 pouch locks up are not the same object in the same attention ecology. The older positive result and the newer null may not be contradictory at all; they may be measuring different technologies. That reframing is worth more than either headline.

The rest of the score literature scatters exactly as you'd expect from small, context-bound effects: Beneito & Vicente-Chirivella (Spain, 2022) found improved PISA scores and less bullying; Lichand et al. (Brazil, 2026) found gains from a Rio ban; Figlio & Ozek (Florida, 2025; [NBER w34388](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34388)) found a short-run discipline spike then test-score and attendance gains in year two, on the order of 0.9 percentile points in middle and high schools — strikingly close to the NBER team's high-school-math number, but crucially *driven by an attendance improvement the national study could not reproduce*. On the true-experiment end, Sungu, Choudhury & Bjerre-Nielsen (2025) ran an actual RCT of in-class phone collection in Indian higher education and found better grades with no significant well-being change, while Shi & Villarroel (Chile, 2026) found classroom restrictions cut in-class use but didn't measurably move scores. The convergence across all of it: **where phone use and distraction were highest — high schoolers, math, low prior-achievers — small positive effects appear; elsewhere they wash out.** The magnitudes are real and modest; the big headline numbers come from older, smaller, or non-US designs.

**On mental health, the signal is more consistent — with one crucial dissent.** The strongest "bans help" study is Abrahamsson's Norwegian work (forthcoming, Journal of Human Resources; [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4735240)), covering 400+ middle schools with health, family and education registries. Girls who started middle school under a ban saw psychological-symptom GP visits fall by nearly 60% relative to baseline, improved GPA and externally-graded math, and a 4–7 percentage-point higher chance of an academic high-school track; bullying fell for both sexes; effects were largest for low-SES girls; boys showed nothing, which Abrahamsson attributes to their lower baseline phone use. That gendered, mental-health-weighted pattern is echoed in the NBER well-being rebound and in what parents and students *expect* — in the paper's own Gallup survey, both groups are most optimistic about mental-health effects (parents +0.597 on a −1-to-1 scale, students +0.223) and least about test scores.

And then the dissent, which is the single most important study for a school actually deciding, and which is the Birmingham cohort work worth reading in full: the **SMART Schools Study** (Goodyear et al., _The Lancet Regional Health – Europe_, February 2025; [thelancet.com](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanepe/article/PIIS2666-7762(25)00003-1/fulltext)). Across ~30 English secondary schools, comparing restrictive to permissive phone policies among Year 8 and Year 10 pupils, it found **no association between restrictive school policies and mental well-being, anxiety, sleep, physical activity, English and maths attainment, or disruptive behaviour.** What *was* associated with worse outcomes was total daily phone and social-media use. The mechanism is the whole point, and the team's qualitative follow-up (Social Science & Medicine, March 2026; [Birmingham release](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2026/school-phone-bans-or-not-digital-lives-of-students-affect-the-school-day)) spells it out: a school-hours restriction does not reduce a student's 24/7 relationship with the device — it "pushes phone use out of school," where it displaces sleep and exercise, while bullying and social-media conflict "blur into the school day" regardless. Their summary is that phone policies are not a silver bullet for attainment, well-being or behaviour. This is observational, not causal — it compares existing policies rather than a change, so confounding is live — but it is a well-matched cohort and it directly tests the hypothesis the whole movement rests on. It reconciles cleanly with the NBER paper: the pouch reliably cuts the *in-school dose*, and in-school dose turns out to be only loosely coupled to the outcomes people most want to move. Birmingham's other 2026 finding — that managing phones eats [more than 100 hours a week](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2026/managing-phone-use-takes-average-uk-school-more-than-100-hours-each-week) of staff time in the average UK school — is the cost side of the same ledger.

## 6. Why the studies disagree (it isn't just noise)

Three axes explain most of the apparent contradiction, and holding them in view is what separates an evidence map from a slogan.

**The device changed.** A 2005 phone distracted by buzzing; a 2026 phone distracts by design, with a recommendation engine optimising for exactly the attention a classroom needs. Studies on older data (Beland & Murphy) and newer data (NBER, SMART Schools) are partly measuring different machines.

**The population changed.** Effects concentrate where baseline use and vulnerability are highest — low prior-achievers (England), girls and low-SES students (Norway), high-poverty urban US high schools (NBER). A study's headline is largely a function of who was in it.

**The outcome changed.** Test scores, GP visits, self-reported worry, bullying and disruptive behaviour are different constructs with different couplings to phone use. "Phone bans work" and "phone bans don't work" are frequently the same finding measured against different yardsticks. The NBER paper is valuable precisely because it measures many at once and shows them diverging inside a single design.

## 7. What a school should actually measure

This is the load-bearing part, and the evidence above turns it from an opinion into a specification. The recurring failure in this debate is a measurement failure: schools either point at a reduced-use dashboard and declare victory, or point at flat test scores and declare defeat. Both are wrong for the same reason — a phone boundary is an intervention on the *conditions* for learning and on school *climate*, not on learning itself. It buys back attention and a calmer social floor; teaching is what converts those into achievement. Measure the thing the policy actually does, guard against the harms it can cause, and never credit the pouch with the learning or blame it for scores it was never going to move.

Concretely, four tiers:

- **Leading indicator — does the boundary do its one job?** Track teacher-reported in-class personal phone use (the NBER survey item — 61%→13% is the benchmark for a policy that is actually enforced), enforcement consistency, and the staff time the policy consumes. This is the input, and it is the one thing the evidence guarantees a real policy will move. Watching it tells you the policy is *live*; it does not tell you it is *working*.

- **The durable payoff — well-being, measured properly.** A validated, repeated subjective-well-being or belonging instrument, run over **two to three years, not one**, and disaggregated by gender (Norway and the NBER well-being arc both concentrate in girls). Pair it, if you possibly can, with some read on *total* daily use — because the SMART Schools result is that a school-hours boundary which merely relocates use after the bell will not move well-being. If you measure only in-school use, you will not know whether you cut the dose or just moved it.

- **The harm guards — read these to catch damage, not to certify success.** Disciplinary incidents (expect a year-one spike of ~15%; pre-commit to reading it as adjustment, not failure); the substitution question, especially for younger students (are phones being swapped for other disruption?); and middle-school academic results specifically, where the sign in the national data is negative. Do *not* set a test-score target as the policy's justification — the evidence says it will not be met and the policy will be wrongly killed.

- **The horizon.** Pre-register a three-year evaluation with an explicit "year one will look worse on discipline and mood" expectation baked in. A school that judges at the end of year one will euthanise a policy that pays off in year two — which is the exact shape of the only positive well-being result in the literature.

The sentence worth carrying out of all of this: **the outcome to define before you start is not "did scores go up" but "what is the boundary for."** If the answer is reclaimed attention and a calmer room, then measure attention and the room — sustained, disaggregated, over years — and let the test scores be a harm-guard in the background rather than the scoreboard.

## 8. The reading for international schools

Three things make a selective international school a poorer fit for the headline than a casual reader would assume, all of which sharpen rather than soften the case for measuring carefully.

**The phone is not the only always-on device — and in a 1:1 setting it may not even be the main one.** Many international schools are laptop-carrying, cloud-productivity environments. The NBER authors explicitly flag that when phones vanish, attention can shift "to digital distractions that are not blocked, such as accessing video or social media sites on laptops." A pouch at a school where every student has an open browser tab addresses a *slice* of the distraction surface, and possibly the smaller slice. Any phone-boundary conversation that doesn't also name the school-issued device is measuring the wrong device — and this is precisely where Ed Tech Coaches add value, by insisting the two be considered together.

**The studied populations are not this school's population.** The NBER sample skews urban, higher-poverty, lower-baseline-scoring US public schools; Abrahamsson's effects run through low-SES Norwegian girls; Beland & Murphy's through low prior-achievers. A selective, high-income international intake is the group in which these studies find the *least* — the top-quartile students Beland & Murphy found "neither positively nor negatively affected." Both the academic near-null and the well-being benefit may transfer weakly, because the students who drive the benefits elsewhere are a smaller share here. That is not an argument against a boundary; it is an argument against importing anyone else's effect size as a forecast for your own.

**This is a learning-change and measurement question, not a device-management one.** The temptation in year one will be to run a reduced-use report and call it done, or to wait for exam data and call it pointless. The contribution Ed Tech Coaches can make is to define — before the policy starts — what "working" means, which validated instrument tracks it, over what horizon, disaggregated how, and with which harm-guards. Getting that specification right is worth more than the policy choice itself, because the policy choice without the specification is exactly how both sides of this debate have been talking past the evidence for a decade.

## A note on sources

The primary studies were read directly wherever possible. NBER Working Paper 35132 was read in full — all main tables and figures — and every coefficient, standard error and quoted sentence attributed to it is transcribed from that text; the same is true of Beland & Murphy's CEP Discussion Paper 1350 and the abstract of the Florida NBER paper (w34388). The SMART Schools papers (The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, February 2025; Social Science & Medicine, March 2026) and Abrahamsson's Norwegian study (forthcoming, Journal of Human Resources) were sourced through the publishing institutions' own releases, PubMed and the working-paper repositories, which agree on design and headline figures; the journal full texts may sit behind institutional access, so the within-study confidence intervals for those nulls and for the girls-only Norwegian estimates were not read line by line. Professor Goodyear's "silver bullet" characterisation is paraphrased rather than quoted, because the versions reachable were second-hand summaries. And one thing remains genuinely open rather than settled: whether Beland & Murphy's 2016 result reflects a causal effect or residual student sorting — the authors run intake and placebo checks, later replications cut against generalising it, and the most likely reconciliation offered here, that the device itself has changed, is an inference and is flagged as one.
