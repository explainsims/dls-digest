# Deep dive: NYC DOE AI Guidance

_Filed 3 May 2026._

> **Lede:** NYC's preliminary AI guidance offers a usable traffic-light scaffold but ducks the hard questions on student use, vendor vetting, and instructional efficacy.

## What it is

NYC Public Schools' **preliminary AI guidance**, published 24 March 2026 under Chancellor Aviles-Ramos and Deputy Chancellor Kamar Samuels. *Preliminary*, not final — the consultation window is **45 days, closing 8 May 2026** at schools.nyc.gov/AI. A comprehensive **Playbook** is promised for June 2026.

NYCPS is the largest school system in the US — ~900k students, 78k teachers, ~1,800 schools — so whatever it codifies tends to ripple. Same district that banned ChatGPT in January 2023 then reversed in May 2023; the three-year delay since is itself part of the story. The DOE says "over 1,000 stakeholders" fed the draft.

## What it actually says

The guidance organises AI use into a **traffic-light framework** scoped to *staff* uses; student-facing use is conspicuously thinly drawn.

**Red — never, no exceptions.**
- AI may not make or materially inform decisions about: grading, promotion, graduation, placement, eligibility, discipline, counseling, or behavioral monitoring.
- AI may not write IEPs or 504 plans.
- Student personal data may never be used to train AI models or be entered into a tool that hasn't been vetted.

**Yellow — proceed with caution, human oversight required.**
- Using AI to spot trends in student data.
- Generating translations or transadaptations for multilingual learners — *must* be reviewed by a certified bilingual/ENL teacher before student-facing use.
- Adapting materials for students with disabilities — IEP-team review required.
- "Critical communications" — AI drafts are not final drafts.

**Green — encouraged.**
- Brainstorming, lesson and unit ideation.
- Organising information, scheduling, formatting.
- Initial drafts of *non-sensitive* communications to families and staff.

**The vetting spine: ERMA.** The Enterprise Request Management Application is NYCPS's pre-existing 10-step vendor review covering OneTrust assessments, data-processing agreements, IT security, and OTI cloud architecture review. Any tool that touches student data must clear ERMA *before* use. The DOE concedes openly that **ERMA today checks privacy and security only — not algorithmic bias, not equity impact, not instructional effectiveness**. Those criteria are flagged as future Playbook work.

**Standing principle (paraphrased throughout):** AI supports, never replaces, professional judgment. The teacher of record owns the grade; the IEP team owns the IEP; the dean owns the discipline.

## Who's saying what

**The DOE line.** Aviles-Ramos and Samuels frame the guidance as an "equity-centered" floor with the Playbook to follow. Talking point: no decision *about* a child gets outsourced to a model.

**Chalkbeat.** Treats the guidance as substantive on the red column but evasive on the yellow — most consequential teacher decisions live in the gray, and "use judgment" isn't pedagogical scaffolding.

**Class Size Matters / Leonie Haimson.** The sharpest sustained critic. Argues ERMA is a checkbox exercise that already failed (cites the >1M-student NYC data breach and continued commercial use of student data). Says the AI Working Group was "stymied, sidelined and stonewalled," refused even the *names* of AI products currently in use. Backs a **two-year moratorium**; a parent petition reportedly has ~2,000 signatures, plus a Fairplay-organised national letter (260 orgs) calling for a five-year moratorium on student-facing AI.

**UFT / MORE caucus.** UFT itself has been quiet — no MOA on AI as of the last contract, and a $23m AI partnership with Microsoft/OpenAI critics keep pointing at. The dissident **MORE** caucus turned out at the 29 April PEP; the 1 May follow-up ran seven hours with parents demanding a moratorium.

**Council/oversight.** Comptroller Mark Levine has pushed back on specific reading-AI proposals. The DOE *withdrew* its AI-themed high school proposal in late April — political winds shifted between the March release and the May close.

**Ed-tech commentariat.** Watters — predictably hostile, AI as the latest century of teaching-machine hype. Williamson (Edinburgh) — "turbocharged fast AI policy experiments" and missing independent-evaluation infrastructure. Mollick — opposite pole but portable frame: not one-and-done, change-management approach, iterate. AI Snake Oil — no NYC-specific take found, but their general line on automated decision systems maps onto the red column.

## What an international school could borrow

Filtering for what's portable to a Vietnam-based, IB+AP, Google-Apple international school:

**Highly portable.**
- **The traffic-light scaffold itself.** Three buckets, plain language, a one-pager — that's the right altitude for a staff-facing document. The structure can be reused verbatim and the cells re-populated.
- **The "no decisions about students" red line.** Grades, promotion, university recs, behaviour referrals, learning-support plans — keep these out of AI without hedging. This is the cleanest lift, and it's a straight line to IB academic-honesty obligations.
- **Human-in-the-loop on translations.** Genuinely useful for a multilingual cohort. The rule "AI translation is never final draft for parent comms" should be policy on day one — Vietnamese, Korean, Mandarin parent communities will spot machine-translated tone and content drift faster than expected.
- **Required vendor review before student data touches a tool.** A school will need its own version of ERMA — even a five-question form is better than nothing. Tag it to the Google Workspace admin so adoption is enforceable.

**Partially portable.**
- **The yellow column** is where any adopting school will need to do the work NYC ducked. IB has its own AI-and-academic-integrity guidance (updated 2024–25); College Board AP has a separate posture. The yellow column needs both layered in.
- **Public consultation as a process.** Worth borrowing the *idea* — a parent/student/staff feedback window — even if the constituency is smaller and the politics are different.

**NYC-specific, do not lift.**
- The political theatre (PEP meetings, comptroller statements, union proxy fights) — none of this is the operating environment of an international school.
- The 1,800-school scale-out problem. A single-campus school can move faster and more granularly than a megadistrict.

**A practical end-state**, by the start of the 2026-27 school year: (1) a one-page traffic-light document approved by leadership, (2) a vendor-vetting checklist tied to the Google Workspace admin, (3) a translation-review protocol with the EAL/world-languages team, and (4) IB- and AP-specific student-facing guidance that lives separately from the staff document.

## Sceptic's corner

Three things that are weak or hand-wavey in the NYC document, and that are worth expecting to see flagged in the consultation responses:

1. **Yellow is a dumping ground.** "Use careful judgment with adult oversight" is not a policy — it's an aspiration. Most teacher-AI interaction lives here, and the document offers no examples, no decision tree, no PD framework. Mollick's right that policies need iteration; the question is whether the June Playbook actually fills this in or just relabels it.

2. **ERMA is doing more work than it can carry.** The DOE itself admits ERMA doesn't cover algorithmic bias, equity, or instructional effectiveness — i.e., the three things AI tools are *most likely* to fail on. A privacy/security pass is necessary but nowhere near sufficient, and Class Size Matters' point about the previous breach is fair. The take-away: don't pretend a vendor checklist is curriculum review.

3. **Student use is barely addressed.** The document is built for *staff*. What students may and may not do with AI on assessments — the question every teacher is actually asking — is mostly absent. Compare with IB's much more explicit posture. Expect the June Playbook to either fill this in seriously or get hammered.

A fourth, quieter concern: timing. March release, late-April climbdown on the AI high school, 8 May consultation close, June Playbook — a compressed cycle for a system this size. Williamson's "turbocharged fast AI policy experiments" fits.

The lesson for any other school adopting from this template is the inverse: **go slower than NYC, write less than NYC, and put more work into the yellow column than NYC has been willing to.**
