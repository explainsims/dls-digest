# Fifty-Two Papers and a Conditional Verdict: Reading Stanford's AI Evidence Review Properly

_Filed 15 November 2026._

> **Lede:** Stanford's SCALE Initiative more than doubled its count of strong causal studies on generative AI in education, from 20 to 52, and its headline survived the larger sample: AI looks better when students use it to learn than when they use it to produce the work, and neither use reliably improves learning. Read underneath the summary and the picture gets both firmer and narrower. The firmest results are the warnings. The positive results are mostly short, mostly postsecondary, often in science and computer science, and frequently preprints, several of them written or paid for by the companies whose tools were tested. No study in the base shows a gain surviving more than about a week after the tool is taken away. For a secondary school the review is less a permission slip than a test specification: decide what a student should be able to do once the assistance is taken away, then check it ([SCALE, 10 November 2026](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/evidence-base-review/evidence-base-ai-k12-november-review)).

## 1. What SCALE is, and how this review was built

SCALE is a unit of the Stanford Accelerator for Learning at Stanford's Graduate School of Education. Its AI Hub for Education runs a Research Repository that it describes as "Striving to be the comprehensive collection of academic research on generative AI in preK-12 education," sorting papers into descriptive studies, impact studies and systematic reviews, and updated monthly; the page showed 1,754 papers when I checked ([SCALE Research Repository, updated November 2026](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/repository)). The November review is an interim update to the March 2026 review, by Seth Walker, Lily Fesler, JP Martinez Claeys, Chris Agnew and Susanna Loeb, released ahead of a fuller spring 2027 edition. Its acknowledgements list the funders as the Walton Family Foundation, the Overdeck Family Foundation and Google.org ([SCALE November review PDF, 10 November 2026](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20November%202026%20Review.pdf)). That last name matters later.

The method is a funnel. The repository "draws primarily from arXiv," plus papers researchers and practitioners suggest. Of 868 papers added for the period October 2025 to June 2026, 201 were randomised trials or quasi-experiments, 97 went to human review and 32 met the bar. The first filter is a language model: the team prompts Gemini 3.0 Flash to score each paper from one to three on eight dimensions taken from the What Works Clearinghouse Handbook (construct validity, outcome reliability, confounding, baseline equivalence and so on); a paper is dropped only if it scores one on any dimension, and 81 of 185 passed. Sixteen more were added by hand. Stanford researchers then applied a WWC-based questionnaire, with two further reviewers for borderline cases ([SCALE November review PDF](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20November%202026%20Review.pdf)). The scope also narrowed: this update covers generative AI only, dropping the non-generative transcript-analysis and essay-scoring tools the March review admitted.

Each new finding is labelled as supporting, conflicting with, or new relative to March. That device is honest and useful. It also means the review is structured as a running commentary on its own earlier conclusions rather than a pooled estimate: there is no meta-analysis, no overall effect size and, for most studies, no magnitude in the summary at all.

## 2. What the 52 studies actually are

The tone has shifted, not the substance. In March the executive summary said research on AI's impact in K–12 was "still extremely limited" ([SCALE March 2026 review PDF](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20Report.pdf)). In November the evidence is "growing but remains sparse," and the findings "should not be treated as broadly generalizable across U.S. K–12 settings" ([SCALE November review PDF](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20November%202026%20Review.pdf)).

The composition explains why. Of the 32 new papers, 28 study student-facing tools and four educator-facing ones; the combined base has 42 student studies and 12 educator studies. Among student studies, 24 are postsecondary and 16 K–12, and of the 28 new ones, 17 are postsecondary. "Science & Technology" outcomes jumped from 3 to 16 papers, and seven of the 13 new ones in that category are computer science or programming. Several samples are Prolific crowdworkers or lab volunteers; Gardella et al. has 22 programmers, Jain et al. 20 students, Chen et al. 24. Many interventions are a single session. The well-powered K–12 studies are a short list: Oreopoulos et al. with 6,997 U.S. middle schoolers, the two Coursemojo grade 6 studies (2,045 and 2,771 students), Chung et al. with 770 Taiwanese high schoolers over a five-month Python course, the Gemini trial in Sierra Leone with 1,423 analysed, and Bastani et al. with roughly a thousand Turkish high schoolers ([SCALE November review PDF, Appendix A](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20November%202026%20Review.pdf); [SCALE March 2026 review PDF](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20Report.pdf)).

By my count, studies of high-school-age students in ordinary classrooms number fewer than a dozen across both reviews. That is the evidence that most closely resembles a Grade 9–12 classroom, and much of it sits outside the U.S. and outside any setting quite like an international school.

## 3. Support versus produce: the line, study by study

The distinction everyone will quote is defined plainly in the report: AI for learning support means "explanations, hints, practice, or feedback"; AI for producing work means students use it to generate "all or part of an answer or product, replacing work they would otherwise do themselves" ([SCALE November review PDF](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20November%202026%20Review.pdf)). The production side is the better-evidenced half, and it is a warning.

The anchor remains Bastani et al. in PNAS. Nearly a thousand Turkish high school maths students practised with one of two GPT-4 tutors. Practice grades rose — "48% improvement in grades for GPT Base and 127% for GPT Tutor" — but once access was removed, GPT Base students did worse than those who never had it, a "17% reduction in grades." The guardrailed tutor, which withheld answers and used teacher-provided guidance, largely avoided the harm; the authors' explanation is that students used unguarded GPT-4 as a "crutch" ([Bastani et al., PNAS, published 25 June 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)). Note what the guardrails bought: protection from harm, not a demonstrated gain on the unassisted exam.

The new studies push in the same direction. Barcaui's 85 Brazilian business undergraduates using unguided ChatGPT spent 3.2 hours preparing a presentation rather than 5.8, and the traditional-resources group scored higher on a surprise conceptual test 45 days later. Chen et al.'s beginner programmers produced working code with ChatGPT without understanding it ([SCALE November review PDF, Appendix A](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20November%202026%20Review.pdf)). The most striking addition is not an experiment but a natural one. Rismanchian et al. used a ten-year panel of 3.2 million ALEKS maths interactions, comparing text-based word problems (easy to paste into a chatbot) with interactive graph problems (hard to paste). Time on the susceptible problems fell after ChatGPT's release; "high-schoolers show 31.3%" decline, against 9.0% for middle schoolers and no detectable change in Grade 5. On randomly assigned proctored retention items, the odds of a correct answer fell 25%; on unproctored assessment the same estimator showed a large increase in the opposite direction ([Rismanchian et al., arXiv, 20 May 2026](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2605.21629)). That pair of results is the whole problem in miniature: unproctored work looks better while proctored knowledge gets worse. It is also the one result in the base that is specifically about high schoolers at scale.

The support side is where the November review "conflicts" with March. Contractor and Reyes ran a proctored, in-person experiment with 211 U.S. undergraduates studying an unfamiliar topic and writing an essay with or without AI. "AI access raises test scores, and a smaller gain persists a week later." The gains "remain for students who use AI as a tutor ('augmentation') and fade for students who have AI write for them ('automation')" ([Contractor & Reyes, arXiv, first posted 9 July 2026, revised 4 October 2026](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2607.08849)). SCALE is careful that this split is exploratory and "does not establish that how students used AI caused the differences in learning." Idan and Anand found ChatGPT raised knowledge scores among 179 mostly engineering undergraduates, with gains predicted not by GPA or prior knowledge but by "AI Interaction Competence," the ability "to elicit, filter, and verify model outputs" ([Anand & Idan, arXiv, 18 May 2026](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2605.18143)).

Then the complications. Cao et al. found that U.S. college students given the answer and an explanation immediately outperformed those given layered hints first, which runs against the Socratic orthodoxy. Fütterer et al. found that adding motivational and cognitive-strategy prompts to a GPT-4o chatbot did nothing measurable for 241 German students in grades 7–9 relative to standard ChatGPT. SCALE's conclusion is that "educational AI tools are not inherently more effective than general-purpose AI," and that what mattered in Bastani's tutor was teacher-provided content and withheld answers, not the label ([SCALE November review PDF](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20November%202026%20Review.pdf)). Oreopoulos et al., the largest K–12 trial in the base, found an AI tutor slowed practice and did not improve overall delayed learning; the most encouraging result came only when AI was embedded in a mastery rule requiring three correct in a row. The paper's framing is that LLM tutoring helps "but only if students use them to reason through mistakes rather than avoid effort" ([Oreopoulos, Liut, Sungu & Low, NBER Working Paper 35621, August 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35621)).

So the support/produce line is real but it is not a product category. It is a description of what the student's mind is doing during the task, and the evidence says task structure determines it more reliably than tool branding.

## 4. Persistence, transfer and the satisfaction trap

The longest positive follow-ups in the base are three days to one week (Heldreth et al.; Contractor & Reyes). The longest follow-up of any kind that I found, Barcaui's 45 days, favoured the no-AI group. SCALE states it directly: "the evidence does not yet show whether learning gains persist over longer periods" ([SCALE November review PDF](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20November%202026%20Review.pdf)). Transfer to unpractised material is scarcer still: Oreopoulos et al. built it into their delayed test and the gains concentrated on practised material; the March review's Google–Eedi tutoring study in five UK schools reported better transfer to novel topics, with 165 students ([SCALE March 2026 review PDF](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20Report.pdf)).

The satisfaction finding is the most practically useful in the report. Students in Gardella, Henze and Taneja reported better experiences or lower workload with no learning gain; high school and college students rated AI feedback clearer and more motivating than teacher feedback while learning about the same (Meyer et al. 2026; Zhao et al. 2026). March already had the starkest example: Year 10 students in England "preferred the AI chatbot and perceived it as more helpful, despite it leading to the lowest learning performance" (Kreijkes et al., in [SCALE March 2026 review PDF](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20Report.pdf)). A student survey, on this evidence, measures the experience accurately and the learning hardly at all.

## 5. Educators: time saved, motivation spent

The educator section is small (12 studies) but contains the review's most uncomfortable new result. Sungu, Lira and Duckworth randomised 193 teachers in a Turkish private school chain to a GPT-4o teaching assistant aligned to the national curriculum, covering 2,816 students in grades 5–12. Average exam scores did not change; student intrinsic motivation fell, and scores fell for students of lower-performing teachers ([SCALE November review PDF](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20November%202026%20Review.pdf)). SCALE's repository entry puts the motivation effect at 0.11 standard deviations, though that entry is itself an AI-drafted summary ([SCALE repository entry, July 2026](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/repository/generative-ai-can-harm-teaching)). The Hechinger Report's account adds that the draft is not peer reviewed, that the control group was free to use other AI tools, and that researchers did not observe classrooms or analyse the materials, so the mechanism (weaker teachers accepting drafts unrevised) is a hypothesis ([Hechinger Report, 13 July 2026](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-ai-in-teaching/)). SCALE notes teachers mainly generated materials "that were accepted with minimal revision."

Against that sit the narrower wins: AI drafts raised the chance teaching assistants gave feedback by about 11 percentage points (Mahinpei et al.); AI suggestions roughly doubled feedback volume without extra time, though students' later post-test did not improve (Lu et al.); AI summaries of student progress raised assignment starts and completions but not scores (Lim et al.); and the older NFER trial found ChatGPT cut lesson-preparation time without a detected quality loss (Roy et al., 2024). The pattern rhymes with the student evidence. Targeted help on a bounded task looks fine. Broad delegation of the substance looks risky, and it may be riskiest for those with the least expertise to check what comes back.

## 6. Preprints, sponsors and machinery

The report is candid that the repository "consists primarily of preprints from arXiv, which have not undergone peer review." Counting the reference list myself: of the 32 new papers, 20 are listed as preprints or working papers (arXiv, SSRN, Research Square, NBER, one self-hosted working paper); three more are grey literature — a Google technical report and two LXD Research reports on Coursemojo; nine carry a journal or peer-reviewed conference venue ([SCALE November review PDF, References](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20November%202026%20Review.pdf)). SCALE's mitigation is that at least two human researchers review each included paper. That is quality screening, not peer review, and it does not catch what peer review sometimes catches later.

How much does it matter? Less than critics say for well-identified trials — an NBER randomised field experiment with 6,997 students is not weak because a journal has not stamped it — and more than enthusiasts admit for the small, short, single-session studies that make up much of the positive column. The bigger issue is provenance. Two of the positive K–12-relevant results come from the vendors whose products were tested. Heldreth et al. (58 U.S. high schoolers, one 90-minute lab session, 3–7 day recall) was written mostly by Google employees; the paper's conflict statement names Google as "the developer of Learn Your Way," states "This study was funded by Google LLC," and describes the tool as "an experimental research platform" ([Heldreth et al., Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 10 March 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2026.1783117)). The Sierra Leone result, "an intent-to-treat effect of +0.258 SD," is a preregistered trial reported in a Google technical report ([LearnLM Team, Google & Fab AI, 15 May 2026](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/LearnLM/learnLM_sierraleone_may26.pdf)). Google.org also funds SCALE. None of this makes the findings wrong; the Sierra Leone design is serious, and the report notes teachers set the objectives and students worked in pairs. It does mean the reader should weigh sponsor-run studies the way any evidence clearinghouse would, and the review does not flag sponsorship in its summary table.

The machinery is worth noticing too. A language model does first-pass screening; the authors concede it "may incorrectly screen out a relevant causal study before it reaches human review." The per-paper repository summaries are AI-drafted. And the arXiv-first funnel can miss work published elsewhere: the World Bank's six-week after-school GPT-4 English programme in Nigeria, which reported effects of about 0.3 standard deviations, does not appear among the 52 ([World Bank blog, 25 June 2025](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/addressing-the-learning-crisis-with-generative-ai--lessons-from-)). I cannot say whether it was screened out or never found; either way, "52" is the count this funnel produced, not the size of the literature.

## 7. How it compares with other syntheses

The meta-analytic literature is noisier and, read carefully, more sceptical. A widely cited 2025 meta-analysis of ChatGPT's effect on learning performance in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* now carries the title "RETRACTED ARTICLE"; Crossref records a retraction dated 22 April 2026 ([Crossref record for DOI 10.1057/s41599-025-04787-y](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1057/s41599-025-04787-y)). A newer systematic review in *Artificial Intelligence Review* pooled 49 peer-reviewed STEM studies and found a positive random-effects estimate with heterogeneity of 96.32% and a prediction interval running from g = −1.52 to g = 3.20; a robust Bayesian reanalysis found the positive effect "can be largely attributed to publication bias" ([Artificial Intelligence Review, 25 August 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10462-026-11665-9)). That is the pooled-estimate version of SCALE's narrative caution: the average hides everything, and what survives correction is the spread.

The OECD's Digital Education Outlook 2026 reached a similar framing — task performance up, durable learning not necessarily — according to Hürriyet Daily News' account, which also reports the OECD drawing on the Bastani figures ([Hürriyet Daily News, 5 February 2026](https://www.hurriyetdailynews.com/oecd-warns-ai-boosts-grades-but-not-lasting-learning-218598)); I could not open the OECD's own pages. In England, the Education Endowment Foundation has opened a £2.5m programme to commission trials on common classroom uses and cognitive offloading, with first results expected in 2027, as reported by Resultsense ([Resultsense, 12 June 2026](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-06-12-eef-ai-learning-research-fund/)); the EEF's own page refused access. In other words, the body most associated with school-level evidence in England considers the question open enough to fund from scratch.

Set side by side, SCALE is the most careful of the narrative syntheses and the most transparent about its limits. Its weakness is the one it names: it is a curated reading list with a verdict, not a quantitative estimate, and its curation leans on preprints and a machine filter.

## 8. The sceptic's case

The strongest sceptical reading is not that AI harms learning. It is that the evidence base cannot yet tell us much of anything a school would act on, and the "support, not produce" message is closer to a restatement of learning science than a finding about AI. Desirable difficulty, retrieval practice and the generation effect predicted every warning in the production column. A tool that does the thinking reduces thinking; nobody needed 52 papers for that.

Second, the positive column is fragile in specific ways. Short interventions, lab volunteers, Prolific samples, one-week follow-ups, science and coding tasks with crisp right answers, and a meaningful share of vendor-authored or vendor-funded work. The most rigorous large K–12 trial (Oreopoulos et al.) found no overall delayed benefit. The best guardrailed result (Bastani's GPT Tutor) prevented harm rather than producing unassisted gains. Remove the vendor studies and the support column for school-age students in real classrooms gets thin.

Third, novelty and Hawthorne effects. Almost every trial compares a new tool with business as usual over weeks. Nobody yet knows what an assistant does in its second year, when it is no longer new and students have learned to route around its guardrails.

Fourth, the review's own reasoning is sometimes doing more work than its data. Usage-type explanations for conflicting results (augmentation versus automation) are plausible and also exploratory; SCALE says so, but the headline travels without the caveat. The strongest honest summary is SCALE's own conclusion: the question is "when, how, and for whom it supports learning—and when it may undermine it." That is a research agenda, not a verdict.

The case for the defence is still real. The warnings are consistent across very different designs — a randomised trial in Turkey, a natural experiment on 3.2 million interactions, a 45-day delayed test in Brazil — and the support results, though fragile, point the same way. Consistency across methods is worth more than any single trial.

## 9. What it means for an international school

For an international high school, three things follow.

First, the evidence most like that setting is the evidence of risk. The one large dataset that isolates high schoolers (Rismanchian et al.) found the steepest decline in time on AI-susceptible practice at that age, and a gap between unproctored and proctored performance. In a Google Workspace school, a capable assistant is one tab away wherever students work. Any assessment completed unsupervised, in any course, should now be read as measuring a student-plus-assistant, and planning that treats such work as evidence of independent mastery is planning on stale assumptions.

Second, running on Google Workspace is not a reason to assume the Google-specific evidence transfers. The Gemini Guided Learning result came from teacher-led, paired, objective-set lessons in Sierra Leone junior secondary classrooms; the Learn Your Way result from a single lab session with a research platform that its own authors say is not a classroom product. Both are useful. Neither is evidence about an AP Calculus student using Gemini alone at 23:00.

Third, the educator evidence speaks directly to anyone leading digital learning. Teachers' time savings are real for bounded tasks; broad delegation of materials may cost student motivation, and the risk may concentrate where teachers are least able to judge the output. Time saved should be reported next to a student-side measure, or not reported as a success. When a school evaluates a learning platform, any AI feature it offers for generating course materials should be judged on what happens to students, not on teacher minutes.

The small evaluation question still holds: what can the student do when the assistance comes away? The review gives that question institutional weight.

## 10. What a school should actually do

**Classify tasks, not tools.** For each course unit where AI is permitted, write down whether the intended use is support (explanation, hints, practice, feedback on the student's own attempt) or production (drafting, solving, summarising on the student's behalf). Production may be legitimate; it should then be assessed as production, not mistaken for learning.

**Build the removal check in.** For any pilot, include one unassisted, supervised task a week or more later on material that was practised with AI, and one item on material that was not. That is the measure the base lacks and the only one that distinguishes help from a crutch. It costs a quiz, not a trial.

**Copy the guardrails that worked, not the label.** The effective conditions in the base are concrete: teacher-provided content and solutions behind the tutor, answers withheld until an attempt, mastery rules before moving on, and paired or teacher-led use. A generic "educational" wrapper did not reliably beat ChatGPT.

**Do not count satisfaction as success.** Collect it, report it separately, and never let it substitute for the delayed check.

**Read vendor evidence with sponsor-awareness.** Ask any supplier for a study with an unassisted delayed outcome, the follow-up length, the setting and who paid. Most will not have one. That is information.

**Pair teacher time savings with a student outcome.** If a department adopts AI for materials or feedback, track one student-side indicator (an engagement survey item, revision quality, a later unaided task) alongside the hours.

**Revisit in spring.** SCALE's next full review is due in spring 2027 and the EEF's first trial results in 2027. Note now what result would change the school's position, so the update is read against a decision rather than for reassurance.
