# PISA’s AI Snapshot: What It Measured, What It Did Not, and the Assessment Question Worth Keeping

_Filed 07:16, 9 September 2026._

> **Thesis:** PISA 2025 gives us a large, timely description of fifteen-year-olds’ reported chatbot use and its relationship with a science score. It does not give us an experiment in which chatbots were assigned, teaching was held constant, and learning was then measured. The useful response is not to turn a correlation into either a ban or a permission slip. It is to make the assistance, the learning evidence and the check on understanding visible in the assessment design ([OECD, *Student school life and beyond*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)).

## 1. What PISA actually measured

PISA 2025 assessed science as its major domain, with reading and mathematics as minor domains, alongside learning in the digital world; its student questionnaires supplied the surrounding information on school life, attitudes and resources. More than 760,000 students participated, representing about 33 million fifteen-year-olds in schools across 91 countries and economies. The AI result in this release is therefore not a trial of a particular chatbot, platform or school policy; it is a questionnaire-and-assessment snapshot collected in the same cycle ([OECD, *What is PISA?*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/what-is-pisa_d18121f2.html)).

For the AI-use measure, students reported how often they used AI chatbots such as ChatGPT for four schoolwork purposes: summarising an assigned text, preliminary research on a new topic, drafting text for a written assignment, and the broader purpose of helping them learn. The OECD’s AI-use index combines those four reported frequencies. That is useful breadth, but it is not a record of the model used, the prompt, the quality of its answer, what the learner did before or after opening it, or the teacher’s task design ([OECD, *Student school life and beyond*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)).

## 2. Who the results cover — and where the edges are

PISA’s target population is students aged 15 years 3 months to 16 years 2 months who were enrolled in school and had completed at least six years of formal schooling. It spans public and private, academic and vocational, full- and part-time provision, but it is still a population of enrolled fifteen-year-olds rather than every learner, every year group or every assessment setting. The reported system estimates come from probability samples, not a census: PISA required at least 150 sampled schools and normally at least 6,300 assessed students in computer-based systems, subject to smaller-population exceptions ([OECD, *The PISA target population and the PISA samples*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/the-pisa-target-population-and-the-pisa-samples_fb064025.html)).

Coverage and participation are not footnotes to ignore. The framework permits exclusions for reasons including special educational needs, language differences and inaccessible areas; the desired combined exclusion rate was below 5%, and the OECD says not every participating country or economy met all participation standards. The report also notes specific territorial coverage limits in some systems. Those are reasons to read country estimates as carefully constructed population estimates with uncertainty, not as an individual-school diagnostic ([OECD, *The PISA target population and the PISA samples*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/the-pisa-target-population-and-the-pisa-samples_fb064025.html); [OECD, *Reader’s Guide*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/reader-s-guide_0364240f.html)).

The official Viet Nam note makes the scale of reported use locally legible: 69% of students reported using chatbots to help them learn at least weekly; 40% reported weekly preliminary research, 47% weekly text summarisation and 52% weekly drafting; 4% reported never or almost never using a chatbot for any of the four tasks. Those are national PISA estimates for its target population, not a read-out of any one international school or of whether a particular use improved a particular student’s work ([OECD, *PISA 2025 Results (Volume I): Viet Nam*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i-country-notes_2d4ff9ea-en/viet-nam_d652623e-en.html)).

## 3. The association PISA observed

Across OECD countries, 14% of students said they had never or almost never used an AI chatbot for any of the four schoolwork purposes. The broad pattern was not a single AI effect. For the task-specific uses, non-users generally scored higher in science than users; for summarising and preliminary research, students reporting moderate use tended to score above both limited and frequent users. For the wider purpose “to help me learn”, weekly users had science performance similar to non-users after adjustment for socio-economic profile, while occasional and almost-daily users tended to score lower ([OECD, *Student school life and beyond*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)).

The report also finds that use profiles differ. Students reporting no AI use for schoolwork tended to be more socio-economically disadvantaged, while more frequent users tended to be more advantaged; curiosity and self-regulated help-seeking also varied by task and frequency. In a separate school-learning-opportunity analysis, around six in ten students across OECD countries said they had been asked in lessons to assess AI-generated information, and frequent users who reported that opportunity tended to score slightly higher. These are useful clues about access, dispositions and instruction; they are not a ranking of tools or an estimate of a teaching programme’s impact ([OECD, *Student school life and beyond*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)).

## 4. What the adjustment did — and did not — do

The key comparison behind the “weekly users were similar to non-users” line is more modest than it is sometimes reported. The figure uses predicted science scores from separate regressions of science performance on each AI-use frequency, accounting for students’ socio-economic status. That is a meaningful adjustment for one major confounder, and it improves on a raw comparison. It is not a matching study, a randomised trial, or a model that makes students’ prior attainment, their teachers, the assignment, classroom rules, home access, motivation and reason for using a chatbot equivalent across groups ([OECD, *Student school life and beyond*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)).

The OECD makes the limit explicit: the results “cannot determine causality.” A lower score among frequent users could be consistent with unhelpful substitution, but also with students who are already struggling seeking more help, with different assignments inviting different uses, or with a third factor shaping both use and score. A similar adjusted score for weekly “help me learn” users likewise does not show that weekly use caused learning, nor that it is harmless in every task. The data describe relationships at the time of PISA 2025; they do not settle the direction of the arrows ([OECD, *Student school life and beyond*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)).

## 5. The causal questions PISA cannot answer

PISA cannot tell us whether a given chatbot response displaced productive struggle or gave a learner a usable explanation; whether a teacher’s guidance changed the result; whether a student verified an answer; or whether the same learner would have scored differently with a different rule. It also cannot separate a tool’s availability from the instructional and social conditions that shape why students use it. Those are not defects in a survey designed for international comparison. They are the questions that require designs with known timing, comparable tasks and a credible counterfactual — none of which follows from four self-reported frequency items and one cross-sectional performance comparison ([OECD, *Student school life and beyond*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)).

There is a second scale limit. OECD averages are arithmetic means of country estimates, and the set of countries contributing can vary when data are unavailable or a category does not apply. A headline about “the OECD student” is therefore a useful international reference point, not a universal learner profile and not a warrant to import another system’s policy unchanged ([OECD, *Reader’s Guide*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/reader-s-guide_0364240f.html)).

## 6. A cautious reading for international-school assessment design

The practical lesson is to design for observable learning, not to administer a usage-count test. If a task permits AI, state the permitted assistance and ask for evidence that survives it: a source comparison, a short process note, a correction of a faulty output, a timed in-class transfer, or a brief oral defence. If the task does not permit AI, say what the assessment is trying to observe and provide a setting in which that observation is credible. These are design responses to PISA’s actual finding — use is widespread and associated with different performance patterns — rather than claims that PISA has already proved a single best policy ([OECD, *Student school life and beyond*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)).

The report’s more constructive signal is not “give students more chatbot access.” It is that students reported uneven opportunities to assess AI-generated information, and that the OECD itself says access alone is unlikely to produce benefits without guidance on appropriate and effective use. A sensible school-level question is therefore: *what does the learner have to do before, with and after AI assistance for this assessment to remain evidence of understanding?* PISA has made that question harder to avoid. It has not answered it on a school’s behalf ([OECD, *Student school life and beyond*, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)).
