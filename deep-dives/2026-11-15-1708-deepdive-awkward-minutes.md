# The Awkward Minutes: Where Attention Actually Leaks in a Device-Rich Lesson

_Filed 15 November 2026._

> **Lede:** A New South Wales research team sat at the back of 68 classrooms and found students on task about 90% of the time, with lapses that clustered around waiting, changes of activity, login trouble and unclear tasks rather than around screens as such. It is a useful, modest finding: five-minute sampling cannot time a lapse, the lessons were picked for device use, and fewer than half the students had consented to be counted. It still matches decades of classroom-management research, in which lost minutes gather at a lesson's seams, and the multitasking experiments add that an open browser makes drift easy and costly to the student behind. As Australian ministers head for a "digital detox", the practical question for an international high school on one-to-one laptops is narrower: what happens between "open your laptops" and the first real thinking? ([Macquarie University, 5 October 2026](https://screensineducation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/ClassroomObservationReport.pdf)).

## 1. What the project is, and what it measured

The Best Practice in Screen Use for Education project is funded by the NSW Government through its Screen Use and Addiction Research Fund. Its 16-member team draws on all 11 NSW-based universities and is led from Macquarie University. It has published two reports, both released on 5 October 2026. The survey report covers 4,628 retained responses gathered between 15 August and 23 November 2025: 1,057 students (666 primary, 391 secondary), 1,686 parents, 914 teachers, 166 school leaders and 805 pre-service teachers, drawn from public, Catholic and independent schools ([Macquarie University, Survey Findings, 5 October 2026](https://screensineducation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/SurveyReport.pdf)). The observation report covers 68 classes in 20 NSW public schools, observed in person between September and November 2025, totalling 57 hours ([Macquarie University, Classroom Observation Findings, 5 October 2026](https://screensineducation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/ClassroomObservationReport.pdf)).

On 9 November, Southern Cross University announced parent and teacher seminars in Lismore for 11 November, quoting one researcher: "Our results suggest educational screen use in schools is often moderate and for specific purposes" ([Southern Cross University, 9 November 2026](https://www.scu.edu.au/news/2026/do-classrooms-need-a-digital-detox/)). The toolkit landing page links resources by audience but sets out no classroom routines itself ([Screens in Education toolkit, accessed 15 November 2026](https://screensineducation.org/toolkit/)); I could not reach the teachers' sub-page, so nothing below relies on it.

The survey is the source of the headline exposure figures. Students in Years 7–8 (NSW "Stage 4") reported about 131 minutes of learning-related screen use at school on their most recent school day, against 68 minutes in Years 5–6. Leisure use at school rose from about 18 to 72 minutes across the same step, and learning-related use at school reached about 153 minutes in Years 11–12. The authors are candid that these figures come from range midpoints and "should only be treated as estimates" ([Survey Findings](https://screensineducation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/SurveyReport.pdf)).

## 2. Inside the observation report: where the minutes went

Researchers combined field notes with a "planned learning activity check" (PLA-check), a momentary time-sampling technique from 1970s behavioural research: every five minutes the observer records what teacher and students are doing with technology and counts the consenting students off task at that instant. The 57 hours yielded 686 intervals, coded (with overlap allowed) for teacher technology use, teacher talk, management strategies and student technology use ([Classroom Observation Findings](https://screensineducation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/ClassroomObservationReport.pdf)).

**The headline numbers.** Across the 55 lessons kept for the engagement analysis, consenting students were on task 90% of the time. By stage, the figures ran from 96% (Stage 1) to 82% (Stage 5, Years 9–10), with Stage 6 (Years 11–12) back at 96%. At 48% of sampling points, students were not using screens at all. The executive summary concludes that disengagement "often appeared related to the pace of lessons, transitions, waiting time, task clarity, and occasional technical issues, rather than the presence or the absence of screens."

**The seam codes.** Two student-side codes describe the awkward minutes directly. "Organisation and management" covers collecting devices, opening laptops, moving trolleys and logging into Google Classroom to find the task. It appeared in 11% of intervals overall and peaked at 18–20% in Stages 1, 3 and 4. It fell to 12% in Stage 5 and 2% in Stage 6. "Technology issues" appeared in 6% of intervals overall. The report says these "related to access and entry routines": logins, updates, flat batteries and missing devices. One observer noted that logging in could be "exceptionally hard". On the teacher side, technical problems turned up in 11 classes, and a couple of them caused "instances of up to 15 minute delays in restarting the lesson". The conclusion turns the 6% into the statement that such issues "absorbed 6% of class time". Strictly, that figure is the share of five-minute snapshots in which a technology issue was recorded, which is not the same thing as minutes lost. I return to this in section 3.

**Where drift happened.** The field notes describe three patterns. The most common was social talk and "gradual drift during independent work", especially when the teacher was busy, students were waiting for help, or some had finished early. The second was unrelated device use: YouTube Shorts, attempted shopping and social-media logins, email, games. The third was drift linked to waiting and technical failure, such as a pair stalled while one partner logged on, or a reading lesson held up because software would not load. Less often, students seemed unsure what the task was. The authors' summary is that disengagement "was more likely to occur where access, pacing, task clarity or monitoring created opportunities for students to disengage", and that "most observed periods of off-task behaviour were brief."

**What teachers did about it.** The coded strategies are low-tech: positioning of teacher or students (61 of 68 lessons), time limits, often on a visible timer (27), on-the-spot technical help (21), and lids closed or tablets face down to listen (19), sometimes half-closed "to avoid needing to restart the laptop". Real-time screen monitoring was absent: "None of the observed instances involved teachers using monitoring software that allowed them to view student screens in real time." The report's conclusion asks for task designs "that minimise 'dead time'", "reliable access, efficient start-up routines" and professional learning on "reducing waiting time".

## 3. What the method can and cannot see

The report deserves credit for listing its own limitations. Its findings still need to be read within them.

**It cannot measure duration.** Momentary time sampling records whether a student is off task at the instant of each check, not how long the lapse lasted. A ninety-second drift between checks is never counted. Over many intervals the method estimates the *proportion* of time off task reasonably well, but it cannot say how long episodes lasted. The statement that "most observed periods of off-task behaviour were brief" therefore rests on the field notes, not on the quantitative instrument. It is an observer's impression, and probably a fair one, but it is not a measurement.

**The sample is selected three times over.** School leaders nominated "one class in each stage that would involve use of digital technologies". The authors note this "may have introduced selection bias". On average only 45% of students in observed classes had consent and gave their own assent, and engagement was counted only for those students. In Stage 6 the analysis retained 45 consenting students across nine classes, about five per room. Families who return consent forms may not be a random slice of the class, and their children may not be either. Thirteen of the 68 lessons were dropped from the engagement figures, including lessons where recorded off-task counts were "not compatible with observation limited to consenting students".

**Observation changes behaviour.** The report says so: a researcher at the back may have influenced what teachers and students did, and with no recordings or view of individual screens, observers could not see behind a lid facing away from them. The 90% is best read as an upper estimate for nominated lessons with a researcher present.

**It says nothing about learning outcomes.** The design describes practice; it does not estimate the effect of screens, or of any limit, on learning. That makes the findings well-observed hypotheses about where attention leaks, not wrong ones, and they agree with a much older literature.

## 4. The older evidence: lost minutes gather at the seams

Classroom-management research has long found that order breaks down at the joins between activities.

**Routines and structure.** A widely cited systematic review of evidence-based classroom-management practices identified 20 practices with enough support to consider adopting. It put "maximize structure" first, defining structure partly as "the extent to which routines are explicitly defined". The same review reports that active supervision (moving, scanning, interacting) accounted for more of the variance in problem behaviour in transition settings than the ratio of adults to students did ([Simonsen et al., *Education and Treatment of Children*, 2008; accepted manuscript](https://eastfordct.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Evidenced-Based-Classroom-Behavior-Research.pdf)). England's Education Endowment Foundation reached a similar conclusion in its behaviour guidance: "Misbehaviour often occurs in schools around the start and end of lessons and when moving around the school building." It recommends deliberately practising transitions into the classroom. A checklist reproduced in the same report includes "Have clear routines for transitions and for stopping the class" ([EEF, *Improving Behaviour in Schools*, 2019](https://www.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/media/2322806/improvingbehaviourinschoolsguidance.pdf)).

**Interruptions are measurable.** The best-known recent attempt to count lost minutes looked at external interruptions rather than internal transitions. In Providence, Rhode Island, Matthew Kraft and Manuel Monti-Nussbaum made more than 60 classroom observations in five secondary schools. They found about 3.5 minutes of instruction lost to interruptions per hour, while teachers estimated about 6.5 minutes. They estimated that this scales to 10–20 instructional days a year. About half of the interruptions set off a further disruption, typically students going off task while the teacher refocused them ([Kraft, *Educational Leadership*, 1 June 2020](https://www.ascd.org/el/articles/the-hidden-cost-of-classroom-interruptions)). A login failure works much like a knock at the door. It stops the lesson, and the restart costs more than the stoppage itself.

**Activity length matters.** A large observational study of kindergarten to fourth-grade classrooms (more than 50 classrooms, about 1,100 children) found off-task behaviour of roughly 26–29%, higher in whole-group instruction and rising as activities ran beyond about ten minutes. The lead author's advice was "divvying up instructional activities into smaller chunks" ([The Hechinger Report on Godwin et al., *Learning and Instruction* 2016; report dated 4 December 2017](https://hechingerreport.org/teachers-often-ask-youngsters-learn-ways-exceed-even-adult-sized-attention-spans/)). These were young children, so only the direction carries over to high school.

**Task clarity is the quiet variable.** The teacher-clarity meta-analyses sat behind a 403 error, so I cite none of their figures. The NSW notes make the point anyway: some Stage 5 drift came where students "were somewhat unclear about task requirements". A student with no next move and an open browser will find one.

## 5. Where the device still matters

Reading the NSW report as "it's the teaching, not the screens" is half right: the seams create the opportunity, the device decides what fills it.

**Self-reports point the other way.** In the project's survey, 84% of secondary students said they were distracted when using screens in class, against 57% of primary students. Seventy-six per cent of secondary students said other students' screens distracted them. Only 59% said they would always stop using screens immediately when asked, against 81% in primary. The authors suggest this "may reflect not only inhibitory control but also a reduced willingness to follow teacher directions." Teachers' answers were similar. Only 42% said students were often or always able to turn their attention away from screens when asked, and 52% said students were often or always distracted by unrelated content ([Survey Findings](https://screensineducation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/SurveyReport.pdf)). The text does not say whether 84% means "ever" or "often", so read it as prevalence, not intensity. Both findings can be true: lapses brief and rare when an adult is watching, distraction common as students experience it.

**The experimental evidence on multitasking.** In a pair of experiments with university students in a simulated lecture, Faria Sana, Tina Weston and Nicholas Cepeda found that participants who multitasked on a laptop scored lower on an immediate comprehension test. More strikingly, students with a direct view of a multitasking peer also scored lower, even though they were taking notes on paper and were not multitasking themselves. The authors are careful about the limits: "we are more limited in terms of the generalizations we make to younger students", and they did not test delayed recall ([Sana, Weston & Cepeda, *Computers & Education*, 2013; authors' FAQ](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/laptopFAQ.html)). The study used a mock lecture with tasks assigned by the researchers, so it shows the mechanism rather than its size in a real high school. That mechanism is relevant here. A student drifting during a slow login is a cost to the student and also to the view of the people behind them.

**The field experiment.** At West Point, Susan Payne Carter, Kyle Greenberg and Michael Walker randomly assigned 726 students in 50 sections of an introductory economics course to no technology, unrestricted laptops or tablets, or tablets that had to lie flat on the desk. Allowing devices lowered final-exam scores by about 0.18 standard deviations. The flat-tablet condition was also negative, and in subgroup analyses "no group benefits from computer access in the classroom" ([Education Next, 22 August 2017](https://www.educationnext.org/computer-use-college-classes-reduces-final-exam-grades/)). Devices there were optional, not designed into the lesson, so the finding speaks most directly to minutes when a laptop is open with no job: a lid left up during instruction, or during a wait.

Together they say one thing from two directions: idle minutes are where students disengage, and an open, idle laptop is the easiest place to do it.

## 6. The policy weather: caps, a taskforce, and an awkward fit

The research arrives in an Australian policy climate that has already moved on to limits. NSW's own guidelines, updated 24 September 2026, set recommended maximums of individual-device use of "up to 25% of lesson time per subject for Years 7 to 10" and "up to 50% of lesson time per subject for Years 11 and 12", averaged over a week. Schools are encouraged to start in Term 1 2027 and must comply by Term 1 2028. Some specialist subjects are exempt. Students should use devices only when a teacher explicitly instructs them to. The guidelines also ask schools to minimise "use of screens during transitions where possible" ([NSW Department of Education, updated 24 September 2026](https://education.nsw.gov.au/about-us/education-data-and-research/screen-use-research-and-resource-hub/screen-time-usage-guidelines-in-nsw-public-schools)). That last instruction is the one most clearly supported by the observation report.

Nationally, education ministers met in early November and agreed what the federal minister called "a digital detox in schools restricting screen time and the use of AI". They set up an Australian Schools Digital Detox Taskforce, to be led by an independent expert, with government and sector representatives. Its job is to implement restrictions and advise on further practical action ([Region Canberra, 6 November 2026](https://region.com.au/back-to-reading-writing-and-rithmetic-as-schools-head-for-a-digital-detox/1013679/)). EducationHQ reports that the first national moves target generative AI in primary schools and in high-school assessment, that the taskforce is expected to report three times in 2027, and that Victoria has set a two-hour daily limit from 2027 ([EducationHQ, 5 November 2026](https://educationhq.com/news/gone-too-far-ai-detox-anti-semitism-lessons-in-national-education-overhaul-216922/)). I could not open the ministers' communiqué on the Commonwealth site, as the request timed out repeatedly, so the taskforce details above rest on press reporting.

The fit is awkward. In the nominated lessons, Stage 5 students used screens at about 41% of sampling points and Stage 6 at about 65%, both above the coming per-subject caps, in lessons the researchers judged purposeful. The caps are weekly averages and these lessons were picked for device use, so this is not a breach. But the caps target total exposure; the observations point to how particular minutes are managed. Before ministers met, a University of Wollongong-led position statement backed by more than 85 experts and organisations urged governments to "shift the debate from screen time to screen use in schools" ([University of Wollongong, 2 September 2026](https://www.uow.edu.au/media/2026/researchers-warn-digital-detox-in-schools-may-do-more-harm-than-good.php)).

## 7. What it means for an international school

An international high school outside Australia does not answer to NSW caps or a Canberra taskforce. Many have the conditions the NSW report describes, though: Grades 9–12 roughly match Stages 5 and 6, every student brings a laptop, and a learning management system runs on top of a cloud office suite. Three implications follow.

**Grades 9–10 are the band to watch.** Stage 5 was the low point for engagement in the NSW observations (82%), and in the survey it was the high point for students reporting exposure to inappropriate content at school (42%, against 25% in Stage 6) ([Survey Findings](https://screensineducation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/SurveyReport.pdf)). That small sample is not evidence about any particular school. It does suggest that, if lost minutes are going to show up anywhere in the high school, they will most likely show up in Grades 9–10, during independent work, when the teacher is helping one table and the rest have finished or are waiting.

**Personal laptops make the start-up seam narrower and more private.** The NSW start-up minutes were dominated by trolleys, shared iPads and flat batteries. Those costs largely disappear with one-to-one personal laptops, and the NSW Stage 6 figures (2% organisation, 0% technology issues) look like a one-to-one senior cohort. What remains is quieter: an operating-system update that chose this morning, an LMS or cloud sign-in that has expired, a shared document whose permissions were never set. These failures hit one student at a time, so they are easy to miss as a pattern. Each one is a student with an idle device and, as the Sana experiments suggest, a peer behind them looking at that student's screen.

**An LMS change is a seam in its own right.** A school moving to a new learning platform faces a year of new logins, new navigation and new places to find "the task". That is the kind of access friction the NSW observers found creating openings to disengage. It is worth designing the first-weeks routines before the switch, rather than treating them as a training item later.

With students' own laptops, such a school has less hardware control than a NSW public school and more need for routines students carry themselves. The survey's drop in secondary students' willingness to stop when asked says the same: routines must be taught and practised, not announced.

## 8. The sceptic's case

**The researchers are not neutral on the policy question.** Two of the report's authors, Karley Beckman and Tiffani Apps, led the September position statement arguing against blanket time limits ([University of Wollongong, 2 September 2026](https://www.uow.edu.au/media/2026/researchers-warn-digital-detox-in-schools-may-do-more-harm-than-good.php)). That does not invalidate their observations. It does mean the "situational, not device-driven" framing comes from a team that had already argued publicly for that view. It should be checked against the field notes rather than taken from the executive summary.

**"Not device-driven" may be a false split.** Waiting creates the opening; a connected laptop fills it with YouTube Shorts, shopping and games, as the observers recorded. Calling that situational assigns the cause to half an interaction. The West Point and Sana results suggest the device sets what drift costs, and who else pays.

**Ninety per cent is flattering by design.** The lessons were nominated, observed, consent-filtered and sampled every five minutes, and observers could not see screens facing away from them. Every one of those features biases the engagement figure upward. Students' own reports, with 84% of secondary students saying they get distracted, sit uneasily beside 90% on task.

**The policy-makers have a point the design cannot address.** Ministers are reacting partly to attainment trends. A one-term observational snapshot cannot speak to whether total exposure affects reading, writing or sustained attention over years. The case for caps may be weak, but this report does not refute it. It is a different instrument answering a different question.

**Routines are not free either.** Lids-down signals, paper starters and transition cues take time to establish, and over-engineered seams make a lesson of procedure. The review literature itself notes that high structure was unrelated to independent task persistence; in a high school, independence is part of the goal.

## 9. What a school should actually do

Each item is observable, nearly free, and checkable with the NSW team's own method.

1. **Give the first two minutes a job that needs no device.** A question on the board, a paper extract, a pair discussion, while laptops wake up and sign in. A student who gets in instantly loses nothing, and a student stuck on a login has something to do.
2. **Make "lids at 45 degrees" a taught signal, not a request.** The NSW teachers used half-closed lids to avoid restarts. Practise the signal for instruction, discussion and transitions until it takes under five seconds, and use it whenever screens have no job.
3. **Put the next move in writing.** Every device task should come with a visible "when you finish, do this" that is part of the learning: an extension, a peer check, a written reflection. Early finishers were among the NSW observers' commonest sources of drift.
4. **Treat device readiness as a morning routine, not a lesson problem.** Build charging and pending-update checks into advisory or homeroom, and keep a small stock of loan chargers in each corridor. Have a paper version of any start-of-lesson task ready for a student whose device fails.
5. **Teach the login path once, and post it.** At the start of each year, and again at any LMS change, walk students through exactly where the task lives, and post that route visibly. Unclear location is a form of unclear task.
6. **Position for the peer effect.** Circulate from the back so you can see screens, and when a student drifts, the people behind them are affected too. Seat students who struggle with self-management where their screens face the teacher's usual path.
7. **Chunk independent work.** Break long device stretches into shorter segments with brief check-ins. Drift accumulates during long independent runs, especially while the teacher is tied up with one group.
8. **Measure your own seams.** Ask a colleague to observe a lesson and note, every five minutes, how many students are off task and what the class is doing. Then look at where the ticks fall: transitions, starts, waits. It takes one observer and one lesson, and it gives the conversation something better than impressions.
9. **Keep the debate on use, not hours.** If caps come up, ask what students are doing in specific minutes, not how many. The NSW evidence is strong on the former and silent on the latter.
