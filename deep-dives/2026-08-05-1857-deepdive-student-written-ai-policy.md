# The Students First Act: What Ninety-Eight Teenagers Separated That Most AI School Policies Still Merge

_Filed 5 August 2026._

> **Thesis:** The interesting thing the students did is not that they wrote rules; it is that they split a single question everyone else treats as one — "is this cheating?" — into four questions that need four different mechanisms: *permission* (may you use it), *disclosure* (did you say you did), *evidence* (what actually shows a rule was broken), and *appeal* (how a student challenges the accusation). Having separated them, they refused to let an AI-detector score be a verdict: a flag triggers a human investigation, not a grade. That procedural instinct is genuinely ahead of the working adult baseline — in the best available survey of real district codes, fewer than a quarter mention AI at all, and the schools least likely to have a policy are exactly the ones a detector-as-verdict regime would hurt most. But the same bill then mandates a "standardized AI detector" on every written assessment — the one tool the adult evidence says does not work and misfires on non-native and neurodivergent writers — and bans "AI for the act of writing" outright, which is unenforceable and collides with its own disclosure logic. The borrowable core is the scaffolding, not the prohibitions.

## 1. What actually happened, and what they actually built

Between 17 and 19 July 2026, roughly a hundred high-school students — NPR counted 98, AASA said "more than 90," and the 82–16 final tally settles it at 98 votes cast — gathered on the UMass Boston and MIT campuses for what the organisers called America's Youth AI Festival, then moved into a full-scale replica of the United States Senate chamber at the Edward M. Kennedy Institute to pass a model bill ([AASA, 3 August 2026](https://www.aasa.org/news-media/news/2026/08/03/students-produce-national-framework-for-ai-in-america's-schools); [NPR, 30 July 2026](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/30/nx-s1-5853571/students-set-ai-policy)). The convening partners were Day of AI and MIT RAISE (the research group that runs it), AASA — The School Superintendents Association, and the Kennedy Institute itself. Students were sworn in, taught parliamentary procedure by Institute staff, split into four subcommittees, and spent the weekend amending and horse-trading. NPR's account is that the bill nearly died more than once and was revived by negotiation, compromise and floor speeches — which is the part that should make an educator sit up, because it means the coherence in the final text was argued into existence, not handed down.

The bill is styled the STUDENTS FIRST Act, S.2026 of a mock 119th Congress, and it is organised into five sections mapped to the four subcommittees plus a set of general provisions ([Day of AI, July 2026](https://dayofai.org/news/student-senate-student-first-act)). The architecture is worth stating plainly, because it is the architecture that carries the argument:

- **Section 1 — General provisions.** Vendors must "address all known biases within their generative AI" and disclose them to schools, who pass them to parents; educators keep the right to decline AI without penalty; nothing in the act overrides existing privacy law ([AASA resource page](https://www.aasa.org/resources/resource/students-first-act), the only section reproduced verbatim by a primary source).
- **Section 2 — Students** (the STUDENT subcommittee). AI literacy from the moment devices enter the classroom; no AI "for the act of writing," with editing, brainstorming and studying permitted after eighth grade; disclose-or-it-is-plagiarism; no AI on graded assessments; the right to refuse AI and take an alternative assignment; and the right to appeal a wrongful-use finding, with two officials plus detection software required before a conviction.
- **Section 3 — Parents and guardians** (PARENT). Annual notice of how AI will be used; a right to an explanation of any classroom AI rule; and the striking one — school-licensed chatbots must detect when a student is seeking "emotional support or life advice" and route to a human, because "machines cannot fulfill students' requests."
- **Section 4 — Teachers** (TEACHERS). Publish a written AI policy in the syllabus each semester; may require an oral defence of any suspect work; may not rely solely on AI to decide grades or misconduct; and — the provision in tension with everything above — "must use one standardized AI detector" on written graded assessments, plus lockdown browsers online, investigating personally before reporting.
- **Section 5 — Administrators** (ADMIN). Publish the rule set; consult students, staff and families before drafting it; run an appeals process; ban AI from hiring; and bar counsellors from using AI in mental-health contexts.

Day of AI's Jeff Riley called it "written by students, for students"; the Institute's chief education officer, Sarah Yezzi, framed the students as asking for neither prohibition nor a free-for-all but for "clear, fair rules that protect privacy, preserve the ability to think" ([AASA, 3 August 2026](https://www.aasa.org/news-media/news/2026/08/03/students-produce-national-framework-for-ai-in-america's-schools)). AASA says it will circulate the text to its network of more than 10,000 school leaders, many of whom are writing their own policies right now with nothing to copy from. That distribution is why this is worth examining closely: the document is about to function as a template whether or not it deserves to.

## 2. The move the adults keep missing: four questions, not one

Almost every real school AI policy collapses under a single verb — *cheating* — and then argues about where to draw its line. The students' bill is better because it quietly refuses that collapse. Read across the sections and four distinct questions fall out, each with its own mechanism:

1. **Permission** — *may you use it, and for what?* Answered by the syllabus policy (§4) and the graded-work ban (§2): a per-assignment, teacher-set boundary, published in advance.
2. **Disclosure** — *did you say you used it?* Answered by the cite-or-it-is-plagiarism rule (§2): the offence is the concealment, not the use.
3. **Evidence** — *what actually demonstrates a breach?* Answered by the oral defence, the handwritten check and the in-person discussion (§2, §4): the proof of understanding is relocated to a setting a model cannot sit in for you.
4. **Appeal** — *how do you contest the accusation?* Answered by the two-official review and the administrator appeals process (§2, §5): a detector output is an input to a human process, never the output of one.

Separating these is the whole contribution, and it is not obvious — it took a weekend of argument to reach. Most adult policies fuse disclosure and evidence (treating an undisclosed use and a detector flag as the same event) and skip appeal entirely. The students' instinct is closer to how any defensible integrity system works: you specify what is allowed, you require candour, you decide what counts as proof, and you build a route to be wrong about someone and correct it. That is due process, and student voice here produced it rather than the permissive slogan the phrase "student voice" usually invites. The rest of this analysis takes the four questions in the order that matters most for a school actually writing a policy — evidence and appeal first, because that is where the adult failures are worst and the student contribution sharpest.

## 3. Evidence and appeal: the detector-is-not-a-verdict rule, and why it is the strongest thing here

The bill's best sentence is the one that says a flag is not a finding. Section 2 requires that before a student can be "convicted" of wrongful AI use, two school officials — not one, and not an algorithm — examine the work alongside any detection software; Section 4 tells the teacher to "personally investigate" before reporting; Section 5 stands up an appeals process. Put together, that is a due-process chain: notification, human review, a second human, a route to challenge. The reason this matters is that the tool it disciplines does not work, and the adults have known for two years.

The foundational evidence is Weixin Liang, James Zou and colleagues' study in *Patterns*, which ran seven popular GPT detectors over essays by non-native English writers and US eighth-graders: the detectors misclassified more than half of the non-native TOEFL essays as AI-generated — one flagged nearly 98% — while correctly clearing more than 90% of the native-speaker essays ([Liang et al., *Patterns*, 2023](https://www.cell.com/patterns/fulltext/S2666-3899(23)00130-7)). The mechanism is not incidental; it is structural. Detectors score "perplexity," and text built from common, predictable words scores low, which the detector reads as machine-like. A student with a smaller English range is penalised for exactly that. Zou's own recommendation was blunt: "we should be extremely careful about and maybe try to avoid using these detectors as much as possible."

Institutions acted on it. Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin's AI detector in August 2023 and showed its working: at Turnitin's own claimed 1% false-positive rate, across the roughly 75,000 papers Vanderbilt submitted in 2022, about 750 student papers could have been wrongly flagged — and the feature had been switched on "with less than 24-hour advance notice… no insight into how it works." Their conclusion: "We do not believe that AI detection software is an effective tool that should be used" ([Vanderbilt, 16 August 2023](https://www.vanderbilt.edu/brightspace/2023/08/16/guidance-on-ai-detection-and-why-were-disabling-turnitins-ai-detector/)). A large multilingual international school makes this sharper, not softer: a school where a large share of students write English as a second, third or fourth language is precisely the population the Stanford data says gets falsely accused. In many international schools that is not a hypothetical equity concern; it is the modal student.

The adults are now, belatedly, arriving where the students already are. California's Department of Education model AI policy, released in summer 2026, states that AI-detection software should not be the sole basis for a disciplinary action or grade penalty, that undisclosed AI-generated work constitutes plagiarism, and that "clear criteria for rebutting" an accusation must be made available to students and parents ([California Department of Education model policy, 2026](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ci/pl/aipolicy.asp)). That is the same detector-is-not-a-verdict architecture the teenagers reached — arrived at independently and in the same season. When a student senate and a state education department converge on the identical procedural safeguard, the safeguard is probably not naïve.

And the real cost of *not* having it is documented. In the Hingham High School case (litigated as *Harris v. Adams*), a Massachusetts student was failed and excluded from the National Honor Society for AI use; he argued the student handbook contained no AI-specific policy at all. The court upheld the school under existing plagiarism and unauthorised-technology rules — but the case only exists because the school was adjudicating a novel offence with no AI-specific code and no purpose-built appeal ([Curran & Goo, University of Florida EPRC, October 2025](https://education.ufl.edu/eprc/files/2025/11/EPRC_PolicyBrief-AI-and-School-Discipline-Curran-and-Goo-2025.pdf)). The students' bill is, in effect, the policy that would have made that dispute procedurally clean before it started.

## 4. Disclosure and the published semester policy: the quiet, borrowable part

The least glamorous provisions are the most immediately usable. Two stand out.

First, disclosure as the load-bearing offence. The bill makes concealment — not use — the violation: cite how and where AI was used, "or else it will be considered plagiarism." This is the same logic real districts have started reaching for. The University of Florida analysis surfaces Volusia County's "Guidelines for AI Use," which permit AI "as a feedback mechanism as part of the writing process" but require "fully transparent citation of AI use," and California's model policy treats undisclosed AI as academic dishonesty by definition ([Curran & Goo, October 2025](https://education.ufl.edu/eprc/files/2025/11/EPRC_PolicyBrief-AI-and-School-Discipline-Curran-and-Goo-2025.pdf); [California Department of Education, 2026](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ci/pl/aipolicy.asp)). It is also the spine of the most-used academic framework in the sector, the AI Assessment Scale of Leon Furze, Mike Perkins and colleagues — five levels from "no AI" to "full AI," each requiring the student to state where on the scale a task sat and how the tool was used ([AI Assessment Scale](https://aiassessmentscale.com/)). The students, Volusia, California and the AIAS all land in the same place: the honest thing to build is a disclosure grammar, not a detection dragnet.

Second, the requirement that every teacher publish a written AI policy each semester stating exactly when AI is "permitted, restricted, or banned." This is the cheapest high-value idea in the document. The reason is in the data: in the University of Florida study, allowed use was frequently left to "the discretion of the teacher" with no published standard, "leaving students to navigate what might be different expectations across classrooms" ([Curran & Goo, October 2025](https://education.ufl.edu/eprc/files/2025/11/EPRC_PolicyBrief-AI-and-School-Discipline-Curran-and-Goo-2025.pdf)). A student moving between six classrooms currently faces six unstated, mutually contradictory rules and is then disciplined against whichever one they guessed wrong. A one-paragraph, per-course, per-semester statement does not solve AI; it solves the ambiguity that turns AI into an integrity case. It is the single provision worth lifting into an international-school policy unchanged.

## 5. The chatbot-counsellor rule: the most novel provision, and the one that most needs a governor

Section 3 contains the provision no widely used adult framework has yet attempted: school-licensed chatbots "must be able to detect when students attempt to use them for emotional support or life advice," and when they do, the software "should take action, which may include alerting school officials," on the stated principle that "machines cannot fulfill students' requests." Section 5 reinforces it by barring counsellors from using AI in mental-health contexts.

The students are reading the room correctly. The past year has been one long argument about exactly this. California's SB 243, signed in 2025 and in force from 1 January 2026, requires companion chatbots to disclose they are not human and to run protocols that refer minors to crisis services when suicide is discussed, reporting referral data to the state ([KQED, 29 October 2025](https://www.kqed.org/science/1999388/california-warns-families-to-watch-out-for-teens-as-character-ai-shuts-off-chatbot-access)). Character.AI, under lawsuits and regulatory pressure after a 14-year-old's suicide, barred under-18s from open-ended chats entirely from 25 November 2025; Common Sense Media's Robbie Torney said the field now needs "all other platforms that offer AI companionship or AI mental health advice or AI emotional support to follow Character.AI's lead immediately" ([KQED, 29 October 2025](https://www.kqed.org/science/1999388/california-warns-families-to-watch-out-for-teens-as-character-ai-shuts-off-chatbot-access)). The students' instinct — that a school chatbot should hand a distressed child to a human rather than counsel them — is aligned with, and in one respect more operationally specific than, a law an actual legislature passed.

But this is also where the naïveté shows, and it is instructive naïveté. The provision assumes the software can reliably *detect* "a request for emotional support or life advice." That is the same unsolved classification problem as AI-detection, pointed at a far more sensitive target: a system that over-triggers turns every "I'm stressed about this exam" into an alert to school officials and a privacy breach; one that under-triggers misses the disclosure that mattered. "Machines cannot fulfill students' requests" is the right value and an overbroad rule — a crisis-referral protocol (the SB 243 model) is implementable; a general prohibition on a chatbot ever offering "life advice," reliably enforced, is not. The students correctly identified the duty and underestimated the detection problem — which is, tidily, the exact mistake they made about academic-integrity detectors one section earlier.

## 6. Where the students are genuinely ahead of the adults

On the central procedural question, they are ahead of the working baseline by a wide margin, and the evidence for that is not impressionistic. The best current census of real practice is the University of Florida analysis of all 73 Florida school-district codes of conduct for 2024–25: only 23.3% referenced AI at all, while 93.2% had academic-dishonesty policies and 100% had digital-misuse policies ([Curran & Goo, October 2025](https://education.ufl.edu/eprc/files/2025/11/EPRC_PolicyBrief-AI-and-School-Discipline-Curran-and-Goo-2025.pdf)). More than three-quarters of districts, in other words, were disciplining students for AI use — teacher-reported AI discipline cases had risen 16 points to 64% the prior year — against codes of conduct that never mention AI. The students wrote the AI-specific due process those codes lack.

The equity gradient in that same study is the part that should be read twice, because it inverts the intuition that policy sophistication tracks need. The districts *least* likely to have any AI language were those with the highest proportions of White students (the top quartile referenced AI in 0% of cases), the highest free-and-reduced-lunch enrolment (top quartile, 0%), and rural districts (0%, versus 41% of urban districts). Nationally, the same authors find nearly two-thirds of districts silent on AI, with more racially diverse and economically disadvantaged districts *less* likely to have guidance ([Curran & Goo, October 2025](https://education.ufl.edu/eprc/files/2025/11/EPRC_PolicyBrief-AI-and-School-Discipline-Curran-and-Goo-2025.pdf)). Layer that onto the Stanford detector finding and you get the actual failure mode the students legislated against: the students most likely to be falsely flagged by a detector are disproportionately in the schools least likely to have an appeal process. The teenagers built the safeguard for the population the adult system is quietly failing. That is not a small thing to have understood in a weekend.

## 7. Where the bill is naïve, unworkable, or quietly self-contradictory

An honest map has to mark the swamp. Four problems, in rough order of severity.

**The detector mandate contradicts the detector-appeal.** This is the sharpest internal fault. Section 2 subordinates detectors to human review — correctly. Section 4 then *requires* every teacher to "use one standardized AI detector" on all written graded assessments. Having built a due-process wall around the tool because it is unreliable, the bill makes the unreliable tool compulsory. Vanderbilt's whole point was that the least-bad option is often not to run the detector at all ([Vanderbilt, 16 August 2023](https://www.vanderbilt.edu/brightspace/2023/08/16/guidance-on-ai-detection-and-why-were-disabling-turnitins-ai-detector/)); a policy that mandates one on every paper manufactures the exact false-positive volume its appeals process then has to absorb. The mature version keeps §2's appeal and deletes §4's mandate.

**"No AI for the act of writing" is a value dressed as an enforceable rule.** It is unenforceable at the boundary (where does permitted "editing" end and prohibited "writing" begin?), it contradicts the disclosure logic (if concealment is the offence, disclosed assistance should not be a separate absolute ban), and it is the provision most likely to generate the false accusations the rest of the bill is built to prevent. It reads as a sincere defence of thinking; it functions as a tripwire.

**Lockdown browsers are surveillance with their own equity tax.** The bill reaches past the human oral-defence — its best evidence mechanism — to mandate lockdown browsers for online assessments. Proctoring software has a documented record of false flags against students with disabilities, unstable connectivity, and non-standard testing conditions. The oral defence is the students' good idea; the lockdown browser is the reflex it should have replaced.

**The scaffolding is US-federal and needs translating.** States, federal funding lines, IEP/504 plans, National Honor Society — the bill's plumbing is American public-school plumbing. None of it maps cleanly onto an international school outside the United States, with its own curriculum and no federal anything. The *provisions* travel; the *machinery* does not.

There is also a quieter limit worth naming: this was a simulation, run by organisations (Day of AI, MIT RAISE) with a professional interest in AI-in-schools, over three days, by a self-selected cohort engaged enough to spend a July weekend on parliamentary procedure. That does not diminish the text, but it should discipline the "teens figured out what adults couldn't" framing the coverage reached for. They figured out a *good procedural skeleton*. They did not, and could not in a weekend, stress-test it against implementation.

## 8. What an international school could actually borrow

The residue is smaller than the bill and firmer than the headline. Three tiers.

**Adopt roughly as-is.** (1) The disclosure-is-the-offence rule — cite AI use or it is treated as plagiarism — because it is enforceable, honest, and matches where the AIAS, California and real districts have landed. (2) The per-course, per-semester published AI statement — permitted / restricted / banned, in the syllabus — because it kills cross-classroom ambiguity for near-zero cost and is the cheapest defensible thing on the list. (3) The oral defence / in-person check as the *primary* evidence of understanding, which is the one assessment move generative AI cannot sit in for and which suits a multilingual cohort better than any detector.

**Adopt with adaptation.** (4) The detector-is-not-a-verdict chain — keep the two-human review and the appeal, but do *not* pair it with a detector mandate; for a school where most students write English as an additional language, the Stanford evidence argues for using detectors sparingly if at all, precisely so the appeal process is not swamped ([Liang et al., 2023](https://www.cell.com/patterns/fulltext/S2666-3899(23)00130-7)). (5) The chatbot-crisis rule — implement the SB 243-style crisis-referral-to-a-human protocol, not a blanket "no life advice" prohibition, because the former is buildable and the latter depends on detection that does not yet exist ([KQED, 29 October 2025](https://www.kqed.org/science/1999388/california-warns-families-to-watch-out-for-teens-as-character-ai-shuts-off-chatbot-access)).

**Leave on the table.** (6) The universal detector mandate and (7) the lockdown-browser requirement — both import the false-positive and equity problems the rest of the framework exists to avoid. And (8) the "never use AI for writing" absolute, which a disclosure-plus-oral-defence regime already covers more honestly and more enforceably.

The sentence worth carrying out of this is not "the kids wrote a better policy than the adults." It is narrower and more useful: **the students correctly separated permission, disclosure, evidence and appeal, and correctly refused to let a detector be a verdict — then undercut themselves by mandating the detector anyway.** Borrow the separation. Borrow the appeal. Leave the mandate.
