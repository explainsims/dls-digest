# What lockable phone pouches change at school

_Filed 19 November 2026._

> **The clearest result is less phone use during the school day.** The new Harvard study adds a small increase in annual maths and reading growth when the two subjects are pooled. The estimate is sensitive to analytical choices, and a second national study places average test-score effects close to zero. For a school weighing pouches, the most useful question is how much more reliably they would keep phones out of use than the routine already in place.

## 1. What Harvard found

Burland and colleagues at Harvard’s Center for Education Policy Research used fall and spring NWEA MAP scores from 2021–22 through 2025–26. The report covers roughly 3.3 million U.S. students across 4,600 schools, including 844 pouch adopters in the final treatment sample. The team compared changes in annual growth after schools introduced Yondr with changes at similar schools. Adopters and comparison schools showed no clear divergence in growth before adoption. Schools chose to adopt, so the estimate still depends on the groups having followed similar growth trends in the absence of pouches. [Harvard report, pp. 10–16 and Figure 7](https://cepr.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum9881/files/2026-11/Phone-Free%20Schools%20and%20Achievement%20Growth_1.pdf).

**The pooled estimate was +0.0087 standard deviations** of additional fall-to-spring growth, with a school-clustered standard error of 0.0043. An approximate 95% confidence interval runs from essentially zero to +0.017. Maths (+0.0079) and reading (+0.0095) both point in the same direction, with wider intervals that include zero. The main pooled result just clears the usual 5% significance threshold. Keeping unusually large gains and losses in the sample brings it down to +0.0058, with an interval spanning zero. The adjustment for fall-score measurement error specified in the preregistration also gives a small positive estimate whose interval spans zero (+0.0068). Pooled estimates for the first and later years are similar, with more uncertainty in later years. The study leaves open whether small gains persist and accumulate over time. [Harvard report, p. 17, Table 2, Figure 7 and Appendix Tables A3–A4](https://cepr.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum9881/files/2026-11/Phone-Free%20Schools%20and%20Achievement%20Growth_1.pdf).

**One to two weeks is a comparison of score gains.** The authors compare their estimate with typical annual progress and with studies of extra school days and changes to the school week. They did not time interruptions or count extra teaching minutes. The comparison with earlier national score declines also gives a sense of scale. The pouch study cannot establish the historical contribution of smartphones to those declines. [Harvard report, pp. 6–8, 22 and 39–40](https://cepr.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum9881/files/2026-11/Phone-Free%20Schools%20and%20Achievement%20Growth_1.pdf).

## 2. Why the earlier national result still matters

Allcott and colleagues used school-average scores from state tests in middle and high schools. Their September revision put the pooled effect at **−0.006 school-level standard deviations** (standard error 0.010). The high-school maths estimate was +0.047 on that scale, about +0.024 on the authors’ conversion to an individual student scale. The estimate for the combined middle-school score was −0.025 school-level standard deviations. Harvard found little clear evidence of differences by school level. Its outcome is individual fall-to-spring growth on a common assessment, while the earlier study tracks the level of school-average achievement on different state tests. Harvard’s authors report overlapping confidence intervals that allow small positive effects. The studies cannot tell us whether the same effect holds in each grade. [Allcott et al., September revision, §V.C and Table III](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35132/w35132.pdf). [Accepted manuscript, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*](https://academic.oup.com/qje/advance-article/doi/10.1093/qje/qjag050/9005628). [Harvard report, pp. 22–25](https://cepr.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum9881/files/2026-11/Phone-Free%20Schools%20and%20Achievement%20Growth_1.pdf).

Both teams used Yondr records to identify adoption and Advan location data to estimate device activity. Their U.S. school samples can overlap, while their achievement measures come from separate sources. Harvard acknowledges Overdeck and Walton family foundation support. Allcott and colleagues list six funders, including Walton, and separately disclose Yondr’s data partnership and assistance. Across the two studies, falling campus device activity and teacher reports point in the same direction. Allcott’s retrospective survey put the share of students using phones in class for personal reasons at 61% before pouch adoption and 13% after. Location pings can include adults and idle phones, so neither proxy counts minutes of student attention. [Harvard report, acknowledgements and §IV](https://cepr.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum9881/files/2026-11/Phone-Free%20Schools%20and%20Achievement%20Growth_1.pdf). [Allcott et al., acknowledgements and §V.A](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35132/w35132.pdf).

## 3. The wider evidence points to implementation

**The starting point matters.** In one Florida district, students were told to silence phones and keep them in backpacks all day. Test scores improved more in the second year at schools with higher prior phone activity than at schools with lower activity, under the same district rule. Suspensions initially rose, especially for Black students, before easing. In Rio de Janeiro, researchers found a +0.06 standard deviation improvement on quarterly assessments in middle schools that tightened their rules more under the citywide policy, compared with schools where strict rules were already in place. Those comparisons suggest there may be more to gain where prior rules left more scope for phone use. Their estimates describe local policy changes and distinct tests. [Figlio and Özek, §§2, 4–5 and Tables 2–4](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34388/w34388.pdf). [Lichand et al., abstract and §1](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35233/w35233.pdf).

**A pouch is one way to make a rule stick.** In Harvard’s retrospective staff survey, pouches and other strict policies such as central storage were associated with similar reductions in reported personal use. The same report’s statewide analysis gave mixed achievement results, and comparison states were already on different trajectories before bans took effect. Neither comparison can establish which storage method yields more learning. [Harvard report, Table 7 and Figures 10–11](https://cepr.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum9881/files/2026-11/Phone-Free%20Schools%20and%20Achievement%20Growth_1.pdf).

**Wellbeing takes separate evidence.** England’s SMART Schools study compared 1,227 pupils aged 12–15 in 30 schools at one point in time. Pupils at restrictive schools reported about 40 minutes less phone use during school. The study found no clear differences in overall phone use or mental wellbeing. Most restrictive schools required phones to stay switched off in bags, and one used pouches. Allcott’s pouch study likewise gives grounds for caution. Discipline incidents rose at adoption, then eased. The early wellbeing dip and later improvement drew heavily on different groups of adopters, so the study cannot show that the same pupils recovered. [Goodyear et al., Tables 1 and 3](https://pure-oai.bham.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/256423016/GoodyearVA2025School.pdf). [Allcott et al., §V.B and Figure IV](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35132/w35132.pdf).

## 4. The decision a school can actually make

An international school can start by checking where the current routine breaks down. If the main problem is interruptions during lessons, count phone interruptions and minutes spent dealing with them. If the aim is a more sociable lunch, observe lunch and ask pupils about it directly. Phone interruptions can also move onto watches or laptops, so record where the disruption goes. If tighter storage is under consideration, compare it with the simplest routine that could plausibly achieve the same goal. Account for arrival and dismissal queues, staff time, repeat referrals, family contact and access for students who rely on a phone for health or accessibility.

Tighter storage earns its keep where it reliably removes a problem staff and pupils can already see. A school can judge that through comparable observations before and after a change, with a follow-up once the rollout settles. An effect as small as Harvard’s is unlikely to be distinguishable from ordinary year-to-year variation at a single school. Local decisions should turn on how much better the school day gets and what it takes to make that improvement stick.
