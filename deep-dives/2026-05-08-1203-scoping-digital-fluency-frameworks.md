# Deep dive: Scoping the digital-fluency framework landscape — what's out there, what holds up, and which two or three to read first

_Filed 8 May 2026._

> **Lede:** The "digital natives" line — that students born after 1995 absorb digital fluency by osmosis — has been falsified for a decade in the literature ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10123718/)) and is now collapsing in a second way: the AI-tool gap between students and teachers is not a generational head-start, it's a trajectory divergence. RAND's 2025 panel found teachers are 1.8× more likely than students to use ChatGPT weekly, but students lead in tutoring (64%) and college/career advice (49%); over 80% of US students said teachers had not taught them how to use AI for schoolwork ([RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4180-1.html); [EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/technology/rising-use-of-ai-in-schools-comes-with-big-downsides-for-students/2025/10)). Schools that want to be the people who shape that trajectory — rather than catch up to it — need a digital-fluency framework underneath whatever AI policy they're shipping. This piece maps the public-domain frameworks that are actually contenders, looks at which were designed with AI as a layer **on top of** existing digital fluency (the editorial heart of this piece), runs the sceptic's check, and names the two-to-three documents an Ed Tech Coach planning a pedagogy programme should read first.

## What this piece is — and isn't

This is a **scoping piece, not a recommendation**. The brief is to map the landscape so pedagogy work has somewhere to start. Frameworks shape what gets taught, what gets assessed, and what gets defended at parent meetings; picking one without reading the field is how schools end up with a poster on the wall and a policy that nobody operationalises.

The scope is **high-school age (~14-18)**, with international-school context preferred where the frameworks distinguish. This piece does not cover: in-house frameworks at named peer schools that aren't published publicly (some exist; most aren't downloadable); AI **policy** documents (acceptable-use, prohibited-use) — those are a separate genre from competence frameworks; or curricula at the unit/lesson grain.

## The framework landscape — what actually exists

There are roughly **three families** of public-domain framework worth knowing:

### Family 1: Established digital-literacy/citizenship frameworks (pre-AI, retrofitted)

**ISTE Standards for Students (v4.02, 2024 update)** is the most widely adopted document in this category — adopted by every US state and by school networks worldwide, currently on its 2024 update. The seven student standards are: Empowered Learner, Digital Citizen, Knowledge Constructor, Innovative Designer, Computational Thinker, Creative Communicator, Global Collaborator. ([ISTE Standards](https://iste.org/standards/students); [ISTE 2024 v4.02 PDF](https://cms-live-media.iste.org/ISTE_STANDARDS_2024_v02.pdf); [EdTech Magazine on the 2024 update](https://edtechmagazine.com/k12/article/2024/07/prepare-and-interpret-iste-standards-students)). ISTE explicitly chose to keep the seven-standard structure and update inside it rather than rebuild around AI — their position is that "Empowered Learner" and "Digital Citizen" hold up across technology cycles. That's a defensible call; it's also exactly the call critics push back on, because what counts as "digital citizenship" when half the citizenship choices are now mediated by an LLM is not the same question it was in 2016.

**Common Sense Education's K-12 Digital Citizenship Curriculum** is the other heavyweight in this family — developed with Harvard's Project Zero, widely used internationally, and the curriculum Connecticut adopted as its statewide K-12 model ([Common Sense Education](https://www.commonsense.org/education/digital-citizenship); [press release](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/press-releases/common-sense-educations-digital-literacy-curriculum-selected-as-connecticuts-statewide-model-for-k-12)). The 9-12 band is on a four-unit structure with **updated lessons coming in August 2026** — worth flagging because that's exactly the moment most schools' pedagogy leads would be reading it. Common Sense has a 20-minute "What is AI?" module designed for grades 6-12 plus quick-lessons on AI ethics, and a separate "Navigating AI in Schools" hub ([Navigating AI in Schools](https://www.commonsense.org/education/ai-in-schools); [AI Basics for K-12 Teachers training](https://www.commonsense.org/education/training/ai-basics-for-k-12-teachers)).

**DigComp 2.2 (European Commission, JRC, 2022)** — the EU citizen digital-competence framework, updated to 2.2 in 2022 specifically to integrate AI, IoT, datafication examples ([DigComp 2.2 publication](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC128415); [JRC project page](https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/projects-and-activities/education-and-training/digital-transformation-education/digital-competence-framework-digcomp_en)). The 2.2 update added an appendix with 70+ examples of AI encounters in everyday life. DigComp is the substrate that Estonia, much of Eastern Europe, and pieces of the Singapore framework draw on — worth reading not because international schools will adopt it whole but because it's the most-cited reference frame in the European policy literature.

**Australia's Digital Technologies Curriculum (Version 9, ACARA)** is the national-curriculum example most worth scanning — Version 9 replaced the older Information and Communication Technology Capability strand with a **Digital Literacy** sub-strand and was rolled into AI-explicit teaching guidance ([V9 curriculum](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/resources/stories/artificial-intelligence-in-the-australian-curriculum); [ACARA submission on generative AI](https://www.acara.edu.au/docs/default-source/default-document-library/augovsubmission_ai_australian_curriculum_20230630.pdf?sfvrsn=ef534c07_2)). It's a **national-curriculum document, not a competence framework**, so it doesn't transfer cleanly to an international-school context; it's most useful as a worked example of a state shipping an AI-integrated digital-literacy strand at scale.

**Singapore's National Digital Literacy Programme (NDLP)** is the most-watched state-level secondary programme in the region — four-component structure (Find, Think, Apply, Create), Personal Learning Device for every secondary student since 2024, Edusave-funded ([eastspringsec.moe.edu.sg](https://www.eastspringsec.moe.edu.sg/student-development/ndlp/); [kentridgesec.moe.edu.sg](https://www.kentridgesec.moe.edu.sg/school-information/national-digital-literacy-programme-ndlp/)). It's not a deeply-articulated competence framework in the ISTE/UNESCO sense — it's lighter, and it's bound to MOE's device-and-curriculum stack. It's worth knowing the **structure** (Find/Think/Apply/Create), because it's the cleanest four-word articulation of the same content the bigger frameworks sprawl across.


### Family 2: AI-native frameworks (built around AI as the centre of gravity)

**UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students (September 2024)** is the highest-profile UN-system document in this family — twelve competencies across four dimensions (Human-centred Mindset / Ethics of AI / AI Techniques and Applications / AI System Design), three progression levels (Understand, Apply, Create) ([UNESCO framework page](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-competency-framework-students); [draft framework PDF](https://www.unesco.org/sites/default/files/medias/fichiers/2024/04/UNESCO-Draft-AI-competency-frameworks-for-teachers-and-school-students.pdf); [UNESCO companion teacher framework](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-competency-framework-teachers)). UNESCO's editorial choice is clear: **lead with the human-centred and ethics framing, not the technical**. That's an editorial position, not a neutral one — and it's one of the things to weigh.

**OECD–European Commission "AILit" Framework (review draft, May 2025)** is the newest entry and the most consequential in the medium term. Joint EC + OECD initiative with Code.org support. Four domains (Engage with AI / Create with AI / Manage AI / Design AI), 22 competencies. **Crucially, the AILit framework is being built as the foundation of PISA 2029's Media and AI Literacy assessment** ([AILit Framework site](https://ailiteracyframework.org/); [Review Draft PDF, May 2025](https://ailiteracyframework.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/AILitFramework_ReviewDraft.pdf); [Learn & Work Ecosystem Library summary](https://learnworkecosystemlibrary.com/initiatives/ai-literacy-framework-for-primary-secondary-education-oecd-ec/)). When PISA tests it in 2029, it becomes a de facto international benchmark whether schools adopt it as a framework or not — that's what makes it the document with the longest shadow.

**AI4K12 — Five Big Ideas in AI (CSTA + AAAI, NSF-funded)** is the framework that actually pre-dates the LLM wave — 2018 onwards, oriented to computer-science teachers and curriculum writers. The Five Big Ideas: Perception, Representation & Reasoning, Learning, Natural Interaction, Societal Impact ([AI4K12 site](https://ai4k12.org/); [Five Big Ideas poster PDF](https://ai4k12.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/AI4K12_Five_Big_Ideas_Poster-1.pdf); [AI Thinking preprint, Touretzky & Gardner-McCune 2021](https://ai4k12.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Touretzky_Gardner-McCune_AI-Thinking_2021.pdf)). It's the most **technically grounded** of the AI-native frameworks; less helpful for English/humanities teachers, more helpful for CS-curriculum writers.

**MIT RAISE / Day of AI** is the most **adoption-tested** AI-literacy resource in the family — 670,000+ students in 170+ countries since launch, MIT-developed K-12 curriculum free at point of use ([MIT RAISE](https://raise.mit.edu/); [Day of AI](https://dayofai.org/); [2025 Impact Report](https://raise.mit.edu/stories/2025-day-of-ai-impact-report/)). Strictly speaking it's a curriculum/event programme rather than a competence framework, but its scope-and-sequence assumptions are the de facto framework underneath — and unlike most of the field, the adoption numbers are real and measured.

**Stanford CRAFT** is the one **built explicitly for high-school teachers across multiple disciplines** — i.e. not a CS-only framework. Co-designed AI literacy resources, multidisciplinary by design ([CRAFT site](https://craft.stanford.edu/); [Stanford Accelerator for Learning announcement](https://acceleratelearning.stanford.edu/story/bringing-ai-literacy-to-high-schools/); [CRAFT notebook archive on Observable](https://observablehq.com/@craft)). CRAFT is small in adoption-footprint relative to MIT RAISE but high in pedagogical rigour and teacher-design discipline.

### Family 3: Hybrid / district-level documents

**NYC Public Schools AI Guidance (preliminary, March 2026)** is worth flagging as a **district-level worked example** of the layering question — a "traffic-light" framework for permitted/grey/prohibited uses, sitting on top of NYC's existing digital-citizenship and student-data work, with explicit transparency requirements for vendors and a public comment window ([NYC Public Schools AI guidance page](https://www.schools.nyc.gov/about-us/vision-and-mission/guidance-on-artificial-intelligence); [Chalkbeat coverage](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/03/24/preliminary-ai-policy-nyc-schools/); [NY1 coverage](https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/news/2026/03/24/nyc-department-of-education-preliminary-guidelines-ai-classrooms)).

**Holistic Digital Literacy Framework / AI-Enriched Digital Educational Framework (HDLF / AIEDF)** — academic-literature proposal (Nature *Scientific Reports*, 2026) that reads as the most articulate "AI-on-top-of-digital-literacy" proposal in the academic register ([HDLF/AIEDF paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-47133-1); [systematic review companion](https://openpraxis.org/articles/10.55982/openpraxis.17.4.1012)). The four HDLF cores — Core Computational Concepts and Skills / Digital Citizenship and Ethical Competence / Impact of Computing on Society / Digital Competence and Lifelong Learning — are the substrate; AI competencies bolt on. It's a research paper, not an adoptable framework, but it's the cleanest articulation of the chassis the editorial centre of this piece is asking about.


## The "AI-on-top" lens — which frameworks actually got the chassis right

This is the load-bearing question for the piece. Of the frameworks above, **which were built with AI integration as a layer on top of an existing digital-fluency framework, and which fold AI in at the same level**?

The answer is honest and slightly disappointing: **almost none of the AI-native frameworks were built with the on-top architecture explicit in their design**. UNESCO, OECD/EC AILit, AI4K12, MIT RAISE, Stanford CRAFT — all four can be **mapped onto** an underlying digital-fluency frame, but none of the four were **commissioned to layer onto** an underlying frame. They were commissioned to fill a gap. The digital-fluency assumption is implicit, not explicit, and the connective tissue between (e.g.) ISTE Digital Citizenship and UNESCO Ethics-of-AI has to be drawn by the school, not by the framework.

The honest list of frameworks that actually do build AI as a downstream layer:

1. **Common Sense Education's stack** comes closest among the widely-adopted material — the K-12 Digital Citizenship Curriculum is the substrate, and the AI modules are explicitly extensions of it ("Navigating AI in Schools" is positioned as a hub, not a parallel curriculum). The August 2026 9-12 refresh will tell us whether they keep the layering visible or fold it in.
2. **DigComp 2.2's update method** is conceptually layered — they kept the 21 competence areas and added AI/IoT/data examples inside them. That's the architecture; whether you find the AI examples sufficient is a separate question.
3. **The HDLF/AIEDF academic paper** is the most explicit articulation, but it's a research proposal, not a framework you can adopt.
4. **Australia's V9 Digital Technologies** rebuilt the Information and Communication Technology Capability strand as Digital Literacy and integrated AI into the existing learning-area structure — not the same as a school-adoptable competence framework, but the same architectural decision.

The AI-native frameworks (UNESCO, OECD AILit, AI4K12, RAISE, CRAFT) are **all valuable** for what they articulate about AI specifically, but a school adopting one of them without an underlying digital-fluency frame is buying a roof without walls. The chassis-shape — framework → fluency → AI — is the right shape; the field hasn't caught up to it yet, which means the school doing the layering work is doing original work, not picking it off a shelf.

## Sceptic's corner — where the frameworks fall over

Three failure modes worth naming:

**1. "Performing AI literacy" — the Williamson critique.** Ben Williamson's April 2025 piece ([code acts in education blog](https://codeactsineducation.wordpress.com/2025/04/30/performing-ai-literacy/); [NEPC reprint](https://nepc.colorado.edu/blog/performing-ai-literacy)) argues that the OECD/EC AILit framework, by being built as the foundation of a PISA assessment, **inverts what literacy is supposed to be**. Once a competence is going to be tested at international scale in 2029, schools "teach to the test" four years in advance. The framework gets reduced from "what does a critically literate young person need to be able to think about AI" to "what's measurable on a 90-minute computer-based assessment." Williamson: the framework risks becoming "AI literacy-as-numbers," sacrificing the criticality of media literacy. Audrey Watters has made a parallel critique of the US executive-order push and Code.org's involvement, arguing the literacy on offer is industry-shaped — "learning to prompt" as the new "learning to code" ([Watters, "AI Unleashed"](https://2ndbreakfast.audreywatters.com/ai-unleashed/)). [The Conversation has a longer-form treatment](https://theconversation.com/why-we-should-be-skeptical-of-the-hasty-global-push-to-test-15-year-olds-ai-literacy-in-2029-263695). This isn't a knockdown — PISA assessment isn't intrinsically bad — but it's the most serious objection in the field and any framework choice should be made with eyes open to it.

**2. ISTE-style adoption-without-operationalisation.** ISTE Standards have been adopted by every US state and many countries — the question is what "adoption" means. ISTE's own definition: *"a decision to use the standards in any of various ways is considered an adoption"* ([ISTE Standards page](https://iste.org/standards)). That's a low bar. The honest pattern is that ISTE Standards land as a poster on the wall and an alignment-document in the curriculum binder, and the actual classroom practice runs on whatever the local edtech coach is doing. There's no widely-cited body of evidence that ISTE-adopting schools produce more digitally-literate students than non-adopting schools — partly because the standards are designed to flex, partly because the measurement infrastructure isn't there. A 2024 mapping analysis in *TechTrends* surveyed empirical studies on ISTE outcomes and found the literature is thinner than the adoption story suggests ([Springer Nature TechTrends mapping](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11528-024-00973-y)).

**3. "AI Snake Oil" / Narayanan & Kapoor's broader frame.** Narayanan and Kapoor's 2024 book argues that a great deal of education AI — the predictive tools, the surveillance-tools-as-learning-tools, the "personalised learning" engines that don't have evidence behind their personalisation claim — is functioning as snake oil rather than as the AI it markets itself as ([AI Snake Oil book site/Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Snake-Oil-Artificial-Intelligence-Difference/dp/069124913X); [LCFI review](https://www.lcfi.ac.uk/news-events/blog/post/aisnakeoil)). The relevance to framework choice: any framework whose AI competence section is built on the assumption that the AI tools in classrooms work as advertised is going to underweight the criticality students need to detect when they don't. UNESCO's "Human-centred Mindset" and "Ethics of AI" dimensions get closest to this; AI4K12's "Societal Impact" big-idea touches it; the OECD AILit framework's "Manage AI" domain is the most explicit. But none of the four canonical AI-native frameworks treat snake-oil-detection as a first-class competence. That's a gap.

A fourth, structural worry: **the digital-natives critique is now twenty years old** ([Bennett, Maton & Kervin 2008](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131510002563); [PMC narrative review 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10123718/)) and the literature has been clear for a decade that exposure ≠ literacy. Any framework that doesn't price that in — that assumes students arrive with the foundation and need only the AI layer — is mis-specified. The 2025 RAND survey is the relevant data point: 80%+ of US students reported teachers had not taught them how to use AI for schoolwork; the gap between teachers and students isn't generational, it's curatorial. Schools that lean on "they're digital natives" framing in 2026 are repeating a 2005 mistake.


## Peer-school frameworks — what's actually publicly shipped

A reasonable question for any pedagogy lead: what have international schools shipped publicly? The honest answer: **less than you'd hope**.

- **NIST International School (Bangkok)** publishes IB-continuum information and AI-in-classroom blog content but no public competence framework ([NIST](https://www.nist.ac.th/about-nist)).
- **ASIJ (American School in Japan)** publishes a divisional Learning Framework — the High School framework is partially indexed via course-share platforms but the full PDF isn't on a public ASIJ URL ([Course Hero indexed copy](https://www.coursehero.com/file/228222993/24-25-ASIJ-Learning-Frameworkpdf/)).
- **Concordia International School Shanghai** publishes the existence of Digital Literacy Coaches at each division, but not a public scope-and-sequence ([Concordia Shanghai Technology page](https://www.concordiashanghai.org/community/services/technology)).
- **Shanghai American School** publishes a Technology hub page, no public competence framework ([SAS Technology](https://www.saschina.org/academics/technology)).
- **FOBISIA and EARCOS** have run AI as a conference theme through 2025 and have it on the 2026 leadership-conference programmes — *EARCOS 2025 specifically referenced a "Global AI Readiness Study" benchmarking work* ([EARCOS 2025 / ISC Research](https://iscresearch.com/events/earcos-2025/); [FOBISIA Leadership Conference](https://www.fobisia.org/conferences/leadership-conference)) — but neither network has shipped a public framework as a regional document.
- **Singapore American School** restructured to a competency-based curriculum in 2021 with a competency framework introduced the following year ([Getting Smart podcast on SAS](https://www.gettingsmart.com/podcast/20-signs-of-progress-at-singapore-american-school/)); the public-facing detail of that framework's digital-fluency content was not surfaced in research for this piece.

The pattern is consistent: **most peer schools have something internal, very few have something public.** The 2025-26 EARCOS Global AI Readiness Study is the most promising regional signal — worth tracking through the rest of 2026.

## Synthesis — which 2-3 frameworks the pedagogy lead should read first

If a pedagogy lead has time for three documents and three only, the honest list is:

1. **OECD/EC AILit Framework — Review Draft, May 2025** ([PDF](https://ailiteracyframework.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/AILitFramework_ReviewDraft.pdf)). Read it first not because it's the best, but because **it's the document with the longest shadow**. PISA 2029 will test on it; school leaders five years from now will be asked about it; the field will move around it. Read it with Williamson's critique in the other hand and make up your own mind.
2. **UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students, September 2024** ([draft PDF](https://www.unesco.org/sites/default/files/medias/fichiers/2024/04/UNESCO-Draft-AI-competency-frameworks-for-teachers-and-school-students.pdf)). Read it second because it's the framework that **leads with ethics and human-centredness rather than technique**. Whether you agree with that editorial choice or not, the document is internally coherent in a way the OECD draft is still finding, and the 12-competency × 3-progression-level lattice is the most scrutable structure in the field.
3. **ISTE Standards for Students v4.02, 2024** ([standards page](https://iste.org/standards/students); [PDF](https://cms-live-media.iste.org/ISTE_STANDARDS_2024_v02.pdf)) **paired with Common Sense Education's K-12 Digital Citizenship Curriculum** ([Common Sense Education](https://www.commonsense.org/education/digital-citizenship)). Read these together as the **chassis** under whichever AI-layer framework the school chooses. ISTE for the seven-standard structural backbone; Common Sense for the lesson-grain operationalisation. The August 2026 9-12 Common Sense refresh is the moment to re-read.

Two **honourable mentions** worth at least knowing exist: **AI4K12's Five Big Ideas** (read if the school has a strong CS strand to anchor the AI competencies in); **DigComp 2.2** (read for the "AI examples added inside an existing competence frame" architectural pattern, which is the chassis-shape this piece is asking about).

### The four questions to ask any framework before adopting it

The editorial centre of this piece is the *pathway-curation problem* — students are going somewhere with AI, teachers are going somewhere else, and if there's no curated pathway nobody is shaping the trajectory. Four questions to ask any framework with that in mind:

1. **Does this framework distinguish between "digital exposure" and "digital fluency"?** If it doesn't — if it treats the student arriving in Year 9 as a digital native who needs only the AI layer — it's mis-specified. The 2008-2023 literature on the digital-natives myth is settled; any framework still gesturing at it is behind.
2. **Does this framework treat AI as a layer or as a parallel?** A parallel-treatment framework has an "AI literacy" strand alongside "digital citizenship" with no prescribed handshake between them. A layered-treatment framework specifies how the citizenship competencies are exercised under AI conditions (e.g. how do you "manage your digital identity" when an LLM is co-authoring half your output?). Layered is harder to teach and harder to assess — and it's the chassis-shape worth defending.
3. **Does this framework give the school a way to handle the "students-going-sideways" reality?** The pathway-curation problem is that students are picking AI tools and AI uses the school didn't specify, and most frameworks don't have a competency for "evaluate a tool the school hasn't approved and decide whether to use it." UNESCO's "Human-centred Mindset" comes closest; OECD AILit's "Manage AI" gestures at it; Common Sense's digital citizenship is the most operational on it. None of them are fully there.
4. **Does this framework price in critical orientation, or only skill?** The Williamson / Watters / Narayanan-Kapoor cluster of critique is the load-bearing one for international schools that don't want to end up running a vendor-shaped curriculum. A framework whose AI competencies are *only* skill-acquisition (prompting, evaluating, creating) and not also *critical orientation* (whose interests does this AI serve, when does it function as snake oil, when should I refuse to use it) is going to age badly.

## Next watch

- **OECD AILit Framework final text** — the May 2025 review draft is open for consultation; the final document is expected in 2026. Worth tracking the diff between draft and final, especially the criticality-vs-measurability split.
- **PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy assessment** — the framing instrument follows the framework. When the assessment specifications publish (expected late 2027/2028), that's when the framework's editorial choices become testable behaviours.
- **Common Sense Education's August 2026 9-12 refresh** — exactly the moment a school's pedagogy lead would want updated material in hand.
- **EARCOS Global AI Readiness Study** — flagged at EARCOS 2025; if it produces a public document in 2026, it's the first regional benchmarking signal worth comparing to.
- **UNESCO companion teacher framework** — the September 2024 release also includes an [AI Competency Framework for Teachers](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-competency-framework-teachers); reading the two together is the cleanest way to think about teacher-development implications of whichever student framework gets adopted.
