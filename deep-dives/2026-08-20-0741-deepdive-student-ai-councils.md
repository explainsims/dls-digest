# One Seat in Eighteen: What a Standing School AI Council Actually Changes When Students Are On It

_Filed 20 August 2026._

> **Thesis:** The student-authored document was the easy part; a standing body that survives past graduation is the hard one. The case for putting students on a High School AI Council is real but narrower than its champions claim. The honest evidence does not show that student membership produces measurably *better decisions* — the school-governance branch of the student-voice literature has hunted that outcome for twenty years and mostly not found it, and only got a validated way to measure the practice at all in 2025. What student presence reliably buys is different and still worth having: legitimacy, buy-in, early warning on how the tools are actually used, and — the part that connects to everything the students got right about academic-integrity procedure — a body that can keep an integrity rule calibrated to the students it will be used against. The failure mode is equally well-documented and thirty years old: a seat that is decoration. Northwestern's AI committee seats exactly one undergraduate among eighteen members ([Northwestern Office of the Provost, 2026](https://www.northwestern.edu/provost/about/committees/ai-and-education-advisory-committee/)); that ratio, not the Brookings brochure, is the design problem. Build the council for the narrow real gains, wall off what students genuinely cannot be shown, and engineer against tokenism and turnover from the first meeting — or don't bother standing it up.

## 1. From a weekend bill to a standing body — and why the body is the harder problem

An earlier deep dive in this series dissected a document: the STUDENTS FIRST Act, a model bill that roughly ninety-eight high-schoolers argued into existence over a July weekend at a replica Senate chamber ([earlier deep dive, 5 August 2026](2026-08-05-1857-deepdive-student-written-ai-policy.public.md)). The finding was that the students had done something the working adult baseline mostly had not: they split the single question every AI policy collapses into — *is this cheating?* — into four questions that need four different mechanisms. **Permission** (may you use it), **disclosure** (did you say you did), **evidence** (what actually shows a rule was broken), and **appeal** (how you contest the accusation). Having separated them, they refused to let an AI-detector score be a verdict — a flag triggers a human investigation, not a grade.

Good procedure. But a document has no owner. A weekend simulation produces a text and then disperses; the text does not update itself when a new model ships in October, and it does not notice that the detector everyone bought misfires on the kids who write English as a fourth language. The interesting question the document raises but cannot answer is *who holds and evolves this over time* — and that is a question about a body, not a bill.

That is the pivot, and it is why the standing council is the harder problem. Rebecca Winthrop's Brookings essay of 18 August 2026, "Enhancing student agency through school AI councils," is the current high-water mark of the argument that the body should include students ([Brookings, 18 August 2026](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/enhancing-student-agency-through-school-ai-councils/)). Her thesis, in her framing, is a move "from giving students voice to centering it" — students not consulted but co-designing: beta-testing tools, curating AI-literacy resources, co-developing policy, "contributing to procurement decisions," and reviewing data privacy. It is a genuinely thoughtful piece, and it should be read with its incentive in plain view. Winthrop directs the Center for Universal Education at Brookings, a body whose stated purpose is to champion exactly this kind of youth agency ([Brookings, 18 August 2026](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/enhancing-student-agency-through-school-ai-councils/)); the essay's own editor's note discloses that Microsoft's Copilot "provided the search for relevant sources and light copyediting." None of that makes the argument wrong. It makes it advocacy, and advocacy is exactly the thing to test against independent evidence rather than repeat. This piece takes Winthrop's thesis seriously enough to try to break it.

## 2. The actual inventory: what student-inclusive AI governance looks like when you go looking

Before theory, a census. Go looking for real bodies — not aspirations — where students sit on AI governance, and here is what is actually there in August 2026, with the honest read on each.

**Study Hall School, Lucknow, India.** Winthrop's lead example: after learning about AI-enabled cognitive offloading from the school's founder, students formed an AI task force, researched benefits and risks, "developed guidelines for responsible use," and are "helping shape school policy" ([Brookings, 18 August 2026](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/enhancing-student-agency-through-school-ai-councils/)). The school is real — founded 1986 by Dr Urvashi Sahni ([Study Hall Educational Foundation](https://studyhallschool.in/)). The tell: Sahni is herself a Brookings Center for Universal Education nonresident fellow ([Wikipedia: Urvashi Sahni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urvashi_Sahni), corroborated on the school site). This is the advocate's own network describing the advocate's own example, and no independent evaluation of what the task force actually changed appears in the public record. Treat it as an existence proof, not evidence of effect.

**SPARK — Student Policy Advisory for Responsible AI in K-12, Austin, Texas.** A junior at Westwood High School, Ruhan Gupta, has *proposed* a twelve-member high-school council to his district to "test and audit AI tools, contribute to policy development, and lead engagement with peers and families" ([Brookings, 18 August 2026](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/enhancing-student-agency-through-school-ai-councils/); corroborated in [The 74's summary of Gupta's op-ed](https://www.the74million.org/article/when-it-comes-to-developing-ai-rules-who-asked-the-students/)). Note the verb: *proposed*. As of now this is a well-argued student op-ed, not an operating body.

**Colorado.** A group of students is urging state lawmakers to establish an AI-in-education task force that would advise districts ([Colorado Politics, 11 August 2026](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/2026/08/11/colorado-students-push-ai-task-force-as-schools-navigate-new-tech-era/)). Again: a proposal, advisory, upstream of any seat.

**The IB Youth Advisory Council.** The most relevant international example, and a real seated body: the International Baccalaureate stood up its first-ever Youth Advisory Council in 2025 — twelve members, IB students and recent alumni, one-year terms, four meetings (one in person at the IB Global Conference in The Hague, 22–25 October 2025, three virtual), working alongside IB leadership including the Director General ([IB, 2025](https://www.ibo.org/news/news-about-the-ib/ib-announces-its-first-ever-youth-advisory-council/)). Two caveats that matter. It is *organisation-level*, not school-level — it advises the IB, not a headteacher on a Tuesday. And it is *advisory*: it supports "design, development and implementation," it does not decide. It was also selected from roughly 1,400 applicants down to twelve — an elite filter worth returning to.

**Northwestern University's AI and Education Advisory Committee.** The cleanest actually-seated example, and the most instructive. It has eighteen members. One is an undergraduate (Umar Kamara, McCormick School of Engineering); the other seventeen are faculty and staff. Its charge is to advise the Provost "on pedagogical issues and resource development related to AI, including academic integrity and instructional policy and practices" ([Northwestern, 2026](https://www.northwestern.edu/provost/about/committees/ai-and-education-advisory-committee/)). This is higher education, not a high school, but it is the shape the K-12 version will most likely take: a mature institution that genuinely wants student input seats exactly one student among seventeen adults, advisory to a senior administrator. That is not tokenism by intent. It is tokenism by ratio, and the two are hard to tell apart from the student's chair.

**The default, for contrast.** The modal school "AI task force" has no students on it at all. Sunnyvale School District's 2025–26 body is a *staff* AI task force ([Sunnyvale SD, 2025](https://www.sesd.org/view-all-news/default-board-post-page/~board/sunnyvale-news/post/sunnyvale-school-district-launches-2025-2026-staff-ai-task-force)). And the governance wave is real and adult-run: CoSN's 2026 survey found districts with an AI framework jumped from 57% to 79% year on year, and those addressing AI in academic-integrity policy from 32% to 46% ([CoSN, 2026, via EdWeek reporting](https://www.edweek.org/technology/5-best-practices-for-crafting-a-school-or-district-ai-policy/2025/10)). The councils are being built at speed. Students are, so far, mostly not in the room.

The pattern in the census is the story: a great deal of *proposed* and *advisory* student involvement, a few genuinely seated students who are heavily outnumbered, and one high-profile *simulation*. The thing everyone is citing as a movement is, on the ground, a handful of real seats and a lot of op-eds. That is not a reason to dismiss it. It is a reason to be precise about what a seat is worth.

## 3. The ladder is thirty years old: tokenism, and why "centre voice" is the top rung relabelled

The AI framing makes this feel new. It is not. Student participation in school decisions is one of the most thoroughly studied and most thoroughly disappointed ideas in education, and the map was drawn in 1992.

Roger Hart's *Children's Participation: From Tokenism to Citizenship* (UNICEF Innocenti Essays No. 4, 1992), adapting Sherry Arnstein's 1969 ladder of citizen participation, laid out eight rungs ([Hart, 1992, UNICEF ICDC; ERIC ED359090](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED359090)). The bottom three — manipulation, decoration, tokenism — Hart labelled *non-participation*. Tokenism, in his exact sense, is where children "are ostensibly given a voice but, in practice, have little or no discretion regarding the subject and how it is presented and little or no opportunity to formulate their own opinions." The top rungs are child-initiated and adult-shared decisions. The whole point of the ladder is that a seat can sit on any rung, and most sit low.

Winthrop's "giving voice versus centering voice" distinction — which she attributes to Emily Markovich Morris and Omaer Naeem's 2023 Brookings work "Handing over the mic" ([Markovich Morris & Naeem, Brookings, August 2023](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/handing-over-the-mic-the-difference-between-centering-and-giving-youth-voices-in-research-and-practice/)) — is Hart's ladder in new clothes. "Giving voice" is the consulted-but-not-deciding middle; "centering voice" is the top. The relabelling is fine; the useful thing is that it comes attached to a body of research about how rarely schools climb the ladder — and the most damning line comes from the advocates' own side. Brookings' own November 2025 student-voice brief, drawing on a survey of 9,580 young people aged 12–22 across ten countries, states flatly that "even when countries have mandated schools to have student councils to harness the leadership of young people, these councils only weigh in on low-stakes decisions—such as organizing events—and not more high-stakes decision making like school-wide change and transformation" ([Hoysala & Markovich Morris, Brookings, 21 November 2025](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/prioritizing-student-voice-in-education-system-decision-making-and-transformation/)).

Read that twice, because it is the strongest sceptic's evidence in the whole file and it is friendly fire. The people making the case for student AI councils are reporting that the default student council, even where the law requires one, does dances. An AI council that includes students starts on that rung by default and has to climb deliberately. Anyone who claims student membership is self-evidently "centering voice" is describing the brochure, not the ladder.

## 4. Does student presence improve the decisions, or the legitimacy? (the crux)

Here is the question the advocacy quietly elides: when a student sits on the council, do the school's AI *decisions* get *better* — or does the school just get better *buy-in* for the decisions it was going to make anyway? These are different goods, and the evidence treats them very differently.

Start with the honest state of the field. For twenty years the claim that student voice improves engagement, agency and learning "rested primarily on qualitative evidence, because the field lacked a valid and reliable instrument to measure student voice practices in schools" — a gap only closed in 2025 with the first validated student-voice scales ([Conner, Holquist, Mitra & Boat, *AERA Open*, 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/23328584251326893)). That is a remarkable admission for a field this old and this well-funded: until last year, nobody could reliably measure the thing everybody was advocating.

Now the outcome evidence, such as it is. Dana Mitra's foundational question — literally the title of her 2004 paper, "Can Increasing 'Student Voice' in Schools Lead to Gains in Youth Development?" ([Mitra, *Teachers College Record*, 2004](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9620.2004.00354.x)) — has a nuanced 2025 answer, and the nuance is exactly the thing that matters for councils. The recent large-scale work finds that having *one teacher* who uses student-voice practices is associated with "significantly greater agency, better math grades, higher grade point averages, and lower absence rates." But it also finds that participation in *school-level* student-voice opportunities is "unrelated to improved student achievement" ([*Journal of Educational Change*, 2025, via PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11836230/)).

That distinction is load-bearing and it cuts against the council. The measured academic benefit of student voice lives in the *classroom* — a teacher who listens. The *governance* branch — committees, councils, school-level bodies, which is precisely what an AI Council is — is where the outcome evidence is weakest to absent. So the defensible claim about a student-inclusive AI Council is *not* "it will produce better decisions" or "it will improve outcomes." The honest claim is narrower: student voice at the governance level is associated with youth-development goods — agency, belonging, competence, the "ABCs" Mitra documents — and with legitimacy and engagement, not with measured decision quality.

Offered as interpretation rather than established fact: this is not a weak case, but it is a *different* case than the one being sold, and the difference is where schools get into trouble. Brookings, Day of AI and every vendor with a student advisory board have an incentive to let "students improve legitimacy" quietly become "students improve decisions," because the second sentence justifies more and sounds better. Refuse the slide. A student-inclusive AI Council is worth building for legitimacy, early warning, and calibration. Sell it internally as a decision-quality upgrade and you have promised something the evidence does not support, and manufactured a disappointment on a two-year delay.

## 5. Where student presence genuinely pays: the four functions, mapped to the four questions

The legitimacy case is real but it is not the whole case, and the rest of it is where student membership earns its keep on the merits. The argument is not "students have a right to be heard" (true, and a slogan). It is that on specific questions students hold information adults structurally cannot have, and those questions map cleanly onto the four-questions frame from the earlier analysis in this series.

**Evidence — the ground truth of use.** The single hardest thing for an adult committee to know is how the tools are *actually* being used this month: which apps, for which tasks, with which workarounds, under which unstated norms. Adults survey it; students live it. The College Board data makes the scale concrete — high-schoolers' use of generative AI for schoolwork rose from 79% in January 2025 to 84% by May 2025 ([College Board, 6 October 2025](https://newsroom.collegeboard.org/new-research-majority-high-school-students-use-generative-ai-schoolwork)). (Winthrop cites "85%"; the College Board's actual student-usage figure is 84% — the 85% does not match that usage stat. A small drift, but the kind worth catching.) A standing council with students on it is the cheapest early-warning system a school can build for that 84% — the body that notices the new tool in October, not the policy review in June.

**Evidence and appeal — calibrating the integrity rule to the people it will hit.** This is the strongest function-specific argument for student membership, and it is an equity argument, not an agency slogan. The earlier analysis's spine was that a detector flag is not a verdict, because the detectors do not work: Liang and colleagues' *Patterns* study ran seven GPT detectors over essays and misclassified more than half of non-native English writers' TOEFL essays as AI-generated while clearing over 90% of native-speaker essays ([Liang et al., *Patterns*, 2023](https://www.cell.com/patterns/fulltext/S2666-3899(23)00130-7)); Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin's detector on exactly that reasoning ([Vanderbilt, 16 August 2023](https://www.vanderbilt.edu/brightspace/2023/08/16/guidance-on-ai-detection-and-why-were-disabling-turnitins-ai-detector/)). In a highly multilingual school where a large share of students write English as a second, third or fourth language, the falsely-accused population is the modal student. The students on the council are the standing check that keeps a detector-as-verdict regime from being adopted over the heads of the people it will misfire on — because they are those people, or sit next to them. An adult committee can *know* the finding; a student council makes it *unignorable* in the room where the tool gets approved.

**Disclosure — is the rule followable or performative?** The earlier analysis's most borrowable idea was disclosure-as-the-offence: cite your AI use or it is plagiarism. But a disclosure grammar only works if students can actually follow it, and students are the only people who can tell you whether "cite where and how you used AI" is a clear instruction or an unfollowable one that will criminalise honest confusion. That is design feedback adults cannot generate.

**Permission and beta-testing — unintended consequences.** Winthrop's "beta-test AI tools via design sprints" is the function with the best cost-benefit: students surface the misuse pathway, the accessibility failure, the way the tool actually behaves in a real classroom, before procurement not after. This is genuinely valuable and genuinely low-risk — right up until it shades into "review procurement contracts," which is a different thing and the subject of the next section.

The through-line: student presence does not improve decisions in general, but it measurably improves the school's *read* on tool use and the *calibration* of its integrity procedure — which is precisely, and only, what a standing body evolving the permission/disclosure/evidence/appeal procedure needs from its members. That is the defensible case, and it is enough.

## 6. "It's our data" meets the NDA: what a student council realistically can and cannot see

The most quotable line in Winthrop's essay is a student's, and it is the one to interrogate hardest: "We should be able to review procurement contracts—it's our data" ([Brookings, 18 August 2026](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/enhancing-student-agency-through-school-ai-councils/)). It is a great line. It is also, taken literally, a promise safeguarding law will not let a school keep, and the gap between the sentiment and the mechanism is where a well-meaning council gets a school into trouble.

A signed edtech procurement contract typically contains three things a student cannot be shown. Commercial-in-confidence pricing and terms the vendor discloses under a non-disclosure agreement. Provisions bound by student-data-privacy regimes — FERPA-style confidentiality obligations in the US, and their analogues elsewhere — under which the contract *itself* is the instrument controlling who may see student data ([student-privacy contracting overview, studentprivacy.ed.gov](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/sites/default/files/resource_document/file/Vendor%20FAQ.pdf)). And, not infrequently, references to other students' records. "It's our data" is precisely the reason a student *cannot* be handed the document: the data-protection logic that makes the data theirs is the same logic that makes the contract confidential.

For an international school this is sharper, not softer. Under a data-sovereignty regime where student data cannot leave the school's control and sits under the host country's jurisdiction rather than any US-federal machinery, the compliance constraint is the dominant one in the whole system, and a student council is downstream of it. So be exact about the realistic student role, because the honest version is still substantial:

- **What a student council can do on procurement:** shape the *requirements* the school takes to a vendor — what data practices to demand, what to refuse to sign, which use of student data is a dealbreaker; pressure-test the vendor's claims in a live demo; and write the privacy *asks* that the adults then negotiate into the contract. That is real influence over the thing that matters — the school's negotiating position — and it does not require showing anyone a confidential document.
- **What it cannot do:** read the signed contract, see the pricing, or handle anything containing identifiable student data.

Advocacy that promises students will "review procurement contracts" is writing a cheque the compliance office will bounce, and when it bounces the student learns that the seat was decoration after all. The defensible design gives students authority over the *ask* and the *demo* — upstream, where they can actually move the outcome — and is honest that the paperwork is walled off. That is not a fudge; it is the difference between a council that lasts and one that collapses the first time a student asks to see the file and is told no.

## 7. The failure modes, searched for on purpose

The strongest case against a student-inclusive AI Council is not that students are incapable. It is that the structure fails in five well-documented ways, and each has to be engineered against or the council becomes worse than none — because a tokenistic council manufactures legitimacy without the substance, which is the one outcome worse than visible neglect.

**Tokenism by ratio.** Northwestern's one-of-eighteen is the warning ([Northwestern, 2026](https://www.northwestern.edu/provost/about/committees/ai-and-education-advisory-committee/)). A lone student among adults, however well-intentioned the invitation, sits on Hart's lower rungs by arithmetic: they cannot form a bloc, cannot carry a vote, and face every documented pressure to agree with the credentialed adults around them. "Centering voice" is structurally impossible for a single token.

**Selection bias, and the equity inversion.** Councils recruit the self-selected engaged — the confident, the already-heard, the debate-club fluent. The IB Youth Advisory Council filtered 1,400 applicants to 12 ([IB, 2025](https://www.ibo.org/news/news-about-the-ib/ib-announces-its-first-ever-youth-advisory-council/)); that is an elite instrument by design. Here is the trap specific to AI: the students most likely to volunteer and be selected are the high-achieving, fluent-English, low-risk writers — which is to say, the students *least* likely to be falsely flagged by a detector. If the council's equity rationale is protecting the multilingual student from a detector-as-verdict regime, and the council is composed of exactly the students that regime does not threaten, the rationale eats itself. The mitigation is deliberate: seat the students the policy will actually be used against, not only the ones who raise their hands.

**Continuity.** Students graduate. Every year a fraction of the council's institutional memory walks out the door, and a body with no memory re-litigates settled questions and loses the thread of a multi-year policy — a problem sharper for student councils than any adult committee, and one the advocacy literature is largely silent on. A council that forgets last year's reasoning is doomed to relive last year's argument.

**The consultation-versus-decision gap.** The best-documented failure in the whole student-voice literature: input is solicited, gathered, thanked, and ignored, and students learn faster than adults expect that their voice was ornamental ([the tokenism critique, e.g. Perry-Hazan & Bauml, *Sage*, 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09075682231169363)). Brookings' own brief concedes this is the norm even where councils are legally mandated ([Hoysala & Markovich Morris, 21 November 2025](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/prioritizing-student-voice-in-education-system-decision-making-and-transformation/)). A council with no defined decisions of its own is this failure waiting to happen.

**The honest evidentiary limit.** And underneath all of it: there is no hard evidence that student councils improve *outcomes*. The case is legitimacy, calibration, early warning, and youth development. If a school cannot state a real function it wants the council to perform — a decision it will actually let students own — it should not stand one up to perform participation.

## 8. What a good student-inclusive HS AI Council actually looks like

The residue, as the earlier analysis put it, is smaller than the brochure and firmer than the slogan. Here is a concrete, evidence-grounded design for a High School AI Council that includes students and survives its own failure modes.

**State the purpose honestly, and measure that.** This body exists to keep the school's AI and academic-integrity procedure calibrated and current, to give it legitimacy with the students it governs, and to serve as early warning on how the tools are actually used — *not* to raise attainment, because the evidence does not support that promise ([*Journal of Educational Change*, 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11836230/)). Name the real function so success is measurable: did the integrity procedure get better calibrated, did the school catch the new tool early, do students regard the rules as legitimate.

**Membership: a bloc, not a token.** The one-of-eighteen ratio is the thing to design out. Either give students a real share of seats — a third to a half, enough to carry a position and second each other — or run a parallel student chamber with a defined remit whose recommendations get a mandatory response. And recruit against the selection bias on purpose: seat the multilingual and EAL students the detector evidence is actually about, not only the fluent volunteers, or the equity rationale collapses.

**Mandate: own something, advise on the rest.** Give the council *real authority* over the pieces where student information is decisive and the stakes are governable: the integrity procedure itself — the four questions of permission defaults, the disclosure grammar, the evidence standards, and the appeal route; the AI-literacy resources; and the rollout of a published, per-course AI statement. Make it *advisory but consequential* on tool adoption and policy. And give it *requirements-shaping* authority — not document access — on procurement and data, per the previous section. The rule of thumb: students decide where they hold the information and the risk is bounded; students advise where the decision needs authority they cannot hold.

**Write the wall explicitly.** Students see: aggregated, anonymised usage data; tool demos; draft policy; the integrity procedure. Students do not see: signed contracts, pricing, identifiable student data, or individual integrity cases. Putting the wall in writing at the first meeting is what lets a student be told "no" to the contract without learning the seat was fake — because the boundary was a stated design principle, not an improvised brush-off.

**Cadence and continuity, engineered.** Meet on a real cycle — monthly in term, not annually — because a body that convenes once a year is decoration. Stagger terms so half the students carry over each year, and keep a *living decision log* — the council's running record of the four-questions procedure and why each choice was made — so knowledge survives graduation. Appoint a faculty custodian who holds continuity without holding capture: present to carry the memory, disciplined not to fill every silence.

**Anti-tokenism, made mechanical.** The single cheapest and most powerful device: every council recommendation gets a *written adult response* — acted on or not, and why. That one habit converts consultation into accountability and is the difference between Hart's tokenism rung and genuine participation. Publish the council's remit with the named decisions it actually owns. And put students in the room when the decision is *made*, not consulted beforehand and thanked afterward — the distinction between centering and giving voice, made concrete.

The sentence to carry out of this is not "students should be on the AI council because it is their future." That is true and it is a slogan, and slogans build tokenistic councils. The defensible version is narrower and more useful: **a standing AI Council is the body that can own and evolve the permission-disclosure-evidence-appeal procedure the students already got right, and student membership is worth building not because it demonstrably improves the decisions but because it keeps that procedure calibrated to the people it is used against, catches the tools early, and makes the rules legitimate — provided you give the students a decision they actually own, wall off what they genuinely cannot see, and engineer against the token seat, the elite filter, and the graduating memory from the first meeting.** Build it for what the evidence supports. Do not sell it as what it does not.
