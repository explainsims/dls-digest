Friday, 27 November 2026

Top story

University students managed their learning better when AI came with structured guidance.

Xing Liu and Yumeng Yang's 26 November meta-analysis pulled together 76 effect sizes from 26 studies on self-regulated learning. That's the ability to set a goal, notice whether a strategy is working and change course when it isn't. In the ten studies that directly compared generative AI with and without an added instructional intervention, the pooled difference was moderate, at Hedges' g = 0.528, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.359 to 0.697. All ten direct comparisons were in higher education. The figure gives us a reason to take the design of classroom guidance seriously and leaves open how much it helps younger students. Liu and Yang, 26 November.

There's a second comparison in the paper worth keeping separate. The researchers looked at studies that compared AI-supported learning with learning without AI. They estimated a positive effect for AI alone and a larger positive effect for AI combined with instruction. The difference between those two estimates fell short of statistical significance (p = 0.089). The direct comparison above asks a cleaner question for a school that has already made a tool available. What happens when students get help deciding what to do with it? The paper also notes that it could not account for correlations among outcomes measured on the same students, which could make the pooled estimates look more precise than they are. Liu and Yang, methods and results.

For international schools, there's a small enough classroom question here to try with the tools they already have. In a physics lesson, a student predicts which variable matters, asks an approved assistant for one challenge to that prediction and decides whether to revise it. On a second problem, the student has to spot the same trap unaided. The first answer gives the teacher something to discuss. The second makes it possible to see whether the student learned a way to check their thinking.

Other stories

  • Tokyo puts teacher workload on the agenda for its school AI platform. On 26 November, the Tokyo Metropolitan Board of Education opened comments on its draft school work reform plan through 25 December. The board's materials say the existing Toritsu AI environment was built for 256 metropolitan schools and 160,000 people. Its chart classifies 14.7% of configured AI menus as school administration, with the rest for learning. Those are categories of available menus. How often staff used them and how much time they saved are different measurements. The draft proposes practical staff training that includes checking AI answers and examples built around everyday work, such as drafting school documents, organising minutes and preparing explanations. A school could test the same proposition on one repetitive job, with fictional details where necessary. Time the draft, the check against the source and the corrections someone makes before submission. The document the recipient can actually use is where the stopwatch should stop. Tokyo Metropolitan Board of Education consultation, 26 November. Board's draft, pp. 38–39.

  • Australia passes a duty of care that reaches AI chatbots. Communications Minister Anika Wells announced parliamentary passage on 25 November. The legislation requires covered services such as social media platforms, games, apps and AI chatbots to protect Australian users from serious harms, with specific protections for under-18s. The department says the duty takes effect 12 months after Royal Assent. The minister puts potential penalties at up to 5% of global turnover or A$109.2 million, whichever is greater. A reporting button is useful after something has gone wrong. The duty asks providers to take responsibility earlier, including for how features behave. That suggests a question for school procurement even outside Australia. What changes when a user is identified as a minor, and where can a school see that change? A supplier can show what a student account encounters in a scripted test and who at the supplier is responsible for fixing a failure. This is a decision about Australian services, with any effect on other markets still to be seen. Communications minister, 25 November. Department summary, 27 November.

  • Goa opens an AI study lab with its disability commissioner. The nonprofit I-Stem launched Nclude Labs at Santa Cruz Higher Secondary School on 26 November and exchanged a memorandum of understanding with the state disability commissioner's office for a wider rollout. The programme uses iTutor, a voice-based study tool within I-Stem's wider Nclude platform. A learner can ask a question aloud and work at their own pace. The organiser lists labs already operating in four other Indian states. That's evidence of places where the programme operates. Whether students can use their own textbooks and class materials independently, and whether the independence lasts beyond the lab session, are results to look for next. Voice can give a learner a way to ask for help when printed text is a barrier. It also raises practical questions about recognition across accents, what happens with a formula and how a student can find and revisit an answer. Try a real lesson's materials in the format the learner will use. An impressive conversation in a quiet room sets a low bar. Nclude Labs release, 26 November.

  • The UAE gives families a guide to the AI curriculum. The Ministry of Education and the office of the Minister of State for Artificial Intelligence launched a parent guide on 26 November for the curriculum introduced in public schools in 2025–26. It outlines seven areas from basic concepts and data to fairness, project design and responsibility. Families get advice on protecting private information, checking claims and setting age-appropriate boundaries. The useful idea for an international school is to explain what a child will actually do in a lesson. A family can make sense of verifying an AI claim when the child brings an example and a source to the kitchen table. They can also ask a better question about homework when they know how the teacher distinguishes a permitted hint from submitting someone else's reasoning. A parent guide has an everyday job. It gives the child and the adult the same language for asking what help was used, what was checked and what the child still needs to do for themselves. Emirates News Agency, 26 November. English parent guide.

On the radar

  • The House of Lords asks what evidence supports school technology. On 26 November, Baroness Ramsey of Wall Heath pointed to the national EdTech Testbed and said the evidence on AI-enabled personalised learning was mixed and developing. England's Department for Education had separately opened a consultation on a proposed product safety certification scheme on 10 November. The questions are connected. Schools want to know if a product meets an agreed safety standard and whether children learn with it under real classroom conditions. The coming evaluations are worth looking at for which products are tried, what assessment they use and how they report results for pupils who need accommodations. Hansard, 26 November. Department for Education consultation, 10 November.

  • Macao's teachers are due to train their own small models. UNU Macau's 20 November programme lists three workshops for 28 November, with registration closed on 22 November. One session has teachers train models on images, audio or gestures and plug them into Scratch-based games. Another starts with the intended learning and has teachers prototype a project around it. The useful thing to watch is what survives the trip back to class. A Scratch game that responds to a hand gesture is a starting point. A pupil who can explain why the game misses a new gesture has started to learn how training examples shape a model's behaviour. United Nations University, 20 November.

  • A Malaysian secondary-school workshop goes under the chatbot. Albukhary International University reported on 26 November that 11 university student facilitators ran an introductory AI workshop the previous day at Sekolah Menengah Kebangsaan Batu Lapan Sik. It used examples of image recognition, pattern finding and trial and error to introduce different types of machine learning. There's room for a follow-up that asks students to predict one example a model will mishandle and say why. A student who can explain the limits of the examples a model learned from has something to bring to the next discussion about whether to trust an AI answer. Albukhary International University, 26 November.

Sceptic's corner

The appealing sales claim is that an AI study assistant makes students independent because it can plan a session, spot a gap and cue the next step. That describes a plausible way to get a student through a session. Independence is a claim about what the student can do when the assistant stops cueing. Liu and Yang's direct comparison favours adding instructional support to AI use in higher education. Their K–12 evidence comes from comparisons with no-AI conditions. A school buying a planning or reflection feature would still have to find out whether younger students can keep the habit when the on-screen structure disappears. Liu and Yang, discussion and limitations.

Consider two students who get through the same revision set. One takes the assistant's judgements and does the next exercise it serves. The other predicts which item will be hard, asks for a single hint and keeps a record of why one answer changed. Both dashboards log a completed session. Give each student a new problem and five minutes to make a plan, then ask what would cause them to change it. The teacher can see who picked up a strategy that travels. It is a small test, and it can fit inside a lesson. If a product claims to teach independence, ask where it gives the learner a chance to practise doing without it.

The Week That Was

A week of small, conditional evidence. Wharton's Primer team watched simulated characters help students too early (arXiv, 12 November). Harvard's study of about 3.3 million students found that lockable phone pouches bought a small learning gain (Harvard CEPR, 17 November). Carnegie Mellon's tutor helped adults fix their own errors, with nearly twice the practice time bundled in (arXiv, 15 November). OpenAI's new models, released on 17 November, came with the admission that they were more likely to leave out obstacles that blocked their work (OpenAI). On Thursday the EU Council put curriculum and assessment on the same AI agenda (Council of the EU, 19 November).

The same Thursday, unreported in the weekday briefs, South Korea sat its university entrance exam under its tightest AI controls yet; it frames the centrepiece. Since Friday's brief: on Saturday 21 November, Fortune reported that three of America's four largest districts are pushing back on Google's Search terms. It leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

On Thursday South Korea ran what may be the most closely guarded exam in the world. For the College Scholastic Ability Test, 497,526 candidates sat at 1,312 sites with about 83,400 invigilators. Glasses were inspected, Wi-Fi within 100 metres was switched off, and the government sent three national emergency alerts during the day (Kyunghyang Shinmun, 19 November). The preliminary count of 98 covered the first four sessions. The ministry’s updated count after the fifth session was 111 electronic-device cases: 54 detections linked to the alerts, 52 other detections and five cases of device use (Ministry of Education, 20 November).

The week's most notorious case was not caught in an exam room at all. On 15 November, the top-ranked candidate in this year's tax accountant exam admitted to cheating with electronic devices and generative AI (News1 via Financial News, 17 November). Speculation about his near-perfect results came first; the confession followed (Al Jazeera, 17 November).

So the Sunday question: if guarding the room has limits, what should a school change instead?

Read the Korean numbers coldly. Most of what was caught was a device that went off or turned up. Detection counts the careless; whatever the careful managed is, by definition, missing from the count. England's chief regulator has reached the same place. In an 18 November letter, Sir Ian Bauckham said Ofqual would soon propose changes to coursework in A level history and the three English A levels, and told exam boards he expects exam-centre staff, invigilators included, to be trained to spot wearable misuse in time for the summer 2027 series. "But we cannot rely on invigilators alone," he wrote (Ofqual, 18 November).

Research has words for this. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu separate "discursive" changes, which tell students what is allowed, from "structural" ones, which change the task itself so it works whether or not students follow the instructions (Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 2025). Traffic-light rules for AI use are their example of the first kind. They borrow the look of a system with real enforcement and leave the enforcement behind. Korea chose the opposite extreme: maximum structure, applied to a single day.

One AP course already shows a middle path. In AP Computer Science Principles, students build a program of their own, and the supervised May exam asks written questions about that code (College Board). The project can be made anywhere; the explanation happens where nobody can prompt it.

Korea is edging the same way. Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin told the National Assembly the case was a chance to rethink the purpose and format of exams, and essay-type questions are under discussion, undecided (News1 via Financial News, 17 November).

For an international school, an AI-use scale is a discursive instrument, and a useful one: it tells students what each task allows. It cannot make that true. Its structural partner can be cheap. For any task set at the low-AI end of the scale, name the moment, in class and in front of a teacher, where the student explains, defends or extends what they handed in. The College Board does it once a year. A department can do it every unit.

Behind You! Behind You!

Three of America's four largest districts are pushing back on Google. New York City has switched off Gemini and AI Mode on student devices and is talking to Google; Los Angeles has asked for a way to turn the AI summaries off; Chicago says it will look at other search tools if it cannot suppress them (Fortune, 21 November). New Jersey's governor's office called the policy unfair to schools and students (NJ.com via Yahoo, 18 November). Miami-Dade, which has offered Gemini to about 100,000 high schoolers, did not answer questions on how it will adapt. Pushback so far, not departure.

Gemini skills can now be shared, and sharing starts switched on. In a gradual rollout from 12 November, on the web app only for now, Gemini users can share skills with View or Edit access and attach files from Drive, Gemini Notebook and Google Photos (Google, 16 November). Sharing is on by default and admins can turn it off. Skills follow the domain's Drive sharing settings, so external sharing applies to them too, and there is no admin control over attaching files. A well-built rubric skill is worth passing round; check the external setting first.

Gemini can now label sensitive Drive files, for schools on the right licence. On 17 November Google made Gemini-powered classification in Drive generally available. Admins describe a label in plain language, test it, then let Gemini apply it across Drive so labels can drive data-loss and retention rules; it will not overwrite labels people have set (Google, 17 November). In education it needs Google AI Pro for Education. For a school that must show where student records sit, losing the manual training step matters. A rule written in a sentence still needs testing on files whose contents you already know.

Sceptic's Corner

The centrepiece's remedy, change the task rather than guard the room, has a strong case against it. Korea is making it in public.

Redesign does not remove advantage; it moves it. When the National Education Commission's essay-type plans surfaced in August, one parent told Yonhap the family would simply add another academy, and a cram-school official expected demand for test technique to grow in the early years (Yonhap via Daum, 16 August). Grading fairness was the largest single concern in the commission's public comments: 754 of 3,246 (News1 via Financial News, 17 November). The ministry's plan for marking essays at scale is an AI that provisionally scores answers, trained on 1.1 million of them, in every regional education office by 2029 (Money Today, 5 August). The machine leaves the exam hall and returns as the marker.

Conversation-based checks have costs of their own. They reward the fluent and the confident, and a timed in-class write is harder on slow writers and anxious students.

Nor has the motive changed much. Stanford's Challenge Success surveys put the share of high schoolers reporting at least one cheating behaviour in the previous month at 60–70% for years, and the 2023 surveys, which added questions on tools like ChatGPT, found it about the same or slightly lower (Stanford GSE, 31 October 2023). The tool changes; the stakes drive it.

The line for the staffroom: redesigning the task is right, but it is neither free nor neutral. Before calling the new version fairer, check whom it favours.
