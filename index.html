Friday, 13 November 2026

Top story

The awkward minutes of a lesson deserve a closer look.

On 9 November, Southern Cross University announced two Lismore seminars for 11 November, one for families and one for teachers, to share evidence and a practical toolkit on classroom screen use. The Macquarie University-led project collected data between August and November 2025 and released its survey and classroom observations on 5 October 2026. This week's development is the work of putting those findings in the hands of teachers and parents. The research behind it spans 1,057 surveyed students, 914 current teachers and 1,686 parents or carers, alongside 57 hours of observation across 68 classrooms.

The survey estimates about 68 minutes of learning-related screen use at school in Years 5–6 and 131 minutes in Years 7–8, using the midpoints of the time ranges students selected for their most recent school day. The observers saw disengagement cluster around conditions such as waiting, changes of activity and unclear tasks. Most observed off-task periods were brief. Schools nominated lessons in which digital tools would be used, so the observations describe those lessons. The survey helps locate a step change in reported exposure. The observations offer clues about where adults can make a difference inside a lesson. Estimating the effect of a daily screen limit on attainment would require a different design.

For international schools, the primary-to-secondary handover is a useful place to look. Imagine a class opening shared documents to compare evidence. A student who gets in immediately can start sorting examples. The student waiting for access has an idle device and no next move. The teacher can have both students mark up a paper extract or discuss a sample while the documents load, then bring the screens back for the comparison. That keeps the reason for using the device clear and gives teachers something observable to change when a lesson regularly loses attention at the same point.

Other stories

  • A Sheets canvas gets a second audience. Google's 11 November Workspace notice lets a canvas editor publish a standalone, read-only link to an interactive calendar, board or dashboard without granting access to the full spreadsheet. The link has its own audience setting. A publisher can choose a static snapshot or automatic republication as the source changes, then change the audience or stop publishing from the web interface. Rapid Release started on 11 November and Scheduled Release starts on 2 December, with up to 15 days for visibility in either case. The listed education entitlement is the Google AI Pro for Education add-on. Google applies the existing Drive setting for publishing files to the web. The school-sized use is a filterable activity calendar that can go in whichever learning platform families already visit, with the planning file still limited to its editors. That division works best when the calendar draws from a separate spreadsheet containing only data intended for its audience.

  • Richmond's incident notice reaches beyond current enrolment. In a 12 November notice, Richmond Public Schools in Virginia said it detected system disruptions on 21 October and brought in outside specialists. The district said personal information may have been exposed and attributed claims of access or acquisition involving current and former students, staff and contractors to the people behind the incident. Its list of categories in affected systems includes contact and identity details and limited medical information. Exposure of particular records remains uncertain. The notice offers free Experian monitoring with separate enrolment instructions for minors and adults. A school can keep a family reachable for everyday announcements through its current portal and still have no workable route to a graduate from five years ago. A records map needs to show how long those files are retained and who is responsible for reaching people whose school accounts have expired. Otherwise the people holding the records and the people managing the response may be working from different lists.

  • A geometry prototype makes the dependencies visible. In a preprint submitted on 9 November, the authors report a small randomised, think-aloud comparison with 12 US secondary students. Ender links steps in a two-column proof to the diagram and shows how claims depend on earlier ones. The comparison group used a digital version of the conventional two-column display with a static diagram. Students averaged 80.5% accuracy with Ender and 58.2% with the comparison display, with the clearest advantage on questions about spotting and fixing mistakes in the proofs. The numbers describe performance with the display in one session, with six students in each group. A delayed proof written independently would answer a different question. One lesson idea survives even if the prototype never leaves the lab. Present a plausible but faulty proof, mark the first place a claim depends on something unproved, and have students defend that choice. The diagram can carry some of the work of finding the step while students still have to supply the mathematical reason.

  • An LMS reading tool puts the layout in the learner's hands. In its 11 November Ally 2.21 release notes, Blackboard says Enhanced Reader is available automatically in existing Blackboard, Canvas, Brightspace and Moodle integrations. Students can select the new alternative format for a course file, open it in a browser, adjust typeface, size and spacing, use a reading ruler, and take the reading offline with their settings intact. The same release upgrades the AI Alt Text Assistant and adds administrator switches for PDF fixes for missing titles, missing language metadata and scanned documents. Reading preferences travel with the downloaded version. The original file still travels through other routes, including email and shared folders. A representative assignment can show whether a student can find and use the alternative format from the course page. Opening the original through the other routes shows whether its headings, tables and diagrams are still accessible. A usable handout needs to survive the way students actually receive it.

  • A French expert panel gives AI literacy a place in primary school. The Élysée received its report on 10 November. The commission's recommendations 12 and 13 call for a dedicated programme from primary school with minimum teaching hours and competencies, and for classroom models suited to age and development. The panel favours tools that guide by questioning and proposes that a scientific education council evaluate classroom labelling. These are proposals from experts. Their pairing clarifies an issue that trips up school conversations about AI literacy. A Year 5 class can compare a machine-produced claim with a reliable source, discuss who takes responsibility for a mistake, and practise explaining the check without signing pupils up for a chatbot. A separate decision applies when children use a model themselves. At that point, a teacher needs to see the tool work on a realistic task, including how it responds when a child asks for the answer and keeps asking.

On the radar

  • Apple puts sign-language interpretation on the player menu. Apple announced on 10 November that supported Apple TV titles would get the option starting on 13 November with Being Heumann, its film about disability-rights activist Judy Heumann. Apple also names American and British Sign Language for F1. Its Apple-device requirements list version 27.2 or later across the supported operating systems. The interpretation was designed and tested with Deaf and native signers. The development worth watching is whether services make it easy for a teacher or student to discover the right language and version before choosing a film. Availability on a given title and device matters at the point of playback.

  • Seoul's standards programme put the curriculum itself on the table. 1EdTech Korea's 11–12 November programme listed a demonstration of Korea's national curriculum and job competency standards as machine-readable data using CASE, alongside a bilingual dictionary spanning 12 standards and a comparison with US and EU skills frameworks. The second day was scheduled as a hands-on session on standards and certification. The useful follow-through is what happens when one system imports a curriculum standard, another attaches it to an assignment, and a later revision has to be traced across both. A shared identifier is the beginning of that test. It matters whether the tools preserve it through a real export and reimport.

  • A group-chat simulator will need a test outside the simulation. In a 10 November preprint, Lilo puts children into a staged peer dispute and uses a mentor character to prompt them to pause and consider their responses. Researchers recruited 28 children aged 9–11, with 26 included in the survey analysis. Reported confidence about coping or intervening rose after the session. The study used one session and had no control group or follow-up measure of real group-chat behaviour. The development to watch is whether a controlled comparison can show transfer, including for students who learn to spot when a situation calls for an adult rather than another reply.

Sceptic's corner

The tempting claim about Google's standalone canvas link is that keeping a source spreadsheet restricted makes its read-only dashboard a safe object to circulate. The release gives the canvas its own audience and optional automatic republication. Google's help page adds a consequential detail. The underlying data goes straight to the viewer's browser and may be accessible there even when the spreadsheet itself is restricted.

Take an activities schedule. At launch it shows the day, room and supervising adult, all intended for the audience. Later, a coordinator adds a pupil roster to the working file. If that working table feeds the canvas, the calendar's visible columns are a poor guide to what its audience could receive. A later filter or detail view may surface a field that was previously out of sight. The people with the published link can still be the same people who bookmarked it when it was simply an activities schedule.

A static version freezes the data at publication and can go stale. A live schedule can draw from a separate spreadsheet with only publishable fields or with aggregate totals where those are sufficient. That keeps the sensitive data out of the published canvas's input. A viewer account can then check how the calendar behaves after a representative edit. Give the canvas only the data its audience is entitled to receive.

Filed Sunday, 8 November 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 8 November 2026 · 1,434 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

The week belonged to Google, and to the question of who decides. On Tuesday it announced that student Gemini and Search would be off by default, said schools rejecting its AI would lose Search, then reversed within the day; a spokesman confirmed they are "separate controls" (GovTech, 4 November). Search still arrives with its AI features (Bloomberg via Claims Journal, 5 November), which Google says will offer step-by-step guided learning (CNBC, 3 November). On Wednesday Google set out how teachers can attach Guided Learning to Classroom assignments and read each student's chat, rolling out since Tuesday (Google, 4 November).

Since Friday's brief: a quiet weekend. Nothing material surfaced on Saturday or Sunday. The biggest story the dailies missed broke on Thursday, US time; it leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

Two of the week's stories sit on opposite sides of one screen. Guided Learning now gives teachers "individual student chat transcripts" (Google, 4 November). A lawsuit alleges that a 13-year-old discussed suicidal thoughts with Google's AI on a school Chromebook for months before her school alerted her parents, and that Google does not provide chat data on student accounts (Bloomberg via Claims Journal, 5 November). So the Sunday question: should a school read its students' AI conversations, and if it does, what is it reading?

Start with what a transcript is evidence of. Nicholas Soderstrom and Robert Bjork's review of the motor- and verbal-learning research separates performance, what a learner can do during practice, from learning, the change that lasts. The first is often an unreliable index of the second, and learners often mistake one for the other (Soderstrom and Bjork, 2015).

AI supplies a vivid demonstration. In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 Turkish high-school maths students, those given an unguarded GPT-4 tutor did 48% better than the control group in practice, then 17% worse on the exam once it was taken away. A hint-giving version lifted practice by 127% and left exam scores about level (Wharton, August 2024; since published in PNAS, 2025). A smooth transcript records performance with help on hand. It shows where a student got stuck. It cannot show what they will know next month.

Then consider what being read does. Help-seeking is social, and part of AI's appeal is that no one is watching. When researchers surveyed 6,960 students at four Australian universities in 2024, 49% were using AI for feedback, and one wrote that "[AI] allows me to ask stupid questions that I'm too ashamed to ask my teacher" (The Conversation, 27 August 2025). Put the teacher inside the chat and some of that safety leaves.

School monitoring shows where reading drifts. In the Center for Democracy and Technology's research, approximately half of students said they were not comfortable expressing their true thoughts and feelings online when they knew they were monitored. Among teachers at schools using monitoring software, 78% reported students flagged for disciplinary action, against 54% reporting referrals to a counsellor or other adult (CDT, August 2022). Software bought for care slides towards discipline.

The lawsuit shows the opposite failure: a private channel with no adult anywhere near it. So the answer is not to look away. It is to look narrowly, and to say so out loud.

For a school, that means three rules before any course switches on Guided Learning insights. Students hear, at the start of the task and in plain words, who can read the chat. Transcripts are read to find misconceptions, never marked or used for discipline. Safeguarding runs through the trained route the school already has, not a class teacher's dashboard. An AI use scale tells students what they may use. It does not yet tell them who is reading.

Behind You! Behind You!

A family is suing Google over a school Chromebook. Parents of a 13-year-old North Carolina seventh-grader have sued in California state court, alleging she spent months discussing suicidal thoughts with Gemini and Search's AI Mode, and that the warning came from her school, not Google (Bloomberg via Claims Journal, 5 November). Google told the New York Times it is reviewing the claims (SheKnows via Yahoo, 6 November). These are untested allegations. For any Google school, the question is which of its own alerts would fire on a chat.

Deepfakes are outrunning the rulebook. One in three of 852 US educators surveyed by the EdWeek Research Center this summer don't know whether their school has a policy on AI-generated fake images and videos; nine in 10 haven't been trained to detect or respond to deepfakes in the past two years (EdWeek via GovTech, 5 November). The same week Google paused a Vids feature that built an avatar from an uploaded image, a day after announcing it. It had no admin control, listed Education Plus, and said nothing about consent from the person pictured (Google, 2 November, updated 3 November).

Gemini now reads Forms responses. Gemini in Google Forms will analyse responses, find patterns across questions and suggest next steps, writing and running Python code so the counts come from the actual data (Google, 5 November). It comes with Education Plus and the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, reaching Scheduled Release domains from 19 November. Course evaluations and wellbeing check-ins are now one click from a model; the setting the post names is smart features and personalisation.

Sceptic's Corner

Google launched Guided Learning in Classroom alongside a stack of evidence: a blog promising "1.5x higher learning gains in just a single study session" (Google, 3 November), and a teacher guide calling the tool "proven to improve learning outcomes" (Google, Classroom guide). Follow the numbers home.

The 1.5x compares gains of 8.1 and 5.3 percentage points after half a day of studying statistics at the University of Tokyo. Google's November AI in Education report gives the same trial as a +0.14 standard deviation improvement (Google, November 2026). One framing sounds like half as much again; the other is a modest nudge from one sitting.

"Proven" rests on a pre-registered trial with 1,763 students in 12 schools in Port Loko, Sierra Leone, which DeepMind put at "roughly 1.2 to 1.7 years of typical learning progress" (Google DeepMind, 9 June). The November report rounds that to between one and two years; the guide quotes 1.7, a conversion calibrated to progress in low- and middle-income countries. What was tested was a package of teacher training, paired shared devices, structured lesson plans and technical support, in a design not built to separate the tool from the rest (EdTech Innovation Hub, June 2026). Students who started stronger gained most.

The same week, the Hechinger Report described an October study by Maryland's Jing Liu, who randomly assigned an always-available AI helper to 15 of 30 professors' courses. Students in those courses earned grades 0.11 points lower on a four-point scale, and only 15% used it (Hechinger Report, 2 November). Google's own report concedes that optional "tutor-on-the-side" AI in its ASU Study Hall and Coursera pilots failed to yield meaningful gains, largely through low adoption (Google, November 2026).

The honest reading: teacher-directed AI inside a designed lesson can help. A tab students may open is a different product, and the brochure sells the second on the strength of the first.

The line for the staffroom: "proven" means it worked somewhere, for someone, as part of something. Ask which part you are buying.
