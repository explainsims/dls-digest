Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Wednesday, 4 November 2026

Top story

Google is making student AI an explicit school decision, with Search inside the boundary.

Its 3 November announcement says access to Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Google Search will be entirely off for K–12 student accounts until administrators enable the products. This is a change announced for the future: Google says specifics will follow in the coming months, ahead of rollout. Separately, teacher-assigned Guided Learning began launching globally in Classroom yesterday; Guided Learning in Search starts with selected US schools later this month (Google, 3 November).

The uncomfortable detail is the relationship between Search and AI. AP reports that schools rejecting AI altogether would also lose Google Search, because the service integrates it. The company is responding to objections over its earlier student chatbot rollout, but the new choice still packages functions schools may want to judge separately (AP, 3 November). An explicit switch is useful. Its usefulness depends on what sits behind it.

For international schools, this changes the questions around a familiar research habit. A student entering a query, receiving a source list and deciding what to read is doing different work from a student receiving a conversational explanation. Either can be educationally useful. Permission for one should not quietly become a judgment about the other. A school also needs to distinguish an account's permitted services from the teaching choices made inside a lesson.

The immediate reading is therefore three separate dates and decisions: what has launched, what is being piloted, and what administrators will eventually have to configure. Those clocks are easy to collapse in a headline. Teachers deserve a clear account of what students will actually encounter before a lesson depends on it. The switch is arriving; the educational purpose still needs a sentence of its own.

Other stories

  • Google's learning-gain claim is worth reading in percentage points. Its new account of a randomised University of Tokyo trial describes more than 300 students spending half a day studying statistics. Both groups had standard online resources; half also had Guided Learning. Reported pre-to-post gains were 5.3 percentage points without it and 8.1 with it: a 2.8-point difference, presented by Google as 1.5 times the learning gain. The same announcement describes a separate four-week pilot across three UK secondary schools and four subjects, with feedback from surveys and interviews informing teacher insights and curriculum anchoring (Google, 3 November). These are two different kinds of evidence. The university experiment is a short-session performance comparison; the school pilot provides experience and reported perceptions. Neither establishes lasting learning across a school year. The useful teaching question is what the learner did between being stuck and being helped. Asking for a quiz, testing an explanation and revising an answer could all support that work. Merely adding more exchanges may not. A promising result earns a closer look at the task and the assessment. It does not make the product name an instructional method.

  • A new automated review separates helping a human from making the best team. AutoAnalysis, a preprint submitted on 30 October, synthesises 1,955 effect sizes from 420 papers in its dated snapshot. The authors report that people working with generative AI performed better on average than people alone. In the smaller subset also measuring AI alone, the combination performed worse than the better solo performer. The manuscript stresses substantial variation between studies, small author-led human audits of its automated pipeline, and the absence of conclusions about longer-term learning or skill retention (Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone, 30 October). This is a preprint about task performance across domains, not a finding that pupils learn best when removed from the task. That distinction matters most in education, where producing the answer and becoming able to produce it are different objectives. Its useful contribution is a sharper comparison for demonstrations. Does the arrangement improve the person's work? Does the human contribution improve the result? What happens when the assistance is removed? Those questions can have different answers. A large literature count makes the distinction worth investigating; it cannot decide which capability a particular lesson is meant to develop.

  • Oxford is putting subscribed scholarship inside ChatGPT Edu and studying what readers then verify. Oxford University Press's 3 November announcement describes an October–December pilot with Harvard Library, Wharton and the University of Oxford. A Model Context Protocol server developed with Silverchair retrieves relevant content that participating institutions have purchased or subscribed to, with citations and links to Oxford Academic. The pilot will examine uptake, output quality and whether trusted-publisher citations change users' verification behaviour (Oxford University Press, 3 November). That final question is the interesting one. A respectable citation may encourage a student to open the source, or make the student feel opening it is unnecessary. The interface alone cannot tell us which. For an international-school library, the wider pattern is worth tracking: the subscription increasingly includes a route into an assistant, rather than requiring every inquiry to begin at the database doorway. That could improve discovery. It could also make the original article a place readers rarely visit. Research teaching will need to preserve the step where a learner checks whether a source supports the particular claim being made. The publisher supplies the material; the reasoning connecting it to an answer still needs inspection.

  • Meet is getting a place to explain recording policy before somebody joins. Google's 3 November release introduces a short administrator-set message in the pre-call Green Room, optionally linking to a fuller policy. It can appear always or when the call is being recorded, transcribed or having Gemini notes taken. The feature is off by default, configurable by organisational unit, and includes Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus. An extended rollout began on 2 November and may take longer than 15 days (Google Workspace Updates, 3 November). For schools, the valuable improvement is timing: an explanation can reach a participant before they enter a conversation. A link buried in a handbook cannot perform that same job at that moment. But a notice does not settle whether recording is appropriate, who receives the transcript, or how long it remains available. Those are decisions the wording should explain, not camouflage. A useful message would make the destination and purpose of the resulting material understandable to a parent or visiting colleague. The administrative control is small. Getting the explanation into ordinary language is where much of the real work sits.

On the radar

  • Google's November teacher training moves towards assessment design. New US modules are scheduled for 4 November, covering interactive assessments, game-based learning about data and model performance, and strengthening assignments against shortcuts. The wider Educator Series is due to expand to Brazil, Mexico, Spain, Australia and sub-Saharan Africa in the coming months (Google, 3 November). These are announced dates, not confirmation that every module is available this morning. The useful test for vendor training is whether a teacher leaves better able to judge an assignment's intellectual demand. A quicker way to generate the assignment is a separate benefit, and a badge measures neither on its own.

  • Scam education is becoming a regional simulation rather than another warning poster. Google says its Be Scam Ready game is expanding to Indonesia, Japan, Malaysia, the Philippines and Vietnam, alongside $6 million in Google.org funding for The Asia Foundation and the Tech for Good Institute's wider resilience work (Google, 3 November). The funding is an input, not evidence of fewer victims. For digital citizenship teaching, the interesting possibility is practising the moment of hesitation: which cue makes a learner stop, verify through another channel or refuse a request? Recognising a scam in a clearly labelled game is only the beginning of that transfer.

  • A small part of Google's teacher funding goes to standards infrastructure. Its new package exceeds $9.5 million across five organisations, including 1EdTech Global's work on public infrastructure for trusted learning standards. Other recipients include CUNY's Computing Integrated Teacher Education initiative, Fab AI, the Krause Center for Innovation and Teaching Lab (Google.org, 3 November). Watch what becomes reusable beyond a single product demonstration. For schools buying connected tools, the mundane questions about compatibility, evidence and continuity can outlast a striking launch. A grant announcement identifies the direction of investment; usable standards, implemented by suppliers, would be the more consequential result to look for.

Sceptic's corner

The easy claim is that putting a teacher in control makes an AI activity educationally sound. Teacher control is necessary in many situations. It is also an incomplete description of what has been controlled.

A teacher might choose the source documents, set the topic and receive a dashboard showing participation. Yet the important decision could occur inside a single exchange: whether the system gives a hint, supplies the missing inference or quietly performs the entire difficult step. A well-bounded topic can still contain an unbounded amount of intellectual assistance. The lesson remains recognisably about the right material while the learner's job changes underneath it.

Equally, removing every form of help is no badge of pedagogical courage. Students sometimes need an explanation, a worked example or a prompt that makes the next attempt possible. The question is which help leaves them more capable, and how the teacher can tell. That requires inspecting the work learners subsequently do, including an opportunity to explain or apply the idea without the same support.

The useful control, then, reaches beyond the product switch. It includes a clear learning purpose, a deliberate stopping point for assistance and evidence of what the student can now attempt. Those are demanding things to fit into a dashboard. They are also why the dashboard should inform the teacher's judgment rather than become its substitute. Turning something on is an administrative event. Knowing what it is doing to a learner remains a teaching responsibility.

The Week That Was

The week kept returning to one gap: naming a problem is not the same as stopping it. New York's Education Department named 38 products, then said it was working with companies to find out whether the offending features could be disabled at all (Chalkbeat, 23 October). The UK's AI Security Institute described an agent that often said an attack would be harmful in the very situations where it went ahead (AISI, 23 October). Gemini's immersive explorations reached the student hub in Education editions with the line "There is no admin control for this feature" (Google, 28 October).

Since Friday's brief: whole-deck editing with Gemini in Slides, which schools get through the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, carries the same line (Google, 30 October). For schools, that makes the add-on licence the only switch. OpenAI said ChatGPT will restrict practical weapons information for teens even without signs of violent intent, end chats that seem fixated on violence, and may pause accounts after repeated ones, telling parents when a teen's is paused. Rollout starts within weeks (Reuters via AOL, 30 October).

What Does It All Mean?

Every story above assumes a lever somewhere. So the question worth a Sunday: when a school cannot switch a feature off, what is its AI rule actually made of?

Start with the lever schools think they have. Among American teens who use AI for schoolwork, Common Sense Media found in August that 44% had had an AI tool blocked on a school network or device, and 59% of those simply moved to a personal device, where there is even less support (Common Sense Media, 18 August). A network block is a detour, not a wall.

Assessment researchers have been making this case for a while. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu split institutional responses into discursive changes, which tell students what is allowed, and structural ones, which change how the task itself works. Traffic-light permission schemes, they argue, borrow the look of a real enforcement system without its power, and leaning on rules like these creates what they call an "enforcement illusion" (Corbin, Dawson and Liu, 2025).

The University of Sydney, where Liu works, had already written the idea into policy: from 2025, AI allowed by default except in exams and in-semester tests, then two lanes (ACS Information Age, December 2024). Secured, supervised tasks leave AI to the examiner; everything else is open, with students guided in its use. Its FAQ is blunt that AI use cannot be restricted in assessments not secured face to face, nor reliably detected (Teaching@Sydney, updated April 2026).

Even the AI Assessment Scale's own authors agree. Leon Furze, one of them, writes that assessment security is not what the scale aims at; it is a framework to support assessment design, and a colourful handout plus "a hopeful expectation" secures nothing (Furze, September 2025).

That would be bleak if rules kept students honest. They never did. A follow-up study published in February, surveying 4,354 students at six US high schools about eighteen months after ChatGPT's release, found an overall cheating rate of 72.06%, which its authors call consistent with historical baselines; AI availability does not appear to have changed overall prevalence (Chen et al., February 2026). The tool changed the method, not the rate. Whatever sets the rate, whether engagement, pressure or a task that feels worth doing, sits upstream of any switch.

So the working answer: an AI rule rests on three layers, in falling order of reliability. First, what is structurally true of the task: done in the room, staged, defended aloud. Second, what the school can actually switch, which is coarse and covers products more than features. Third, what it tells students, which is invaluable for conversation and useless as security.

For a school running an AI use scale, the practical move is small. The scale stays as the shared language. But every task pitched at the no-AI or low-AI end needs a one-line answer to "what makes this true in the room?" If the honest answer is that the student promised, the task is open and should be marked as one.

Behind You! Behind You!

New York will treat unverified chatbot users as children. Governor Hochul signed S.9051B/A.10379C on 28 October. In the coming months, companies may not offer under-18s, or any user not confirmed as an adult, features such as posing as human, claiming a relationship, asking unprompted emotional questions or using a child's private details from earlier chats. The attorney general can seek up to $25,000 per violation (New York State, 28 October). The state's Business Council had argued that educational tools were swept in (TechTimes, 29 October). Watch whether vendors build that default once and ship it everywhere.

States want proof and can't get it. SETDA's 2026 State EdTech Trends report, published 29 October from 73 survey responses, found 79% of state leaders naming AI a top technology priority. Yet 52% said their state collects no data on the use or effectiveness of ed-tech products, and 68% said independently verifying vendor claims about AI products is challenging (GovTech, 29 October). If American states with procurement offices struggle, an international school is checking alone.

Half of the US schools and districts surveyed had a cyber incident last year. The Center for Internet Security's 2026 K-12 report puts it at 51% in the past 12 months, and 66% of districts leave cybersecurity to one or two people (CIS, 27 October). Phishing or email compromise ranked among the top incidents for 87% of leaders, and the report warns of more convincing AI-enabled phishing (K-12 Dive, 27 October). The weak point is staff inboxes, not servers.

The ed-tech backlash is on the ballot, and it isn't partisan. Ahead of Tuesday's US midterms, Chalkbeat found a Republican candidate for California governor promising to ban AI in schools on day one. In Texas, the Republican governor pledges less classroom screen time and his Democratic challenger opposes AI for elementary students. Seven of eight San Francisco school board candidates back limits on classroom technology (Chalkbeat, 26 October). International schools enrolling families from those systems should expect the mood to travel with them.

Sceptic's Corner

Next time someone says the research shows AI improves learning, ask which research.

Jill Barshay reports 39 meta-analyses on AI in education, every one finding substantial benefits. Patrick O'Neill, a community college professor, audited 14 of them and found serious conceptual and statistical flaws, from hallucinated references to underlying studies citing generative-AI papers published before the technology was public. One, published in a Nature journal in May 2025, has since been retracted (Hechinger Report, 26 October).

When O'Neill pooled 44 well-designed experiments, outcomes ranged from very helpful to very harmful, and his preliminary calculations put the average at roughly zero. Susanna Loeb's team gathered almost 900 studies and judged only 20 good enough for its evidence summary. O'Neill's audit is itself a preprint and his numbers are preliminary, so hold him to the same standard.

Two things follow. A literature that is uniformly positive is a warning, not a comfort; real interventions produce null results. An average of roughly zero is not a verdict of harmlessness. It is what you get when good designs and bad ones cancel out, which is why the question is always which use, for whom, checked how.

The line for a staff meeting: there is no effect size for "AI", only for particular uses of it. Anyone quoting one number is quoting a brochure.
