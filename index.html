Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Monday, 14 September 2026

Top story

Four private schools have produced four different AI operating models—and that may be the useful result.

Hawaiʻi Public Radio reported on 11 September that most private schools in the state have created some form of team to review classroom AI, but have not converged on one rollout. Punahou gives teachers and high-school students Gemini for Education, combines departmental decisions with administration oversight, and trains teachers internally; teachers often use the system for rubrics and grading. Mid-Pacific leaves classroom use to teachers, gives younger pupils a monitored SchoolAI environment, and moves high-school students to Gemini. Kamehameha Schools is one of 55 schools in OpenAI’s current ChatGPT for Teachers expansion. Hawaii Baptist Academy is moving more slowly, concentrating on teacher training while pupils receive digital-awareness instruction (Hawaiʻi Public Radio, 11 September 2026).

This is reporting, not comparative evaluation: there are no common measures of learning, workload, safety or uptake, and each school describes its own approach. Even so, the comparison has unusual value for international schools because these are independent institutions making local decisions rather than following a statewide mandate. The schools have separated at least four choices that vendor pitches routinely collapse: staff access, student access, age progression and who gets to decide at task level.

The sharpest question for Monday is therefore not which school has the best chatbot. It is whether a school’s governance can describe those four layers without contradiction. A Google-first environment can still reserve some student-facing work for a more constrained tool; a teacher-permission model can still require school-level data rules; and cautious student access need not mean postponing staff learning. The Hawaiʻi sample does not tell us which configuration improves learning. It does show that a coherent local architecture is possible without pretending there is one universal maturity ladder.

Other stories

  • California has turned companion-chatbot safeguards and pupil-data limits into law. On 10 September Governor Gavin Newsom signed a package that includes SB 1119, requiring companion-chatbot operators to establish child-safety protections, crisis protocols, parental controls, independent audits and annual risk assessments; AB 1159 regulates the use of K–12 pupil data in AI systems; and related measures restrict addictive social feeds for under-16s and expand protections against AI-generated child sexual exploitation (Governor of California, 10 September 2026). This is one jurisdiction and several provisions will depend on implementation, but the procurement signal travels. A vendor saying its general chatbot has a teen mode is not the same as a system designed around minor-specific risk assessment, parent-visible controls and an auditable crisis pathway. That distinction belongs in vendor review. International schools can usefully separate learning assistants from companion systems in approved-use vocabularies; conversational form alone hides materially different purposes and risks.

  • A European survey finds the training gap is about supervising pupils as much as operating tools. Epson published an independently fielded but company-commissioned survey on 9 September covering 1,227 teachers, 1,219 students aged 16–18, 611 teacher trainees and 303 school technology decision-makers across the UK, France, Italy, Germany, Spain and Poland. Eighty per cent of teachers worried AI was entering classrooms faster than schools could manage; 82% wanted more training to oversee student use and 78% wanted guidance for their own work. Among students expecting to use AI, 87% reported using some form at least weekly for school (Epson, 9 September 2026). The four groups were separate online panels and the results were unweighted, so the percentages should not be treated as a single representative European electorate. The useful professional-learning distinction is sturdier: feature training and classroom-supervision judgement are different competencies, and a programme that teaches only prompting answers the smaller one.

  • Thirty-plus AI plans point back to workflow design, boundaries and adult learning. Bellwether published lessons on 10 September from two years of work with more than 30 schools, nonprofits and youth-serving organisations. Its strongest plans connected mission, priorities, exclusions and required system changes; its implementation advice moves from broad promises to one concrete workflow, with named people, information, decisions, safeguards and success measures; and its third lesson treats adoption as sustained adult learning in which enthusiasts and sceptics both contribute (Bellwether, 10 September 2026). This is a consultancy’s synthesis of its own programme, not an independent comparison of outcomes, and it does not publish the 30 plans or a scoring rubric. Still, it offers a better grain size for Ed Tech Coaches than another generic literacy session. A useful coaching conversation starts with one current workflow and the human judgement it must preserve, then tests whether AI improves the output—not with a tour of everything a model can generate.

  • A small high-school app-building pilot produced artefacts, but not yet evidence of learning. A Rockefeller Foundation account published on 10 September says four West Virginia schools completed a Thunkable programme in which about 120 pupils in grades 9–12 built an estimated 80 mobile apps. Teachers received professional development; weekly work combined tutorials, app development, entrepreneurship and innovation; and projects included accessibility, wellbeing and small-business tools. About half the students initially reported little or no familiarity with AI concepts, coding or algorithms (Rockefeller Foundation, 10 September 2026). The announcement provides no post-test, comparison group, retention measure or independent review of app quality. That makes 80 apps an output count, not a learning result. For school prototyping, the stronger pattern is the task structure: identify a real user, build something inspectable, explain design decisions and evaluate the finished artefact. AI fluency should be visible in the reasoning and revision, not inferred from the number of generated screens.

  • Identity verification is moving inside the credential network rather than sitting at the login screen. Instructure announced on 9 September that Parchment Transcript Services is piloting Veriff checks before transcript fulfilment. The network connects more than 6,200 higher-education institutions and 6,700 K–12 schools and districts. Where enabled, a government ID and live selfie produce a verification status and confidence score; single-sign-on or institution-token users skip the extra step; questionable orders route to staff rather than being automatically denied. Instructure says Parchment stores status and metadata, not biometric data, with US general availability planned for November (Instructure, 9 September 2026). The release does not disclose Veriff’s image-retention terms, error rates across document types or pilot demographics. Those omissions matter for international schools where identity documents vary widely. The architecture is still worth watching: stronger assurance at the high-risk transaction, with human review and a lower-friction SSO path, is more proportionate than demanding biometric proof at every ordinary LMS visit.

On the radar

  • Toddle’s event is over; its recordings are now the evidence to inspect. The 12 September How Schools AI page says registrants will receive recordings and describes a four-month Munich International School pilot involving 180 pupils in Grades 3 and 4, with classroom alignment, teacher guidance and checks for understanding presented as the lessons (Toddle, event held 12 September 2026). Watch for the actual task designs, data and failure cases—particularly whether understanding was measured independently of the tutor and whether teachers recorded when it failed. A retrospective event page still contains programme claims, not a published evaluation.

  • A second five-year, $20 million award will test adaptive narrative systems in K–12 classrooms. NC State announced on 10 September that the National Science Foundation renewed the EngageAI Institute, whose next phase will develop self-improving interactive narratives, explore world-model storytelling and give teachers and pupils more authorship control (NC State, 10 September 2026). Watch how learning is measured over a school year, what the system is allowed to change from one cohort to the next, and whether continuous adaptation remains interpretable enough for teachers to challenge. Interpretability is part of instructional control.

  • AI4MiddleSchools is moving from local cohorts toward regional training infrastructure. Carnegie Mellon reported on 10 September that the programme will expand into four new states, deepen work in three others, and aims over three years to reach roughly 15,000 pupils, 1,700 educators, 100–145 teacher leaders and 100–150 administrators through cohorts, regional hubs and an online platform (Carnegie Mellon, 10 September 2026). Watch whether the expansion publishes curriculum artefacts, completion, classroom enactment and student-learning measures—not only reach—and whether regional hubs preserve the teacher co-design that shaped the original Georgia curriculum.

Sceptic's corner

The weekend’s most tempting line is that schools are already past the point of choice. In the Hawaiʻi reporting, several schools have adopted different tools; in Epson’s survey, student use is already frequent; Bellwether begins from staff experimentation outrunning strategy. Put those together carelessly and acceleration starts to look like prudence: if pupils are using AI anyway, the school had better institutionalise it quickly.

The evidence supports a narrower conclusion. Existing use makes silence untenable; it does not make every product, cohort or task appropriate. The Hawaiʻi schools are interesting precisely because they did not converge: some separate younger and older pupils, some focus first on teachers, and some leave more discretion at classroom level. Epson measured perceptions in four unweighted panels, not the effect of any school response. Bellwether’s advice is to name what an organisation will not pursue and to test a concrete workflow, not to maximise access.

International schools need enough shared language that teachers and students are not inventing safety rules alone, but shared language is not a mandate for uniform use. The staffroom sentence is simple: ubiquity creates a duty to govern, not a duty to deploy. A mature school can be fast at clarification and deliberately slow at permission—and should be able to explain both without sounding confused.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 13 September 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 13 September 2026 · 1,415 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

If you are catching up, the week had a spine and a mood. The spine was the arrival of PISA 2025 on 8 September, the first international snapshot of how fifteen-year-olds actually use AI for schoolwork (OECD, 8 September 2026). The mood was governance running hot: ministers adopted a joint statement in Paris (UNESCO, 9 September 2026), a union-and-Microsoft school-AI standard acquired contractual teeth, and Instructure floated Canvas Lite as a free, individually owned LMS. The connective tissue: "available" and "governed" kept drifting apart. On the weekend itself, Toddle's free How Schools AI meetup ran on Saturday 12 September, sessions recorded for later release (Toddle) — so the artefacts, not the registration counter, are what to judge. Otherwise it was quiet.

What Does It All Mean?

PISA handed everyone a correlation and a temptation. Across systems, students who used chatbots for schoolwork often scored below those who did not, and moderate users tended to beat frequent users (OECD, 8 September 2026). The OECD was careful to call this cross-sectional self-report, not cause. So which way does the arrow run — is the chatbot degrading learning, or are struggling students simply the ones reaching for it? A population snapshot cannot say. Two bodies of evidence from outside this week's headlines can put real pressure on the question.

The sharper one landed quietly earlier this year. Analysing a ten-year panel of 3.2 million ALEKS maths interactions, Rismanchian and colleagues used a quasi-experimental design that split tasks into those a chatbot can do (text word problems) and those it cannot (interactive graph problems). Study time on the AI-susceptible problems fell after ChatGPT's release — 26.9% cumulatively for college students over eleven quarters, 31.3% for high-schoolers, 9.0% for middle-schoolers, and no detectable change for Grade 5. The tell is what proctoring did: among college students the divergence "vanishes entirely under proctoring," and proctored retention items showed a 25% decline in the odds of a correct answer while unproctored scores rose. The authors call the population-level signature "cognitive surrender" (Rismanchian and colleagues, arXiv, July 2026).

That is the mechanism PISA can only gesture at. Students quietly stop doing the specific cognitive work the tool absorbs, and the deficit stays invisible until the tool is removed. The PNAS mathematics trial this digest has cited before found the same shape from the other direction: unrestricted GPT-4 lifted assisted practice 48%, then its users scored 17% below the no-AI group once it was taken away (Bastani and colleagues, PNAS, 25 June 2025). Performance-with-the-tool and capability-without-it are different quantities, and only the second is learning.

Put together, PISA measured a shadow at population scale; the mechanism literature names the object casting it. The useful school move is therefore not the ban-or-allow argument the correlation keeps being drafted into. It is task design. If the AI-vulnerable step is the step you assess, you are measuring the tool — so build tasks where the graded thinking is the graph, the defence, the revision decision, and keep at least one AI-off checkpoint, because the gap only reappears when the tool is gone.

And the grade gradient complicates this month's prohibitions. New York drew its ban through eighth grade, on the intuition that younger pupils need the most shielding; the ALEKS panel found the study-time collapse concentrated among high-schoolers, with Grade 5 unchanged. If the offloading that erodes durable knowledge runs heaviest among the oldest students, an age line aimed downward governs the cohort least affected and leaves the exam classes — the one place a school is least willing to intervene — untouched.

Behind You! Behind You!

Los Angeles quietly matched New York. On 2 September, the same day New York City announced its moratorium, Los Angeles Unified — the country's second-largest district — restricted all student access to generative AI for 2026-27, rolling back a prior policy that let students aged 13 and over use approved tools after digital-citizenship instruction. Its Generative AI Ad Hoc Committee is to bring recommendations to the board by year's end. New York's ban drew the coverage; the same-day twin in Los Angeles went comparatively unremarked (K-12 Dive, 3 September 2026).

Parents are bringing AI to the IEP table. EdSurge reported on 11 September that parents of children with disabilities are feeding individualised education programs into chatbots to translate jargon, draft questions and even build home goals. "They're using it to translate the jargon, any acronyms. It's helping them to draft questions," said Olivia Coleman of the University of Central Florida. It democratises a famously opaque process — and raises FERPA and disclosure questions no policy has caught up with. One special-education administrators' body offered the opposite warning — that lengthier, AI-drafted parent submissions mean "it will slow down the process." (EdSurge, 11 September 2026).

The detectors flag real student writing — unevenly. A University of Notre Dame paper presented at an AI-leadership summit spanning the turn of the month found AI-writing detectors flagged genuine, recent, human-written peer-reviewed abstracts at 14.9% (Pangram) and 8.9% (GPTZero), against vendor claims of under 1%. Non-STEM writing was flagged far more than STEM (theology 26.6% versus chemistry 2.1% on one detector), and light "polish" edits — a proxy for the guideline-compliant help teachers routinely permit — were flagged at 38 to 80%, with multilingual learners harmed further still. The authors call it an "integrity catch-22" (Karr and colleagues, arXiv, September 2026).

The Riff Returns But Slower

  • 19 September: Google's AI Educator Badge-a-thon. Watch whether the modules teach account boundaries and evidence of learning, or only feature fluency (Google, 3 September 2026).
  • 30 September: Canvas Lite opens worldwide; the old Free-for-Teacher service reopens 30 September–6 October as a migration window. The real question is whether exports carry complete records or the 500 MB ceiling strands larger courses — an exit test wearing a launch's clothes (Instructure Community, 9 September 2026).
  • 15 October: UNESCO's deliberative-governance consultation closes to comment; policy briefs are promised for Digital Learning Week 2027 (UNESCO, 8 September 2026). The briefs, not the ministerial statement, are the artefacts to read.
  • End of 2026-27: Los Angeles Unified's committee reports. A year-long ban that produces only a ban will have answered the liability question and none of the learning ones.
  • Slow burn: the cognitive-offloading literature is consolidating from anecdote into large-panel evidence. Expect "does AI help?" to keep splitting into "help with what, measured when, and with the tool present or absent?"

Sceptic's Corner

The thing being evangelised this week was not a product. It was governance itself. Inside seven days a ministerial statement was adopted in Paris, a union-and-Microsoft "national standard" acquired contractual teeth, two of the largest school systems on earth imposed moratoria, and Google shipped context-aware access controls. Read together, the week can feel like the AI-in-schools problem is finally being brought under management. It is worth being precise about what was actually delivered.

Almost all of it governs procurement and access — not learning. A ministerial statement names public-interest tests without budgets, deadlines or signatory-by-signatory duties. The AFT–Microsoft standard binds a contract, but only for products "primarily designed and marketed" for education, expressly excluding the general-purpose assistants through which most school AI now arrives (AFT School AI Privacy Standard, September 2026). Context-aware access decides who may open a tool from which device, not which task deserves it or what evidence of understanding must survive (Google Workspace Updates, 8 September 2026). And the moratoria remove a tool from some pupils — which is not the same as building a capability. The strongest evidence we have, PISA's correlation and the ALEKS panel's proctoring result among them, does not license a ban as a learning intervention; it licenses one as a risk decision, which is a different and more modest claim (OECD, 8 September 2026; Rismanchian and colleagues, July 2026).

None of this is a reason to wave the week away. A contract you can enforce and an age line drawn on precaution both have their place. It is a reason to keep the categories straight. The question to put to anyone brandishing this week's headline as the answer is unglamorous: what does it change on Monday morning, in one classroom, for one task? A standard that governs a data flow, a statement that governs a ministry, and a ban that governs a login have each done something real. None of them has designed the assignment where a student still has to think. That remained the job this week, and it did not get any smaller.
