Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Thursday, 12 November 2026

Top story

England wants edtech suppliers to show their safety work.

The Department for Education opened a consultation on 10 November on independent safety certification for education technology and AI used in English schools and other education settings. The department wants defined standards, a proportionate way for suppliers to show they meet them, and less duplicated work for education settings making purchasing decisions. The consultation closes on 4 February 2027. The government announced it alongside stronger phone-free school guidance, in the same statement that asks schools to keep phones inaccessible throughout the school day.

The design question is what, exactly, gets certified. A school can license a product for teachers, leave student accounts off and keep outside sharing disabled. Another school can buy the same licence, connect it to its learning platform and let students upload work. The supplier's name on the invoice is identical. The information the tool can see, the people it can contact and the decisions it can influence are different. A useful certificate would state which ages, features, settings and data flows the testing covered, and what change would trigger a new review. Otherwise a vendor can change a feature in February while a school is still relying on the evidence it saw in October.

Ed Tech Coaches can test the proposed scope by following an ordinary assignment from the learning platform into a separately licensed tool. A teacher should be able to see whether a tool can keep student uploads, whether those uploads are visible to others, and who is expected to intervene when a student reports a problem. Evidence that answers those questions once could spare dozens of schools the same round of vendor emails. A school would still need to decide whether its chosen configuration matches what was tested and whether the product serves its curriculum.

Other stories

  • Brazil spells out decisions that stay with educators. On 10 November, the Ministry of Education announced its approval of the National Education Council's opinion covering public and private institutions across educational levels. The framework sets four risk levels, with classification taking account of the intended use, data involved, autonomy and potential impact on students. It would rule out automated emotion inference, AI grading of essays and other authored work that requires interpretive judgement, and sanctions based solely on a detector. It would also restrict direct, autonomous or unsupervised student access through Grade 5, with planned, educator-mediated use allowed. The government says the prohibitions apply upon publication of the resolution, with 12 months from that point to adapt policies, governance and contracts. The useful distinction for a school writing its own policy is between what a tool can do and what staff may delegate to it. A system that drafts a practice exercise and a system that grades a student's argument both generate text. Only one of those actions determines what the student's work is worth.

  • England's higher-education regulator asks what an assessment proves. The Office for Students and Advance HE published findings on 11 November based on conversations with more than 300 students and staff from over 120 institutions, primarily in England. Participants described confusion about permitted AI use, and the report warns that high-quality submissions from unsupervised tasks may tell assessors little about the student's own knowledge. The regulator says it will require valid, reliable assessment, with secure, AI-free assessment needed in many cases. It also expects courses to develop relevant skills, and the report allows for carefully designed assessments that incorporate AI. In secondary school the distinction can fit inside one project. An observed source analysis can show whether a student can weigh evidence independently. A later task can ask the same student to critique an AI-generated analysis and account for every correction. The two scores then tell a teacher two different things. The report is a qualitative account from higher education, so its value here is in the assessment design rather than a prediction of how often school pupils use AI.

  • Google's new frontier model comes with an introductory price and an expiry date. Google released Gemini 4 Carbon on 10 November, with paid API access through Google AI Studio and Gemini for business. For the model intended for public access, Google lists US$2 per million input tokens and US$10 per million output tokens through 30 November, then US$4 and US$20 respectively. It also announced a consumer rollout to Google AI Ultra subscribers in the Gemini app and AI Mode in Search. That last route is a consumer entitlement, and the release gives no student Workspace entitlement. The billing change is the immediate story for anyone prototyping a school utility. Before a team switches the model behind a tool, run it on a small set of fictional school documents with known answers. Compare the cost per usable result with the current model, including retries and the time a teacher spends correcting the output. A cheap-looking first run in November is a poor basis for a December service budget.

  • The chemistry lab acquired a second voice, and anxiety rose. In an 11 November paper in Frontiers in Education, Anshan Normal University researchers compared two Grade 9 classes at one secondary school in eastern China over 16 weeks. Forty students used an emotion-feedback system with cameras and voice sensors alongside inquiry-based chemistry instruction. Thirty-eight received the comparable instruction with conventional teacher interaction. The researchers report that self-rated lab anxiety increased in the AI-supported class and stayed stable in the comparison class. One student described uncertainty over whether to follow system prompts or the teacher's directions. The two-class quasi-experiment and reliance on self-report limit what the result can tell us about other schools or tools. It does give a teacher a concrete question to ask about any classroom system that claims to read how a pupil feels. When a student, the teacher and the system give different accounts of what's happening, who decides what happens next? That disagreement matters in a chemistry lab, where a pupil may need to listen for a timely safety instruction while also managing an unfamiliar procedure.

  • ASEAN's education figures put access and completion on different pages. The UNESCO Institute for Statistics summarised its regional report on 10 November. It puts the primary out-of-school rate at roughly 14% in 2000 and 5% in 2023, with lower secondary falling from about 24% to 11% over the same period. Yet nearly one in three adolescents of upper secondary age was out of school in 2023, and median upper secondary completion in 2024 was about 55%. The institute also identifies inclusive digital transformation as part of widening access to lifelong learning. The figures describe different populations and measures, and the report points to gaps linked to geography, wealth and disability. A school working with a regional partner on digital learning can apply the same discipline at a smaller scale. Count who can access a resource, who manages to finish an activity and who can use what they learned. The third measure could be a short offline task. A platform's records may give the project a detailed picture of the people it reaches while leaving out the people with the weakest connection.

On the radar

  • 1EdTech puts repeatability on the agenda. Its undated listing for the 12 November webinar names Learning Data Insights founder John Whitmer and 1EdTech strategy lead Michael Feldstein. The organisers describe a review of more than 250 studies on scoring, question generation and feedback, with fairness rarely examined and repeated-run consistency examined still less often. That's the organisers' account of the review. The question worth listening for is what counts as a meaningful inconsistency. Two phrasings of a revision comment can help equally. Two scores for the same essay under the same rubric can leave a student with a different outcome because the teacher clicked twice.

  • Singapore's AI-for-education meeting opens next week. The National Institute of Education at NTU hosts the conference on 16–18 November. The organisers released the schedule on 29 October. The Monday programme schedules a workshop led by UCL's Kaśka Porayska-Pomsta on examining ethics through the stages of AI development, and one from the University of Georgia's Xiaoming Zhai on agent-supported science learning. They offer two ways of asking where a lesson's decisions sit. In science, for example, an agent could help a pupil articulate a hypothesis, suggest one or choose a test for it. Each would leave the pupil with a different decision to make. The session materials may make those choices visible enough for a science department to discuss.

Sceptic's corner

An authorship detector can get a high score on one task and leave a teacher guessing about the task that brought them to it. In a 9 November preprint, Nils-Jonathan Schaller and colleagues drew on a study alongside PISA 2025 in which 2,429 15-year-olds in German secondary schools wrote English argumentative essays, with or without access to a chatbot. The study logged keystrokes, clipboard actions and chat. That gave the authors an unusually good record of which words had come from where. On the comparison between human and AI text, the stronger of two research detectors reached a whole-text macro-F1 of 0.96. With mixed text and text written after chatbot use without traceable AI wording added to the AI class, the same detector scored 0.80. These are scores from two defined test sets, with one age group, two tasks and one chatbot in the underlying student study.

Picture the pupil who asks for a counterargument, dismisses the answer, then writes an essay independently. A detector sees the essay. A teacher can ask the pupil which counterargument they considered and why they rejected it. The two procedures recover different evidence. This is where the policy and the task brief do work that a classifier cannot. If the assignment permits brainstorming, a pupil should know how to record that kind of help. If the learning goal is to generate and evaluate counterarguments independently, an observed example gives the teacher evidence about that specific ability.

Filed Sunday, 8 November 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 8 November 2026 · 1,434 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

The week belonged to Google, and to the question of who decides. On Tuesday it announced that student Gemini and Search would be off by default, said schools rejecting its AI would lose Search, then reversed within the day; a spokesman confirmed they are "separate controls" (GovTech, 4 November). Search still arrives with its AI features (Bloomberg via Claims Journal, 5 November), which Google says will offer step-by-step guided learning (CNBC, 3 November). On Wednesday Google set out how teachers can attach Guided Learning to Classroom assignments and read each student's chat, rolling out since Tuesday (Google, 4 November).

Since Friday's brief: a quiet weekend. Nothing material surfaced on Saturday or Sunday. The biggest story the dailies missed broke on Thursday, US time; it leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

Two of the week's stories sit on opposite sides of one screen. Guided Learning now gives teachers "individual student chat transcripts" (Google, 4 November). A lawsuit alleges that a 13-year-old discussed suicidal thoughts with Google's AI on a school Chromebook for months before her school alerted her parents, and that Google does not provide chat data on student accounts (Bloomberg via Claims Journal, 5 November). So the Sunday question: should a school read its students' AI conversations, and if it does, what is it reading?

Start with what a transcript is evidence of. Nicholas Soderstrom and Robert Bjork's review of the motor- and verbal-learning research separates performance, what a learner can do during practice, from learning, the change that lasts. The first is often an unreliable index of the second, and learners often mistake one for the other (Soderstrom and Bjork, 2015).

AI supplies a vivid demonstration. In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 Turkish high-school maths students, those given an unguarded GPT-4 tutor did 48% better than the control group in practice, then 17% worse on the exam once it was taken away. A hint-giving version lifted practice by 127% and left exam scores about level (Wharton, August 2024; since published in PNAS, 2025). A smooth transcript records performance with help on hand. It shows where a student got stuck. It cannot show what they will know next month.

Then consider what being read does. Help-seeking is social, and part of AI's appeal is that no one is watching. When researchers surveyed 6,960 students at four Australian universities in 2024, 49% were using AI for feedback, and one wrote that "[AI] allows me to ask stupid questions that I'm too ashamed to ask my teacher" (The Conversation, 27 August 2025). Put the teacher inside the chat and some of that safety leaves.

School monitoring shows where reading drifts. In the Center for Democracy and Technology's research, approximately half of students said they were not comfortable expressing their true thoughts and feelings online when they knew they were monitored. Among teachers at schools using monitoring software, 78% reported students flagged for disciplinary action, against 54% reporting referrals to a counsellor or other adult (CDT, August 2022). Software bought for care slides towards discipline.

The lawsuit shows the opposite failure: a private channel with no adult anywhere near it. So the answer is not to look away. It is to look narrowly, and to say so out loud.

For a school, that means three rules before any course switches on Guided Learning insights. Students hear, at the start of the task and in plain words, who can read the chat. Transcripts are read to find misconceptions, never marked or used for discipline. Safeguarding runs through the trained route the school already has, not a class teacher's dashboard. An AI use scale tells students what they may use. It does not yet tell them who is reading.

Behind You! Behind You!

A family is suing Google over a school Chromebook. Parents of a 13-year-old North Carolina seventh-grader have sued in California state court, alleging she spent months discussing suicidal thoughts with Gemini and Search's AI Mode, and that the warning came from her school, not Google (Bloomberg via Claims Journal, 5 November). Google told the New York Times it is reviewing the claims (SheKnows via Yahoo, 6 November). These are untested allegations. For any Google school, the question is which of its own alerts would fire on a chat.

Deepfakes are outrunning the rulebook. One in three of 852 US educators surveyed by the EdWeek Research Center this summer don't know whether their school has a policy on AI-generated fake images and videos; nine in 10 haven't been trained to detect or respond to deepfakes in the past two years (EdWeek via GovTech, 5 November). The same week Google paused a Vids feature that built an avatar from an uploaded image, a day after announcing it. It had no admin control, listed Education Plus, and said nothing about consent from the person pictured (Google, 2 November, updated 3 November).

Gemini now reads Forms responses. Gemini in Google Forms will analyse responses, find patterns across questions and suggest next steps, writing and running Python code so the counts come from the actual data (Google, 5 November). It comes with Education Plus and the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, reaching Scheduled Release domains from 19 November. Course evaluations and wellbeing check-ins are now one click from a model; the setting the post names is smart features and personalisation.

Sceptic's Corner

Google launched Guided Learning in Classroom alongside a stack of evidence: a blog promising "1.5x higher learning gains in just a single study session" (Google, 3 November), and a teacher guide calling the tool "proven to improve learning outcomes" (Google, Classroom guide). Follow the numbers home.

The 1.5x compares gains of 8.1 and 5.3 percentage points after half a day of studying statistics at the University of Tokyo. Google's November AI in Education report gives the same trial as a +0.14 standard deviation improvement (Google, November 2026). One framing sounds like half as much again; the other is a modest nudge from one sitting.

"Proven" rests on a pre-registered trial with 1,763 students in 12 schools in Port Loko, Sierra Leone, which DeepMind put at "roughly 1.2 to 1.7 years of typical learning progress" (Google DeepMind, 9 June). The November report rounds that to between one and two years; the guide quotes 1.7, a conversion calibrated to progress in low- and middle-income countries. What was tested was a package of teacher training, paired shared devices, structured lesson plans and technical support, in a design not built to separate the tool from the rest (EdTech Innovation Hub, June 2026). Students who started stronger gained most.

The same week, the Hechinger Report described an October study by Maryland's Jing Liu, who randomly assigned an always-available AI helper to 15 of 30 professors' courses. Students in those courses earned grades 0.11 points lower on a four-point scale, and only 15% used it (Hechinger Report, 2 November). Google's own report concedes that optional "tutor-on-the-side" AI in its ASU Study Hall and Coursera pilots failed to yield meaningful gains, largely through low adoption (Google, November 2026).

The honest reading: teacher-directed AI inside a designed lesson can help. A tab students may open is a different product, and the brochure sells the second on the strength of the first.

The line for the staffroom: "proven" means it worked somewhere, for someone, as part of something. Ask which part you are buying.
