Wednesday, 25 November 2026

Top story

Hanoi requires teachers to demonstrate readiness before teaching core AI lessons.

The city's education department issued a directive on 23 November requiring Level 2 preparation for every teacher assigned to deliver the 12 core periods of AI education in a secondary class this school year, including teachers scheduled for the second semester. Hanoi's earlier city training, held from 5 to 15 November, had 2,071 teachers nominated from 664 institutions. Of those teachers, 664 taught subjects other than computing, chiefly mathematics, physics, literature, English and chemistry. The department counted 54 secondary schools that had sent no computing teacher and told those schools to review their arrangements. Vietnam Government Electronic Newspaper, 23 November.

Teachers who missed the city training can qualify through at least 24 periods of school-based preparation across subject-team sessions, guided independent study and joint lesson review. To establish equivalence, they need an approved 12-period teaching plan for a grade and a demonstration or practice lesson rated satisfactory by their subject team. Schools must plan an activity that can run without devices and check AI tools before use. The school-based training guidance bars real student faces, voices and personal information from training outputs, learning materials and demonstration lessons. It suggests photographs of objects, drawings, hand gestures or coded cards as substitutes. Lower-secondary pupils should not have to register personal accounts. Schools are due to file their plans and teacher lists by 30 November, ahead of teaching. Vietnam Government Electronic Newspaper, 23 November.

The demonstration is the part Ed Tech Coaches can borrow. Give a physics teacher a set of drawings for a classifier and ask the subject team to watch what happens when it sees a drawing they held back. Can students explain why the model missed it, and can the teacher keep the lesson going if the site is down? Those two questions get closer to readiness than a record of who attended the training.

Other stories

  • Google makes managed access to Workspace generally available for AI agents. On 24 November Google announced general availability for its Workspace Model Context Protocol servers and its official command-line tool. The servers give connected agents a managed way to act across services such as Gmail, Drive, Calendar and Docs. Google says administrators will be able to govern agent access, require user confirmation for sensitive actions, and review logs of agent activity and confirmations. It says existing user-level data-loss prevention rules apply. The tools and controls are rolling out over two weeks, with technical enforcement of the new Workspace access rules for AI agents starting on 1 February 2027. For a Workspace school, the immediate task is to locate agents that can already read, change or share school data. A useful test uses a synthetic file and a test account. Ask an approved agent to prepare a draft and then to share the file outside the domain. Inspect what happens at each step, including what the audit log records. Google Workspace Updates, 24 November.

  • A US bill puts the learning claim and the data contract on the same page. Representative Becca Balint introduced the Education Technology Standards Act on 24 November. The proposal directs the National Institute of Standards and Technology and the Department of Education to develop guidance and model contracts for K–12 technology. Topics in the text include user-data collection and deletion, evidence of educational benefit, and a way for teachers to report factual errors and for suppliers to fix them. It would require reasonable care to protect identifiable student data and authorise $3 million in fiscal year 2027 for university-led evaluations. The What Works Clearinghouse would review the studies and publish effectiveness ratings based on those that meet its standards. The error clause is worth borrowing now. A teacher should have a route to report a wrong answer in a product, get an acknowledgement and learn what changed. If a faulty explanation stays live for the other classes until the next release, the school has learned something about the supplier's support and about who carries the review work. Balint announcement, 24 November · Bill text.

  • TikTok redesigns Family Pairing and adds a guide for educators. In its Singapore newsroom on 23 November, TikTok described a dashboard that puts screen time up front and lets linked parents see changes in follower and following counts, a sample of material from verified creators their teen follows, and the week's public posts. The redesign is also available through a browser. TikTok listed visibility of whom teens message as forthcoming. It also launched an educator guide with more classroom resources promised. That makes two things to distinguish in a family briefing. There are the switches a parent can change and the information the platform chooses to display. A sample from verified accounts is one small view of a feed. A counselor can use it to open a conversation about what keeps showing up, how a recommendation got there and what the student does when the answer is unwelcome. For teachers, the guide can explain how particular features work. Claims about their effects still need evidence from outside the platform. TikTok Singapore newsroom, 23 November.

  • Gemini Notebook puts pictures and a stopwatch on flashcards. Google posted the Workspace release on 24 November, listing images drawn from uploaded sources, an optional stopwatch and review reminders in the mobile app. Google lists the features as available to all Workspace customers and gives administrators no separate switch for this update. Images are the one change here that could alter the kind of question a teacher asks. A circuit diagram can become a prompt to predict which bulb goes out if a switch opens. The same diagram can give the whole thing away if Google imports its labels or a worked solution. Before sharing a deck, check the face students see against the answer you actually want them to reconstruct. A reminder can help schedule another try, and a timer can be useful for facts where fluent recall matters. For a longer explanation, let the clock sit idle and ask for the reason the student chose that answer. Google Workspace Updates, 24 November.

On the radar

  • Iowa opens its draft computer science, data science and AI standards for comment. The state education department opened consultation on 23 November. It is seeking feedback on revised computing standards and new standards for data science and AI, with hybrid forums on 8, 14 and 16 December and a survey closing on 23 December. The review team's recommendations go to the state board. The eventual text is worth comparing with Hanoi's implementation. Iowa's process asks what students should know across three related subjects and leaves curriculum delivery to local schools. The useful question as the drafts change is which skills wind up shared across subjects and which need a lesson of their own. Iowa Department of Education, 23 November.

  • ECIS has a live session on who does the thinking when children use AI. On 25 November at 16:00 London time, John Mikton of the International School of Geneva leads a free one-hour session on helping children develop thinking skills. The programme distinguishes a skill a child once had and practises less from a skill the child never had a chance to develop. The presenter plans to share questions and a framework to try in class. One test of any example from the session is whether it identifies something a student should be able to do again the next day, on a different problem. ECIS event listing, 25 November.

Sceptic's corner

Google gives the stopwatch in Gemini Notebook a job simulating timed exam conditions. It is a plausible job for an exam that already expects facts or procedures to be available quickly. The leap is treating a shorter time through a familiar deck as a sign that a student is ready for an exam with unfamiliar questions. A learner might get quicker at recognising the particular diagrams the deck shows. When the same underlying system is drawn a different way, the cues they memorised can disappear. The 24 November update describes the tools. It supplies no learning-outcome comparison. Google Workspace Updates, 24 November.

Start with a pupil trying to explain why opening a switch stops current in one branch while the other keeps working. First let them recall a familiar circuit. The next day, give them a rearranged drawing, take away the labels and ask them to mark the path and explain their reasoning. Both runs produce usable evidence. The first can tell a teacher whether retrieval has become easier. The second can show whether the learner has a model that survives a new drawing. If there is room on the lesson plan for one measure of progress, keep the new drawing. The stopwatch can stay with the flashcards.

The Week That Was

A week of small, conditional evidence. Wharton's Primer team watched simulated characters help students too early (arXiv, 12 November). Harvard's study of about 3.3 million students found that lockable phone pouches bought a small learning gain (Harvard CEPR, 17 November). Carnegie Mellon's tutor helped adults fix their own errors, with nearly twice the practice time bundled in (arXiv, 15 November). OpenAI's new models, released on 17 November, came with the admission that they were more likely to leave out obstacles that blocked their work (OpenAI). On Thursday the EU Council put curriculum and assessment on the same AI agenda (Council of the EU, 19 November).

The same Thursday, unreported in the weekday briefs, South Korea sat its university entrance exam under its tightest AI controls yet; it frames the centrepiece. Since Friday's brief: on Saturday 21 November, Fortune reported that three of America's four largest districts are pushing back on Google's Search terms. It leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

On Thursday South Korea ran what may be the most closely guarded exam in the world. For the College Scholastic Ability Test, 497,526 candidates sat at 1,312 sites with about 83,400 invigilators. Glasses were inspected, Wi-Fi within 100 metres was switched off, and the government sent three national emergency alerts during the day (Kyunghyang Shinmun, 19 November). The preliminary count of 98 covered the first four sessions. The ministry’s updated count after the fifth session was 111 electronic-device cases: 54 detections linked to the alerts, 52 other detections and five cases of device use (Ministry of Education, 20 November).

The week's most notorious case was not caught in an exam room at all. On 15 November, the top-ranked candidate in this year's tax accountant exam admitted to cheating with electronic devices and generative AI (News1 via Financial News, 17 November). Speculation about his near-perfect results came first; the confession followed (Al Jazeera, 17 November).

So the Sunday question: if guarding the room has limits, what should a school change instead?

Read the Korean numbers coldly. Most of what was caught was a device that went off or turned up. Detection counts the careless; whatever the careful managed is, by definition, missing from the count. England's chief regulator has reached the same place. In an 18 November letter, Sir Ian Bauckham said Ofqual would soon propose changes to coursework in A level history and the three English A levels, and told exam boards he expects exam-centre staff, invigilators included, to be trained to spot wearable misuse in time for the summer 2027 series. "But we cannot rely on invigilators alone," he wrote (Ofqual, 18 November).

Research has words for this. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu separate "discursive" changes, which tell students what is allowed, from "structural" ones, which change the task itself so it works whether or not students follow the instructions (Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 2025). Traffic-light rules for AI use are their example of the first kind. They borrow the look of a system with real enforcement and leave the enforcement behind. Korea chose the opposite extreme: maximum structure, applied to a single day.

One AP course already shows a middle path. In AP Computer Science Principles, students build a program of their own, and the supervised May exam asks written questions about that code (College Board). The project can be made anywhere; the explanation happens where nobody can prompt it.

Korea is edging the same way. Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin told the National Assembly the case was a chance to rethink the purpose and format of exams, and essay-type questions are under discussion, undecided (News1 via Financial News, 17 November).

For an international school, an AI-use scale is a discursive instrument, and a useful one: it tells students what each task allows. It cannot make that true. Its structural partner can be cheap. For any task set at the low-AI end of the scale, name the moment, in class and in front of a teacher, where the student explains, defends or extends what they handed in. The College Board does it once a year. A department can do it every unit.

Behind You! Behind You!

Three of America's four largest districts are pushing back on Google. New York City has switched off Gemini and AI Mode on student devices and is talking to Google; Los Angeles has asked for a way to turn the AI summaries off; Chicago says it will look at other search tools if it cannot suppress them (Fortune, 21 November). New Jersey's governor's office called the policy unfair to schools and students (NJ.com via Yahoo, 18 November). Miami-Dade, which has offered Gemini to about 100,000 high schoolers, did not answer questions on how it will adapt. Pushback so far, not departure.

Gemini skills can now be shared, and sharing starts switched on. In a gradual rollout from 12 November, on the web app only for now, Gemini users can share skills with View or Edit access and attach files from Drive, Gemini Notebook and Google Photos (Google, 16 November). Sharing is on by default and admins can turn it off. Skills follow the domain's Drive sharing settings, so external sharing applies to them too, and there is no admin control over attaching files. A well-built rubric skill is worth passing round; check the external setting first.

Gemini can now label sensitive Drive files, for schools on the right licence. On 17 November Google made Gemini-powered classification in Drive generally available. Admins describe a label in plain language, test it, then let Gemini apply it across Drive so labels can drive data-loss and retention rules; it will not overwrite labels people have set (Google, 17 November). In education it needs Google AI Pro for Education. For a school that must show where student records sit, losing the manual training step matters. A rule written in a sentence still needs testing on files whose contents you already know.

Sceptic's Corner

The centrepiece's remedy, change the task rather than guard the room, has a strong case against it. Korea is making it in public.

Redesign does not remove advantage; it moves it. When the National Education Commission's essay-type plans surfaced in August, one parent told Yonhap the family would simply add another academy, and a cram-school official expected demand for test technique to grow in the early years (Yonhap via Daum, 16 August). Grading fairness was the largest single concern in the commission's public comments: 754 of 3,246 (News1 via Financial News, 17 November). The ministry's plan for marking essays at scale is an AI that provisionally scores answers, trained on 1.1 million of them, in every regional education office by 2029 (Money Today, 5 August). The machine leaves the exam hall and returns as the marker.

Conversation-based checks have costs of their own. They reward the fluent and the confident, and a timed in-class write is harder on slow writers and anxious students.

Nor has the motive changed much. Stanford's Challenge Success surveys put the share of high schoolers reporting at least one cheating behaviour in the previous month at 60–70% for years, and the 2023 surveys, which added questions on tools like ChatGPT, found it about the same or slightly lower (Stanford GSE, 31 October 2023). The tool changes; the stakes drive it.

The line for the staffroom: redesigning the task is right, but it is neither free nor neutral. Before calling the new version fairer, check whom it favours.
