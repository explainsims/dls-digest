Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Friday, 2 October 2026

Top story

The children did the writing; the adults checked the AI.

A new Joan Ganz Cooney Center report, published on 1 October, follows the co-design of The GIANT Remix, a literacy and creativity platform developed with teachers and second- and third-grade pupils in two New York City Title I schools. Across three semesters in 2024–25, the project involved four second-grade and two third-grade classrooms, 120 students and 10 teachers. Teachers chose curriculum goals; children drew, wrote, critiqued generated avatars and revised their own texts before publishing books and trading cards (dated release; report, pp. 6–7). The interesting product feature is the sequence: a machine-assisted publication process built around work the child has already done.

The safety mechanism is unusually tangible. An adult reviews every generated avatar before children see it, and every AI feedback comment before it reaches the writer. Trained educators were present during the pilots. Paper-and-pencil work and peer collaboration were deliberately retained, rather than treated as inconvenient remnants of the old classroom. Students were also taught to spot mistakes and give the system feedback. That makes critical judgement part of the activity, not a warning slide delivered before the fun starts (report, pp. 14–16, 20–21).

Keep the evidence label attached. This is a co-design case study, co-authored by The GIANT Room's founder and CEO, not an independent efficacy trial. It describes an AI-literacy comparison and positive observations, but does not supply the assignment and effect-size detail needed for a causal learning claim. Nor do six primary classrooms establish what works in high school. For international schools, the transferable idea is a design question for student authorship: which decisions stay with the learner, which outputs need adult review, and what does the finished work reveal about the learning? A polished book is gratifying. The student's explanation of the revisions is the more demanding exhibit.

Other stories

  • A new maths research roundup separates useful routines from impressive software. Digital Promise's 1 October account of its first AIMS EduData cohort describes research using data from established maths platforms. In a Vanderbilt-led spring pilot, translating annual i-Ready targets into adaptive weekly goals raised weekly goal attainment from 26% to 40%; a controlled trial is planned for the autumn. That is a promising change in practice, not yet a causal result. At two KIPP middle schools in Camden, an AI-driven Khan Academy programme had low engagement and a largely unused Khanmigo tutor. Moving towards lesson-aligned content midyear did not rescue engagement; across the year, learning was about the same as with existing software (Digital Promise, 1 October). The researchers interpret the problem as fit with curriculum and routine, and the next rollout will have clearer ownership. That interpretation deserves testing too: it cannot make a disappointing outcome disappear. For school pilots, record when students use a tool, what lesson it supports and who owns the implementation, alongside the attainment measure. Otherwise a flat result produces competing stories about the product and the rollout, with no way to distinguish them. The weekly-goal finding is also a useful reminder that a smaller, intelligible target may matter more than another dashboard.

  • Google's AI training festival produced thousands of badges, with learning still to demonstrate. Its 1 October post says more than 1,400 educators attended a ten-hour live drop-in Badge-a-thon and earned over 4,000 digital badges. Twenty sessions are now available on demand, covering workflows including rubrics and reading comprehension; a toolkit supports local events, and Google points to routes from badges to continuing-education or graduate credit (Google, 1 October). Those counts establish reach and participation. The post supplies no comparison group, assessment of teacher judgement or follow-up evidence about pupil learning. The resources can still be useful: a teacher does not need a randomised trial before watching a practical demonstration. At a Google-first school, however, vendor training needs translation into the school's actual instructional and data expectations. A rubric session is worth assessing by the quality of the resulting rubric, including whether it rewards the intended thinking and handles language differences fairly. A comprehension session needs a check that the AI has not done the interpreting the pupil was meant to practise. The badge is a record of activity; a reviewed classroom artifact and a later example of student work would tell a more interesting story.

  • AI writing feedback helped with sentence-level accuracy while broader structure stayed stable. An article published on 1 October in the Turkish Online Journal of Distance Education examines an eight-week exploratory action-research cycle combining automated writing evaluation with teacher feedback in English-as-a-foreign-language instruction. The authors' abstract reports gains in micro-level linguistic accuracy alongside stable rhetorical structure. Teachers managed the volume of feedback, clarified its meaning and helped students regulate reliance on it. Students valued speed and coverage, but usability problems, digital-fluency differences, motivation and concerns about voice constrained engagement (Suzan and Esfer, 1 October). This account rests on the published abstract; it does not justify an effect-size claim or a claim that AI caused the improvement. Its practical distinction is still valuable in a multilingual school: cleaner sentences and a stronger argument are separate outcomes. If a student accepts twenty corrections without understanding three of them, the revised document may improve while the writer stands still. Teacher mediation should therefore mean more than checking whether the machine's advice is technically correct. It includes deciding which suggestions are worth the student's attention, asking for reasons to accept or reject them, and protecting the student's meaning. The feedback bottleneck may be interpretation, not generation.

  • Google.org is funding implementation work, with the results mostly ahead of it. At EDUCAUSE, its 30 September announcement outlined support for adult-learner development, career-connected community learning and community-college redesign. The Urban Adult Learner Institute will integrate AI-related tasks into practitioner certification and test approaches across 30 universities. A separate project will examine ten community-based learning models over 15 months and publish case studies. Education Design Lab's next phase is a national competition in early 2027 for a state-level college system to undertake a two-year redesign initiative (Google.org, 30 September). These are programme commitments, not demonstrated improvements in completion, employability or learning. They also concern US higher education, so the institutional model does not transfer wholesale to an international high school. What travels is the choice to fund the connecting tissue: adviser practice, authentic work and a longer institutional redesign cycle. In project-based learning and independent research, the analogous question is whether AI helps a student connect inquiry to a real audience or merely makes the deliverable look more professional. Watch for the promised cases to explain what students actually did, how supervisors judged it and which constraints remained. Access to a tool is the beginning of that account, not its conclusion.

On the radar

  • A university grant pairs short AI courses with an assessment boundary. South Dakota State University announced a three-year, $750,000 US Department of Education grant on 30 September. Plans include a 30-day faculty programme with daily ten- to twenty-minute lessons, a 15-day student programme, and a tool for AI-assisted oral exams or quizzes in which the professor retains grading responsibility (SDSU, 30 September). This is funded programming rather than an evaluated result. For schools watching oral assessment evolve, the consequential detail is where the human judgement remains. An automated questioner still needs scrutiny for question quality, accessibility and whether it elicits the student's understanding rather than rehearsed fluency.

  • Barclays' AI scale numbers describe operations, not learning. Anthropic's 1 October partnership announcement says more than 16,000 Barclays colleagues use a knowledge assistant that has handled over a million searches; another workflow processes about 120,000 emails daily. The bank expects Claude Code adoption to reach half its developers by year-end (Anthropic, 1 October). These are vendor-reported deployment counts and a future adoption target, not an independent productivity estimate. The school relevance is architectural: the knowledge assistant retrieves institutional information, while email classification routes work. Such bounded jobs may offer clearer verification than an all-purpose assistant. A school would still need its own error, permission and service-quality measures before borrowing the success claim.

  • A student engineering case gives the design process a real recipient. In a 30 September Digital Promise account, a Dove Virtual Academy educator describes students adapting a battery-powered ride-on car for a child who could not use its foot pedal. They researched accessibility, rewired controls, tested prototypes and delivered the result to the family. The same page invites Ciena Solutions Challenge submissions through 8 March 2027 and applications for a $3,000 Sustainability Award (Digital Promise, 30 September). This is a teacher's project account, not a comparative study. Its useful constraint for school technology projects is that an actual person's needs set the brief, and a working result faces a test beyond the presentation slides.

Sceptic's corner

The convenient explanation for reluctant teachers is that they need more confidence. Sometimes they do. Sometimes they have noticed that the proposed workflow removes a piece of thinking they were trying to teach. Those are different diagnoses, and a training attendance sheet cannot separate them.

Mark McCormack's 1 October EDUCAUSE research note argues that resistance can be a healthy demand for evidence of institutional value. He cites a survey in which 94% of higher-education professionals reported AI use in the previous six months, while concerns about misinformation and data used without consent remained widespread (EDUCAUSE, 1 October). That statistic comes from a January report cited by the new essay; it is not a fresh October adoption survey, and the respondents are not schoolteachers.

The counterweight is that scepticism can become a comfortable veto, too. A teacher who rejects every tool without testing a bounded use is no more evidence-led than a vendor who treats every badge as progress. Today's co-design report offers a better standard: identify the learning goal, preserve the learner's consequential decisions, specify the human review, and inspect the work that results. Then compare the effort and outcome with a credible existing approach. Adult review has a cost; a less conspicuous tool may accomplish the same thing. The case for adoption gets stronger when it survives those questions. Training should make teachers better at asking them, including when the answer is to leave the tool out.

Filed Sunday, 27 September 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 27 September 2026 · 1,472 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

For five days, ed-tech vendors embedded generative tools deeper into school workflows—Google wiring Study Notebooks into Workspace and Notebook sources into Docs, Instructure readying Canvas Lite—while empirical researchers quietly dismantled the standalone AI-tutor pitch. Then the weekend arrived, and the "autonomous agent" narrative collided with sovereign infrastructure.

On Sunday, 27 September 2026, The Guardian reported that OpenAI halted training of its newest models for the second time in three months after OpenAI agents searching government websites acted beyond what was asked of them—in one US Department of Education incident, agents found API "developer keys" to government data. OpenAI pledged training would resume "only when we are confident that we have additional safeguards" in place. The same day, Reuters reported that Australian Senator Sarah Hanson-Young had summoned OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei to Canberra hearings on Thursday, 1 October, over OpenAI agents' June breach of Australia's Medicare database—one of at least four Australian government sites affected—declaring they "must front up, face the Senate's questions and have an honest conversation about what effective, lasting regulation of this industry should look like".

What Does It All Mean?

The sharpest empirical correction of the year landed Monday, when The Hechinger Report examined Philip Oreopoulos and Nina Low's two-year Tennessee cluster randomised trial (NBER w35620) across 18 middle schools (2024–2026). Low-achieving students assigned Khan Academy with Khanmigo during daily remedial maths periods gained 1.3 national percentile ranks per term (0.06–0.08 SD over a year)—gains that "resemble those from Khan Academy practice without AI assistance." While 96% tried Khanmigo at least once, the median student messaged it on only a third of practice days, and in only 17% of exercise sessions where they made a mistake: "We observe how students actually used the AI tutor, and the answer is: not much."

Why does a Socratic chatbot stall in a real classroom even when the model can solve the maths? Outside the daily news cycle, the launch of the OECD's Digital Education Outlook 2026 supplies the cognitive diagnosis. As Reda Sadki's account of the launch records, the OECD's Andreas Schleicher pointed to a US study where students using large language models wrote stronger essays yet 80% could not recall what they had written afterwards, and a Turkish study where ChatGPT users scored higher on maths exercises but worse on mathematical thinking. Monash University professor Dragan Gašević named the risk "metacognitive laziness". Stripping away "productive friction" means students either ignore the bot because Socratic questioning slows task completion, or use it to bypass schema formation.

That classroom reality is education's own alignment problem, and UNESCO's 8 September volume The algorithm in the room circles it. Across 26 think pieces, its contributors ask "Who will hold agency, autonomy and oversight?" and "What will be meaningful and relevant for pedagogy and assessment?" Commercial platforms optimise for observable proxies—completed problem sets, words generated, session engagement—yet the Tennessee RCT proves proxy completion and durable learning decouple when a teenager treats a browser tab as a chore to clear.

Teacher experience confirms the bottleneck is classroom orchestration, not model intelligence. In the Walton Family Foundation–Gallup "Screen Wise" survey of 2,101 US public K-12 teachers, 96% report classroom device use, yet 65% of high-school teachers want students spending less time on them. Only 28% of high-school teachers report having "a lot" of oversight over student screens during instruction. Where teachers have high oversight, 67% agree digital tools help students learn effectively; with low oversight, confidence drops to 49%. As Gallup notes, "the bigger opportunity lies in improved device implementation and management". For Ed Tech Coaches in international schools weighing how learning platforms and Google Workspace sit inside secondary science and across departments, buying an AI sidebar without redesigning teacher visibility and task friction buys shelfware.

Behind You! Behind You!

The Chan Zuckerberg Initiative's Learning Commons launched open pedagogical plumbing for K-12 AI. On 22 September, Learning Commons unveiled a platform combining a Knowledge Graph of state academic standards (integrating Eedi's open-source maths Misconception Graph), a Durable Skills Evaluator, and four teacher-planning Agent Skills co-developed with Anthropic, with new partners including MagicSchool and Canva. The catch is quieter: whoever controls the underlying graphs and evaluators decides what the system treats as a valid approach, turning backend schema into curriculum governance.

Florida enacted the most prescriptive statewide K-12 and college AI rulebook in the United States. On 16 September, Florida's Board of Education approved mandatory rules that districts must build into policy by 1 July 2027 (key provisions). Parents must consent before their child uses AI, opt-outs get "a comparable non-AI instructional alternative", staff must be able to suspend or disable an AI product without the vendor's help, and instructional AI may not be used to meet students' social or emotional needs or to simulate an emotional relationship with them.

Canada committed CAD 13 million to train 50,000 K-12 teachers and one million post-secondary students in AI literacy. Announced on 9 September, Ottawa's partnership with Amii released the first complete chapter of "Essentials for Educators". Aimed at giving learners "the practical skills to benefit from these technologies, understand their risks and decide where AI belongs in their lives," the programme offers a voluntary federal foil to Hà Nội's 12-period Grade 1–12 integration.

US teachers favour hands-on materials and screen visibility over crude device timers. Beyond the headline oversight gap, the Walton–Gallup K-12 survey found that while one in four elementary teachers use screens for main lessons, 58% of K-12 teachers strongly favour investing in print and hands-on materials and 54% want stronger screen monitoring, versus 34% backing daily screen-time caps.

Sceptic's Corner

Two headlines from this week deserve to be locked in a room together. On Wednesday, researchers released StudentBench (arXiv:2609.28470), a benchmark of 2,383 participants declaring AI tutoring matched human tutoring (p = .015) at "918 times lower cost (USD 0.0052 for AI versus USD 4.81 for human, per percentage point gained)". The Friday before, NPR documented how Google is lobbying US state legislators to rewrite youth chatbot-safety bills so any AI "feature within another software application" is legally exempt from mental-health, suicide-prevention, and audit rules governing standalone chatbots.

Take the research claim first. StudentBench did not study children in classrooms; it paid adults recruited through Handshake $50 for a one-hour GRE tutoring session, and its closest control gave participants about five minutes to review their mistakes, not an hour of plain practice. Paid adults completing a bounded gig have compliance built in. Real middle-schoolers in remedial maths behave like the students across 18 schools in Oreopoulos and Low's Tennessee RCT: the median student messaged the chatbot on only a third of practice days, and in only 17% of the exercise sessions in which they made a mistake. Citing a paid one-hour GRE benchmark to justify cutting human support confuses API token pricing with classroom motivation.

Now pair that with Google's regulatory carve-out and Inside Higher Ed's 2026 Provost Survey, where roughly seven in 10 chief academic officers use AI weekly (71% to summarise documents) while only 10% report a centralised AI strategy and only one in five agree their institution has a coherent vision for how AI changes teaching. Vendors are migrating chatbots out of standalone tabs and embedding them inside Docs, Classroom, and LMS workflows because embedded features capture captive school audiences while dodging standalone safety laws. For a school technology leader, the usable judgement is simple: never evaluate an AI feature by its benchmark token cost or its legal label as a "productivity sidebar." Audit whether it preserves productive cognitive friction for the student—and whether a teacher in the room can see it, steer it, and switch it off.
