Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Tuesday, 4 August 2026

Top story

Mexico's largest university threw out its first-ever online entrance exam and is ordering roughly 58,000 applicants back into a hall to sit it again in person.

The National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM) moved its undergraduate admissions exam online this year for the first time — some 150,000 candidates across three weekends in May and June — a change it framed as widening access for applicants outside Mexico City. The results broke the pattern so sharply they became their own evidence: 16.3% of candidates scored 100 or more points, against a 3.5% average between 2021 and 2025 (Times Higher Education, 3 August 2026). UNAM annulled about 3,000 exams — roughly 2% — for suspected cheating, filed a formal complaint, suspended new undergraduate enrolment on 25 July, and has now ordered a fresh, invigilated in-person exam for everyone already told they were admitted, plus rejected applicants whose scores matched or beat the lowest passing mark of the past five years. The semester starts 10 August; as of 3 August no date or venue for the re-test had been set.

Rector Leonardo Lomelí Vanegas pitched the stakes above the institution: "This doesn't just affect the university; it affects society as a whole. We cannot normalise these kinds of situations." He apologised to the students being recalled through no fault of their own — "I apologise to the applicants who, having been accepted fairly, will be called to take the entrance exam, but it is necessary to ensure fairness and equity in access."

The reason this matters to international schools isn't that anything here runs near that scale; it's that this is the largest concrete instance yet of a tension the field has mostly argued about in the abstract. The convenience of assessing through a screen and the integrity of the result are now in open conflict, and when the stakes were high enough, a serious institution chose integrity and ate the cost — an in-person re-run for 58,000 people, mid-admissions-cycle. "Return to in-person exams" stopped being a think-piece and became a logistics operation. It doesn't say every screen-based task is compromised — low-stakes formative work sits on a different risk calculus entirely — but it says any high-stakes result certified through a remote channel now carries a demonstrated failure mode whose fallback is expensive and disruptive. The usable lesson isn't "ban online" or "trust the detectors"; it's to decide, before the stakes climb, which assessments can survive being run at a distance and which have to be watched — because retrofitting that decision after 150,000 people have already sat looks exactly like this.

Other stories

  • Big Tech's AI bill hit the balance sheet, and for the first time investors flinched. In nine days of earnings — Alphabet on 22 July, Microsoft and Meta on 29 July, Amazon and Apple on 30 July — the four heaviest spenders guided 2026 capital expenditure toward roughly $725–760 billion, up from about $413 billion in 2025: Microsoft near $175B, Alphabet $195–205B, Meta $130–145B, and Amazon carrying a trailing-twelve-month $173B (digitalapplied, 31 July 2026 · Tom's Hardware, July 2026). The split in the market's reaction is the actual story: Microsoft and Amazon rose 8–9% because they could point the spend at a metered, external, growing line — Azure up 43%, AWS up 37%, which CEO Andy Jassy called "our fastest growth in 18 quarters" — while Meta fell 9–10%, Apple 4–8%, and Alphabet about 5% after posting its first negative free cash flow (−$5.9B) since its 2004 IPO. The thread to a school runs under every "free for educators" banner: those tiers are marketing spend, not a metered product, and the investors underwriting them have just started pricing the difference between AI outlay that returns cash and AI outlay that only promises to.

  • A Chinese model undercut OpenAI's freshly-cut prices — and shipped drop-in compatibility with OpenAI's own agent tooling. DeepSeek released V4-Flash-0731 on 31 July at $0.14 per million input tokens and $0.28 per million output (explainx.ai, 31 July 2026) — below even the Luna tier OpenAI had discounted to $0.20/$1.20 the day before, and roughly a quarter of Luna's output price. The number that matters more than the price is the format: V4-Flash ships "native support for OpenAI's Responses API format" and "full Codex adaptation," which the write-up frames as letting it be "a lower-cost backend swapped into existing Codex-based agent workflows." No school is routing student data to a Chinese provider, and data governance is exactly why. But the interoperability move is the one to clock: if the cheap models learn to speak the expensive models' tooling, the switching cost that vendor lock-in depends on begins to erode — quietly good news for anyone who built a small classroom tool on one provider and would rather not be married to it. (Keep the caveat the same coverage flags: Chinese-provider pricing "has shifted mid-quarter before.")

  • The largest study yet of a real AI tutor measured how much students use it — not whether it taught them anything. Researchers at Germany's IU International University analysed anonymised usage logs — not surveys — from about 77,000 distance-learning students using Syntea, the university's own AI learning companion, and found 58% used it during the observation window, rising to 64% among Gen Z against 38% of Baby Boomers (research-in-germany.org, 31 July 2026). Lead author Prof. Kristina Schaaff: "Our study is the first to demonstrate, how students actually use an AI learning companion such as Syntea, based on real usage data." Co-author Quintus Stierstorfer: "The findings clearly demonstrate that AI-supported learning has become part of everyday student life at IU." It is the biggest real-behaviour dataset anyone has put on this question and worth reading for the usage patterns alone — when students reach for the tool, which cohorts lean on it hardest. But note precisely what it does and doesn't show: it is a map of engagement, not of learning, and that distinction matters more here than usual — enough that it is the hinge of the Sceptic's corner below.

  • Meta handed out nearly $2 million to put AI glasses on the faces of learners and disabled users. On 27 July Meta named 30 recipients of its AI Glasses Impact Grants — 25 Accelerator awards of $25,000–$50,000 and five $200,000 Catalyst grants, across 18 states, chosen from nearly 500 applicants (Meta, 27 July 2026 · EdTech Innovation Hub, 30 July 2026). The education-facing awardee is Immerse Inc., using the glasses for "real-time, hands-free language tutoring" that Meta says draws on 48 academic studies and serves more than 200,000 learners; others coach construction trainees hands-free and help people with dementia navigate daily routines. Meta's framing is the tell — "When your work requires both hands, full attention, and real-time information...glasses are easier and safer to use" — and the Catalyst apps reach Ray-Ban Meta glasses later this year. It is early and it is Meta's platform, but hands-free, always-available AI is the form factor that would genuinely change what a language or lab classroom feels like — and, like every wearable, it walks the same camera-and-microphone data questions straight into the room.

On the radar

  • The industry's biggest room opens today, with its own founders openly disagreeing about being there. Ai4 2026 runs 4–6 August at The Venetian in Las Vegas — more than 12,000 attendees and nearly 400 exhibitors — and Wednesday's headline session, "The Architects of Intelligence," puts Geoffrey Hinton, Fei-Fei Li and Andrew Ng on one stage despite Hinton and Ng holding openly opposed views on whether AI is an existential risk (TechTimes, 2 August 2026). Agentic AI dominates the agenda. The signal isn't a product; it's that the field's founders can't agree on the risk framing while enterprises book agents at scale — the same gap a school sits in when it's asked to adopt quickly and govern slowly.

  • A second university answered the assessment question the opposite way to UNAM. Stellenbosch, one of South Africa's leading universities, has explicitly rejected a single institution-wide AI rule, pushing the decision down to the individual module and assessment: depending on the intended learning outcomes, lecturers may "prohibit, restrict, allow, encourage or require" AI (EdTech Innovation Hub, 29 July 2026). It treats AI as a teaching-and-learning question rather than a compliance one. Set against UNAM's centralise-and-go-in-person response, the two map the poles the sector is settling between — lock it down from the top, or devolve the judgement to whatever each task is actually trying to evidence. The second is harder to administer and closer to how good assessment already works.

  • Gates put a number, and an AI bet, on the "is a credential worth it" question. The Gates Foundation announced on 29 July a goal of 10 million more US learners aged 18–34 earning "credentials of value" by 2045 — double the current growth rate — naming AI integration into instruction and college advising as central, with roughly $200 billion to spend before the foundation closes for good on 31 December 2045 (Inside Higher Ed, 30 July 2026). "Credentials of value" is the operative phrase, arriving the same season as survey data showing nearly half of adults expect degrees to matter less in the AI era. Worth watching whether "of value" gets defined by labour-market return — and whether the AI-advising money produces guidance students actually act on.

Sceptic's corner

The headline the IU study will travel under is its co-author's line: AI-supported learning has "become part of everyday student life." It is the most quotable sentence in the release, and read quickly it sounds like a verdict on whether these tools work. It isn't.

Look at what the study actually measured: anonymised usage logs from 77,000 students — who opened Syntea, when, and how often. That is a rigorous, large, genuinely novel adoption dataset, and it earns its "largest yet" billing. But usage is not learning. The logs can establish that 58% of students used the companion; they cannot tell you whether those students understood more, retained longer, or — the failure mode the evidence keeps surfacing — quietly offloaded the thinking. The recent evidence base is the counterweight: a Sungkyunkwan study in which the heaviest AI users reported the highest "perceived learning" and posted the lowest test scores, and a Wharton randomised trial in which a teacher AI assistant left students less motivated and lowered exam results for the weaker classes. Engagement rose in those studies too. It was not the good news it looked like.

So the usable judgement, and it is one that Ed Tech Coaches will reach for on repeat this year: engagement is the metric a vendor can always show you — daily actives, adoption curves, "the students love it." Learning is the metric they usually can't, because it is slower and costlier to measure and sometimes comes back inconvenient. When the dashboard is all usage, the polite question is the sharp one — show me the outcome data, not the login data. "Everyone is using it" and "it is working" are different claims, and this study, for all its scale, only closes the first.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 2 August 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Generation got cheap; verification did not — so grade the interrogation, not the polish.

Week ending Sunday, 2 August 2026 · 1,453 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

The date everyone had circled is today. From 2 August 2026 the European Commission can finally enforce the AI Act against general-purpose model providers — demanding documentation, running evaluations, and fining a lab up to 3% of global annual turnover or €15 million, whichever is higher — and the Article 50 transparency duties, chatbot disclosure and AI-content marking among them, go live alongside (EU AI Act enforcement). What did not land today is the tell: the high-risk education obligations once due now were pushed to December 2027 by last Monday's Digital Omnibus (Reg 2026/1744). The model-makers got two more years; the clauses pointing at the classroom didn't move.

Otherwise: Opus 5 turned the reasoning budget into a dial, the Hugging Face breach ran all week, and a quiet stack of student-wellbeing research piled up — most of it, as it happens, pointing the same way.

What Does It All Mean?

The question the week left on the desk is a plain one. If a chatbot now reliably produces the finished artifact — the essay, the lab write-up, the worked solution — what is a grade on that artifact actually measuring?

Start with the week's most quietly damning pairing. A late-July Sungkyunkwan University study of 88 students found that those who used AI during problem-solving outscored classmates who used it only to understand concepts — yet the heaviest AI-reliant students posted the lowest real test scores, all while reporting markedly higher "perceived learning" and satisfaction (Phys.org). Performance up, learning down, and — the dangerous part — the learner's own gauge reading backwards.

It is not a new effect. Fan and colleagues, in the British Journal of Educational Technology, gave it a name: "metacognitive laziness." Their ChatGPT group wrote the best essays of any condition and showed no better knowledge gain or transfer than students left to struggle (Monash / BJET).

Now the mirror at the other end of the sophistication scale. Last week's cryptography headline was really about verification. Anthropic reported a model producing a novel attack in about a week, then noted that "the vast majority of our time over the past few months has been in verifying the correctness of Claude's results" (Anthropic). Matthew Green's gloss: "just because a model spits out an apparent new result, this does not mean the result is real" (Green). Generation got cheap; verification did not. A frontier lab with unlimited money now spends its scarce hours checking, not producing.

That is precisely the asymmetry the students walked into from below. When the fluent output is nearly free, the entire value — and the entire difficulty — migrates into the checking: knowing whether the answer is right, where it is thin, what it quietly assumed. Which drops the assessment problem on the desk. A task scored on the polish of its output now measures the tool's capability, not the student's judgement, and this week's evidence says the two have come apart.

The instinctive fix — detection — is the wrong one, and the field is already conceding it, shifting weight toward in-person, oral and process-based assessment (Times Higher Education). The better move follows from the diagnosis: grade the verification layer — the interrogation of the output — because that is the thing AI cannot hand over, and the thing the crypto lab just confirmed is now the whole job. And it all lands into a governance vacuum. Stanford's 2026 AI Index found four in five students using AI while just 6% of teachers call their school's policy even clear (Stanford HAI).

Behind You! Behind You!

The rigorous study the week's adoption surveys drowned out. Amid the usual "teachers save weeks with AI" reporting, a Wharton team — Alp Sungu, Benjamin Lira Luttges and Angela Duckworth — ran the thing this field rarely gets: a randomized controlled trial. Across 193 teachers and some 2,800 students in a Turkish school chain, giving teachers a GPT-4o assistant left students less motivated — classes rated less interesting, less important — and lowered exam scores for students whose teachers were already weaker (FutureEd). Sungu's read: teachers "might be using AI as a crutch ... and that lowers the quality of their teaching." A 2025 working paper, not yet peer-reviewed — but a kind of evidence the cheerful adoption counts are not.

A tool students use changed underneath them. Google renamed NotebookLM to Gemini Notebook (rollout from 16 July), moved it onto Gemini 3.5, and — the part worth flagging — now runs each notebook on its own secure cloud computer that can write and execute code for deeper analysis (Workspace Updates). A research aid that has quietly gained a code interpreter is a different data-governance object than the one on last term's approved list.

The half of the Classroom redesign that actually touches students. The new role-based Classroom homepage was noted mostly for its dashboards; the under-reported half is the AI layer. From 27 July, students hovering over an assignment get a "Learn with Gemini" menu of study actions, and a "Class learning tools" module surfaces teacher-assigned Gemini notebooks and Gems — appearing only where Gemini is switched on for the org unit (Workspace Updates). Gemini is now one hover from every assignment.

The Riff Returns But Slower

  • 10 August — OpenAI's Student Collective campus-lead applications close. The schools version of the ambassador model is coming; worth a settled view before it arrives.
  • 2 December 2026 — the EU AI Act's two new intimate-imagery prohibitions bite, alongside machine-readable marking of general-purpose systems already on the market. The high-risk education obligations now wait until 2 December 2027.
  • "Coming weeks" — OpenAI's promised technical report on the Hugging Face breach. The document to actually read, against the four gaps observers already named: disclosure thresholds, containment standards, unmonitored monitoring, and a capability-over-defence bias.
  • Slow burn — the open-weight frontier keeps cheapening. Kimi K3 and Switzerland's multimodal Apertus 1.5 both landed this week; the visible effect for schools is downward pressure on hosted-inference prices, not anything a school would self-host.
  • Slow burn — the evidence base is thin but no longer only correlational: it now holds a genuine RCT (above), resurfaced by this week's coverage. Watch whether the autumn's full Jed Foundation report brings method to match.

Sceptic's Corner

If you read only the safety headlines, this was the week the grown-ups arrived. NVIDIA convened a 37-member Open Secure AI Alliance (The Hacker News) and more than 1,100 frontier-lab employees signed a letter asking Washington to help "pace" AI development (Trending Topics). Microsoft began paying eighteen university labs to red-team its models (Microsoft), the EU's enforcement powers over model-makers went live today (EU AI Act), and even the connector plumbing hardened with a security-focused revision of the MCP standard (Model Context Protocol). A staffroom optimist could reasonably conclude the guardrails are finally going up.

Now stand where a school stands. Every one of those mechanisms points at the vendor, the frontier lab, or the government — and not one touches the two AI risks that are actually inside a school this term. The first is deepfakes of classmates: Common Sense Media found 44% of teenagers have seen AI-generated sexual imagery, and a quarter of those said it depicted them or someone they know (Common Sense Media). The second is quiet over-reliance: the Jed Foundation found nearly three in five students turn to AI when sad, stressed or lonely (Jed Foundation). No alliance, letter or enforcement power that shipped this week moves either number.

The asymmetry underneath is worse than an oversight. The offensive capability escaped a lab a fortnight ago (TechCrunch); the defensive Gemini model built to find vulnerabilities is gated behind a government whitelist (TechCrunch); and the four companies actually building the agents arriving on education tiers — OpenAI, Google, Meta, Anthropic — were the names missing from that alliance. The scaffolding is real, but it is going up around the vendor floor of the building, several storeys from the classroom.

So the usable judgement: a school waiting for "the industry to sort out safety" before it writes a deepfake-incident protocol or a pathway for the student talking to a chatbot at one in the morning is waiting for the wrong contractor. The two controls that matter this term are ones the school writes itself — and nothing that made the safety headlines this week gets them written.
