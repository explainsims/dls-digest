Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Wednesday, 29 July 2026

Top story

Europe rewrote its AI rulebook on Monday, and the one clause pointing straight at school practice got weaker rather than merely later.

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 — the Digital Omnibus on AI, adopted 8 July and published in the Official Journal on 24 July — entered into force on 27 July, the third day after publication, having been drafted to take effect "as a matter of urgency" (EUR-Lex). It amends the 2024 AI Act on three fronts a school would feel. The timetable first: full obligations for Annex III high-risk systems now fall due on 2 December 2027, and 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products (AI Front Page, 27 July 2026). Education sits squarely inside Annex III — systems that determine admission, evaluate learning outcomes in ways that steer the learning process, assess the appropriate level of education for a person, or monitor for prohibited behaviour during tests (Annex III). Second, two new Article 5 prohibitions bite on 2 December 2026: generating or manipulating realistic intimate material without "freely-given, specific, informed, unambiguous and explicit consent", and the equivalent involving minors (NicFab). Article 5 breaches carry the top penalty band, up to €35 million or 7% of global turnover, and the transparency duty for newly placed generative systems still applies from 2 August 2026, with machine-readable marking of systems already on the market due 2 December 2026 (Modulos).

Third, and least covered: Article 4's AI-literacy duty has been softened from ensuring staff hold sufficient AI literacy to taking measures that support it, with explicit text that the obligation "does not require... guarantee any specific level". Schools outside the EU are not deployers under this regulation, but vendors ship one compliance posture globally and the marking rules change what appears inside tools everywhere. The deadlines that slipped were the vendors'. The clause aimed at practice moved the other way — and it moved down.

Other stories

  • NVIDIA convened 36 partners to secure AI agents, and every frontier lab stayed home. The Open Secure AI Alliance launched on 27 July with 37 organisations — NVIDIA plus 36 — among them Microsoft, Cisco, Cloudflare, CrowdStrike, Hugging Face, IBM, Palo Alto Networks, Red Hat, Elastic and the Linux Foundation; OpenAI, Google, Meta and Anthropic do not appear on the inaugural membership list (The Hacker News, 27 July 2026). NVIDIA contributed NOOA, an Apache 2.0 research framework meant to make agent behaviour testable, traceable, auditable and governable, and Jensen Huang framed the whole thing against last week's incident: "During the Hugging Face incident, closed AI blocked essential forensics. An open-weight frontier model helped contain the intrusion" (EdTech Innovation Hub, 29 July 2026). Exabeam CISO Kevin Kirkwood named the gap plainly: "The major frontier model developers need to be at the table, and the industry needs agreed rules for liability when an agent exceeds scope" (Infosecurity Magazine, 28 July 2026). The practical read for a school is unglamorous: the agents arriving on education tiers this term are built by the four companies not in the room.

  • Canvas institutions start receiving their own stolen files this week, three months after the intrusion. Instructure began sending secure ShareFile links to designated institutional security contacts from 26 July, with live webinars explaining the delivered data files running 29–31 July; institutions had until 17:00 ET on 22 July to nominate up to two named individuals — explicitly people, "not shared inboxes" — to receive them (Instructure incident update). Delivery had been paused on 14 July "out of an abundance of caution" after the third-party platform chosen to distribute the packets was itself reported to face a security threat; Instructure's 21 July note says that threat "did not involve Canvas or any Instructure systems, and your data was never at risk." On the underlying intrusion the two available accounts disagree, and the primary one is worse: early coverage put it at 30 April, detected 1 May (Hackread, 6 May 2026), while Instructure's own page dates detection to 29 April, records a second incident on 7 May, and confirms containment only on 9 May. ShinyHunters claimed 3.65 terabytes and 275 million records; Instructure's confirmed categories are usernames, email addresses, course names, enrollment information and messages, plus API provisioning records and user reports — with core learning data (course content, submissions, credentials) not compromised, and "no evidence that passwords, dates of birth, government identifiers, or financial information were involved." Just under three months from intrusion to "here is what they took from you" is the number to carry into any LMS transition — and a school running neither Canvas nor its parent's stack should treat this as a rehearsal rather than an incident.

  • A 152,000-student analysis says the lever is clearer goals, not more feedback — and it ran on pre-AI data. The Education University of Hong Kong published research on 23 July from a team led by Professor Zi Yan analysing 151,969 students across 19 countries and regions in PISA. Frequent feedback alone did not lift attainment; school-wide clarity of learning goals combined with systematic progress monitoring was associated with a 0.158-standard-deviation gain in reading achievement. Yan's framing is that "An assessment ecology is shaped jointly by school policies, teacher practices and teacher-student interactions", and that in an examination-oriented system "teachers may devote considerable time to feedback, yet students may not use it effectively" (Phys.org, 23 July 2026). Read the framing carefully before quoting it as AI evidence. The paper is "School Assessment Policy, Teacher Assessment Practice and Training, and Reading Achievement: A Multi-Level Analysis of PISA 2018 Data", published in Education Sciences in April, and PISA 2018 predates generative AI entirely. The AI belongs to two platforms the group has since built, EASE and FAITH, at roughly 2,200 and 350 registered users. The assessment finding stands on its own merits; it is not a finding about AI, and the press framing quietly conflates the two.

  • Britain's new prime minister put a technical route at 14 at the centre of the schools plan. Andy Burnham announced on 28 July that pupils in England will be able to take high-quality technical subjects — engineering, healthcare, digital technology — alongside core English, maths and science from age 14, with regional mayors, colleges and employers rather than Whitehall shaping which specialisms a locality runs: advanced manufacturing, AI, digital tech, clean energy, life sciences, construction, health and care, creative industries. "For too long in this country, students have been told that you must take the academic path" to succeed, he said, adding that "From today, Britain will value the hard hat every bit as much as the graduation cap" (Times Higher Education, 28 July 2026). The supporting numbers are the actual argument: more than a million UK 16-to-24-year-olds are not in education, employment or training, and higher-level apprentices' median earnings five years after completion run £37,300 against £32,100 for first-degree graduates five years after graduation (EdTech Innovation Hub, 29 July 2026). Any school whose counselling model quietly assumes university is the destination now has a national data point arguing the other way.

  • A university named an associate provost for AI and pointedly did not name a platform. Wake Forest appointed William Fleeson, Hultquist Family Professor of Psychology, as Associate Provost for AI Initiatives on 28 July, coordinating an "AI for Human Flourishing" programme covering campus AI priorities, support for faculty and staff leaders across schools and divisions, and long-term academic strategy. Three cross-school pilots launch in 2026-27: AI Compass on ethics and societal implications, led by Ana Iltis; an AI Teaching Studio on teaching and learning; and an AI Impact Clinic running applied projects with outside partners, led by Shannon McKeen, Paul Pauca and Errin Fulp. The stated framing is that the goal is "not simply to adopt new technology" (EdTech Innovation Hub, 28 July 2026). Two details travel down to secondary regardless of the higher-education setting: the lead is a psychologist, not a technologist, and the deliverables are three named pilots with named owners rather than a licence count. That is a structure which can report progress without reporting seats filled — which is the metric most institutional AI programmes quietly default to when nobody specifies a better one.

On the radar

  • OpenAI picked Dublin for its EU headquarters, two days after the omnibus took effect. The company will take the Tropical Fruit Warehouse in Dublin's south docks and add 250 roles over two years on top of a team of roughly 100 built up over three years, with the OpenAI for Ireland programme running from the site and IDA Ireland backing the investment; Emma Redmond, who heads OpenAI Ireland, said the HQ "will help ensure that the benefits of AI reach communities, businesses and institutions here and across the region" (EdTech Innovation Hub, 29 July 2026). Establishment is not a press-release detail. Where a provider's main EU establishment sits determines which regulator any school-side data complaint actually lands with.

  • An AI-literacy platform for schools raised $4.5 million with an unusually loud cap table. imagi closed a seed round on 24 July led by Brighteye Ventures and Day One Capital, with We Are Human (founded by Kahoot's founders), Morgan Stanley, FemInvest and Sparkmind Capital, plus angels including will.i.am, ElevenLabs founder Mati Staniszewski and former GitHub CEO Thomas Dohmke. Founded by Dora Palfi and Beatrice Ionascu, it gives K-12 students supervised access to AI tools inside curriculum-based learning, partnering with OpenAI and Lovable for guardrails, and reports operating across more than 100 US school districts (EdTech Innovation Hub, 24 July 2026). Watch whether "supervised access" survives contact with scale, or quietly becomes a settings page.

  • OpenAI is hiring someone whose job is to get its products inside funded research labs. A newly posted Academic Research Partnerships & Programs Lead, based in San Francisco or New York, combines business development with programme management and targets adoption of Pro, Codex, the API and ChatGPT Edu across funded US laboratories and R1 universities, building on existing relationships with labs at MIT, Harvard, the Max Planck Society, A*STAR Research, Oxford and Stanford; Education Lead Jayna Devani framed it as closing a gap in "health, science, and other public-benefit domains" (EdTech Innovation Hub, 28 July 2026). Job postings are the cheapest available forecast of where a vendor's next institutional push lands.

Sceptic's corner

The claim already circulating is that Brussels blinked — that the Digital Omnibus is a retreat and the compliance pressure is off until 2027. It is an easy story to tell, because the headline number genuinely moved: Annex III conformity work slid to 2 December 2027, and the proposed "standards-readiness trigger", which would have tied deadlines to technical standards arriving, was deleted for fixed calendar dates (Modulos).

Now test it from the school's side of the desk. What moved is work vendors owe: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, human oversight, database registration. What did not move is 2 August 2026 for transparency on newly placed generative systems, or 2 December 2026 for the intimate-imagery prohibitions and marking (NicFab). And Article 4, the only clause in the Act that ever pointed at staff capability, did not get a later date. It got a lower bar — an obligation that explicitly "does not require... guarantee any specific level".

So the net effect on a school is worse than "two more years", not better. The deployer's duty of care is unchanged, the prohibitions that touch students soonest arrive on the original schedule, and the vendor documentation that would have given a school something concrete to read before signing is now more than a year further away — landing inside the window when most schools choose their AI tooling.

The staffroom line: December 2027 is the vendors' deadline, not the school's. The only thing that reached the school's desk this week is a literacy obligation that no longer has to work.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 14 June 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

A frontier model vanished from every classroom in 72 hours — the lesson is to build on task design, not the tool.

Week ending Sunday, 14 June 2026 · 1,540 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

The weekend handed us the story of the week, after Friday's brief had filed. On 12 June the US Commerce Department ordered Anthropic to suspend Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 — and because the company says it cannot reliably separate foreign nationals from anyone else in real time, it pulled both models for every user on Earth, paying customers and its own staff included (Anthropic, 12 June; Bloomberg, 13 June). Secretary Howard Lutnick's letter to Dario Amodei cited national security and an export-control directive barring access "by any foreign national," reportedly triggered by a jailbreak demonstrated against Mythos 5. Fable 5 launched 9 June as the most capable model the company had shipped; three days later it was a global blackout by government fiat. Anthropic is complying but disputing — it says the vulnerability was minor, previously known, and present in rival frontier models too (Washington Post, 13 June).

The rest, briefly: Apple's WWDC turned the managed device into an enforcement surface (lockable browser, multi-app mode, harder assessment configs), and OpenAI's rewritten ChatGPT memory began reaching free accounts. The other genuinely-fresh item: Inside Higher Ed and Generation Lab published a Student Voice survey on 11 June — 1,038 students, 203 institutions. Only 7% call themselves "all in" on AI; 40% worry about dependence; just 10% think their institution is handling it well. Students are more sceptical than the staffroom assumes.

What Does It All Mean?

Here is the question the week quietly posed: if a school's whole AI policy rests on disclosure — "tell me which tool you used, and what for" — is that instrument still usable? Three threads from the dailies converged on the same uncomfortable answer.

Thread one, Wednesday: Anthropic's Fable 5 silently routed most biology and chemistry requests to Claude Opus 4.8, so a student often could not know which model answered them (Anthropic). (The model is now suspended — see above — but the legibility point it illustrated stands, and indeed sharpens.) Thread two, Thursday: OpenAI's new memory writes a persistent profile the user cannot fully see, that survives a deleted chat, and that the company admits the summary page does not completely list (implicator.ai, 4 June). Thread three, all week: the EU AI Act's chatbot-transparency obligations land 2 August, leaning hard on disclosure as the safeguard.

Now two voices from outside the headlines. A 2025 paper on the AI Act, Manipulation and the Danger of Mirrors, argues that a label reading "this is AI" does not neutralise an interface engineered to feel reciprocal — knowing it is a machine does not stop the social response. Disclosure assumes a rational reader the architecture is built to bypass. In the other direction, the detection literature keeps hardening: a January 2026 review finds AI detectors generate more false accusations than correct identifications, with documented racial skew against non-native writers. So the school cannot reliably confirm a disclosure that was made, nor catch one that was withheld.

Put the three threads and the two voices together and the conclusion is not "disclosure is worthless." It is narrower and more useful: disclosure is a weak primitive to build on, and a worse one every quarter. The tools are becoming less legible (silent fallbacks, invisible memory) while the regulators lean harder on legibility, and verification fails in both directions.

The shutdown adds a second lesson pointing the same way. A school cannot even see which model answered — and now learns it cannot count on the model being there at all. Fable 5 was available to students on Tuesday and gone by government order on Friday, for reasons with nothing to do with the school. That is an argument against building any classroom routine on a single vendor's top model — the same conclusion the disclosure problem reaches from the other side. Legibility says you cannot fully know what the tool is doing; continuity says you cannot assume the tool will still exist. Both push the weight off the tool and onto the design around it.

This is where last week's three-mode contract earns a second look — not as the answer, but with a correction. Baseline / scaffold / open mode works because it shifts the burden off the student's honesty and onto the task design: a baseline task is built so that AI cannot quietly do the work, regardless of what anyone discloses — or which model is up this week. The honest upgrade is to stop treating "students disclose consequential uses" as the load-bearing control in open mode. Treat disclosure as a teaching habit worth cultivating — and assume it will be partial, sometimes impossible, and never the thing that makes an inference valid. The structural choice carries the weight; the label, and the model, are courtesies.

Behind You! Behind You!

The WWDC education story has an IT half the dailies underplayed. Jamf's 11 June education recap reads the keynote for administrators: expanded Platform SSO and offline authentication, QR-code and guest-access onboarding on iPad, and declarative controls for binaries, app config, and Safari permissions. Its theme — "identity is becoming foundational, management more autonomous" — is the unglamorous plumbing behind every device-mode policy. Worth a read before any Apple-side rollout conversation.

Students want course design, not more AI modules. Buried in that 11 June Student Voice survey: 24% of students said the thing they most want is authentic learning preserved through better assignment design, and 60% want AI offered as personalised support rather than bolted on (Inside Higher Ed). It is higher-ed and self-report, but it points the same way as the teacher data: the demand is for better tasks, not louder tools.

The Riff Returns But Slower

  • Live — Fable 5 / Mythos 5 restoration. When access returns is the open question; as of this weekend both models remain fully suspended, with no court order restoring them, while Anthropic disputes the directive and says it is working to restore access (Anthropic, 12 June). The wider Anthropic–government relationship is already adversarial — the DOJ is contesting a separate, earlier order tied to the winter federal-agency ban — so expect this fought, not quietly resolved. The durable signal is the precedent: a frontier model can now be export-controlled out of every classroom at once.
  • 2026-06-28 to 07-01 — ISTELive/ASCD. As ever, the value arrives after the stagecraft: released session resources and case studies, not keynote adjectives (ISTE).
  • 2026-06-30 — Colorado's AI Act takes effect, covering high-risk systems including those affecting educational access — the first concrete compliance date in this wave.
  • 2026-08-02 — EU AI Act transparency and high-risk education obligations. An Omnibus delay has been discussed but is not law until published; until then the date stands for anyone selling grading or proctoring AI into the EU.
  • Slow burn — Utah's actual district uptake of Gemini for Education; whether consumer-tier memory controls get more auditable under August's regulatory pressure; the steadily growing — but still thin — base of rigorous causal evidence behind all of it.

Sceptic's Corner

The hype to squeeze this week is the quiet assumption underneath every "AI governance is maturing" deck: that transparency is the mature control — that once a system tells you it is AI, once memory has a settings page, once disclosure is required by law, the responsible-use problem is largely architected.

The week argued the opposite, gently and from multiple directions. The page that lists your ChatGPT memories is admitted to be incomplete. The model on the label is not always the model that answered. The detector that would verify a student's disclosure is, per the January 2026 review, more likely to be wrong than right and biased against the students least able to defend themselves. And the regulatory instrument arriving in August leans on exactly the disclosure mechanism a 2025 AI Act paper says users' own psychology is built to ignore.

And the week's own headline made the point sharper: the most transparent thing imaginable — a model with a card and published safety warnings — was pulled offline because of a published safety finding. The lesson from the shutdown is not transparency. It is continuity: a tool you can fully inspect is no use to a class on the morning a government turns it off.

None of that is an argument for cynicism, and certainly not for AI-detector theatre — the detectors are the worst offender here, not the cure. It is an argument for putting the weight somewhere it can bear load. A disclosure norm is a fine thing to teach and a poor thing to enforce; a single best model is a fine thing to use and a poor thing to depend on. The validity of what a student hands in should rest on how the task was built, what evidence of thinking it requires, and what data it keeps off the open web — none of which depend on a label being accurate, or a model still being switched on. Transparency was never going to be the safeguard, and this week it looked less like one than usual.
