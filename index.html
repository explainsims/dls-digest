Monday, 2 November 2026

Top story

Being searchable and being AI training material are becoming separate choices.

Microsoft's 30 October announcement gives web publishers independent preferences for model training and for grounding, the use of web content to inform a generated answer. A site could refuse training while remaining eligible to supply answers and appear in traditional Bing results. The controls are announced, not active: Microsoft will begin honouring them as it crawls from 1 February 2027. Publishers using the older NoArchive or NoCache settings face a transition before 1 March, when those settings stop being read as training or grounding preferences (Microsoft Bing, 30 October).

Google made a related announcement the same day. It plans to support a shared training preference in robots.txt during 2027, based on work at the Internet Engineering Task Force. Existing Google-Extended instructions would continue alongside it. This remains a draft proposal and a planned implementation, rather than a universal control already respected across providers (Google Search Central, 30 October).

For schools, the immediate value is the distinction. A public curriculum guide, an openly licensed teaching resource and a student publication may all deserve discovery while carrying different expectations about reuse. Training changes a model; grounding supplies material while it answers. Neither is equivalent to a reader following a search result to the original page. Asking whether a vendor uses data for AI is becoming too imprecise to produce a useful answer.

There is also a useful literacy lesson here. A student can compare three routes to the same source: opening the page, reading an AI answer citing it, and asking what it would mean for the source to enter training data. The author, the reader and the platform have different interests in each route. Understanding those differences is a more durable skill than memorising which chatbot button currently searches the web. The machinery is changing; the question of who may do what with somebody else's work is keeping its old job.

Other stories

  • Stanford's new research agenda makes the case for measuring what a quick test can miss. Published on 29 October, the SCALE agenda by Carly Robinson, Chris Agnew and Susanna Loeb asks researchers to examine AI's effects on persistence, curiosity, collaboration and self-regulation alongside academic performance. It proposes faster research cycles, shared measures and independent evaluation, and explicitly treats findings of no benefit as useful evidence. This is a programme for research, not a new trial demonstrating that any particular tool works (Stanford SCALE, 29 October). The practical contribution for international schools is a better definition of a successful pilot. Finishing a worksheet faster might be valuable; persisting less when the next task becomes difficult might be a cost. A school should be able to notice both. That need not mean inventing an elaborate measurement system for every experiment. It means naming the intended benefit and the capability that could quietly weaken, then observing each separately. A teacher's short record of what pupils can subsequently explain or attempt without help can reveal something a satisfaction question cannot. The agenda also makes an institutional point: evidence is easier to accumulate when successive pilots ask comparable questions. Every department starting its evaluation from a blank sheet is a surprisingly expensive form of independence.

  • A new writing study offers a concrete teaching routine, alongside a reason to inspect the reporting. A paper published on 31 October follows 88 first-year Ethiopian university students in two intact classes over ten weeks. One class used ChatGPT within a sequence of drafting, prompting, reflecting and revising; the comparison class received conventional teacher feedback. The authors report stronger writing outcomes in the AI-supported class, including an immediate post-test completed without AI. Individual students were not randomly assigned, and reflection and confidence were measured through questionnaires (Wordofa and Abo, 31 October). There is a conspicuous reporting problem: the methods list Cronbach's alpha values of 7.83 and 7.91 for one questionnaire. Those are outside the coefficient's valid range and need correction or explanation. That does not establish that the writing result is false, but it prevents an unqualified recommendation based on the published account. The interesting instructional idea is asking learners to explain a grammatical rule before revising, keeping a visible intellectual step between receiving feedback and submitting polished prose. The study cannot tell us how much of the reported difference belongs to ChatGPT itself and how much to the complete teaching package. A usable classroom idea and secure evidence for its effectiveness are different things; a paper can offer the first while leaving work to do on the second.

  • Jamaica's digital education pilot starts with the systems teachers already have to navigate. UNESCO's 29 October account describes a joint programme with UNICEF, FAO and WFP entering a pilot in 54 ministry-selected schools. It includes expansion of the Education Management Information System and better connections between existing systems holding teacher information. Principals and administrators are also due to receive digital transformation and AI training in early 2027. These are pilot activities and planned provision, not demonstrated improvements in learning or workload (UNESCO, 29 October). The useful contrast with much education technology publicity is where the work begins: fragmented information, duplicated navigation and the people expected to make the systems function. International schools may operate on a smaller scale, but the same failure is familiar when a pupil's timetable, support information and assessment record tell different stories. Adding an assistant can make each fragment easier to search without making them agree. The test of a connected system is whether a teacher reaches a timely, authoritative answer and understands where it came from. That is less photogenic than a new chatbot. It is also closer to the ordinary conditions that determine whether digital work saves a teacher ten minutes or costs another twenty.

  • Google Sheets is getting a small improvement with a clear job: keeping a pivot table readable. Google's 28 October release adds compact layout, nesting row fields in one indented column with controls to expand or collapse the hierarchy. It also preserves that layout when compact Excel pivot tables are imported and subsequently exported. Rapid Release rollout began on 22 October; Scheduled Release starts on 6 November, with up to 15 days for visibility in each rollout. The feature is available across Workspace editions and personal accounts (Google Workspace Updates, 28 October). For an Ed Tech Coach, this is the kind of change that earns its place by removing a specific irritation. A faculty comparing results by year, subject and subgroup can retain the hierarchy without pushing the useful numbers off the screen. It also makes a worthwhile data-literacy example: the same totals can be represented through different grouping and display choices, changing what a reader notices first. Compactness does not improve the underlying data or justify the categories. A narrower table can still conceal an unhelpfully broad group. The teaching opportunity is to ask learners which layout makes their comparison easier to inspect, and what becomes harder to see when categories collapse.

On the radar

  • A small mathematics study separates adaptive recommendations from a general chatbot. Published on 1 November, the study reports a pre-test/post-test controlled experiment with 60 senior high school students, comparing an LMS with an AI-supported recommendation system against personalised learning without AI. It reports gains in conceptual understanding and analytical skills, with an interaction between self-regulation and analytical outcomes (Auna and colleagues, 1 November). Sixty pupils cannot settle how well this travels across curricula. The important boundary is the intervention: selecting learning material through a recommendation system is a specific design. Its results cannot be borrowed as evidence that unrestricted chatbot access improves mathematics.

  • Central Asia's teacher-development discussion is bringing AI literacy back into information literacy. UNESCO IITE's 30 October report describes a 27 October session in Almaty linking AI competence with source verification, bias, privacy and academic integrity. Four existing courses for higher education teachers supplied examples; Kazakhstan's Orleu centre has translated and localised the materials for national professional development (UNESCO IITE, 30 October). This is a report of an approach, not an evaluation of its impact. The useful signal is curricular: AI training can strengthen how teachers evaluate information and platforms, instead of becoming a separate race to learn the latest interface.

Sceptic's corner

Publisher controls sound like a tidy settlement: authors state their preferences, machines respect them, and everyone gets on with their work. Today's announcements are useful progress. They also depend on a rather large word: participation.

A machine-readable preference helps a provider that chooses to honour it understand what the publisher wants. It does not, by itself, prevent an uncooperative crawler from copying the page. Nor does it answer every rights question about the text, images and pupil contributions collected there. Microsoft's announcement explicitly says these preferences do not establish licensing or payment arrangements (Microsoft Bing, 30 October). A setting is one part of a relationship, with contracts, attribution and enforcement still to do their own work.

The opposite conclusion would be equally lazy: because a control is incomplete, it is pointless. Standard signals can reduce confusion, make compliance easier and expose the difference between a provider respecting a preference and ignoring it. Those are real gains. They become less useful when a school turns them into a blanket assurance that public student work is protected.

The proportionate position is to distinguish a publication decision from a reuse preference. First decide whether the work belongs on the open web and whether the people represented understand that choice. Then express the permitted uses as clearly as the available mechanisms allow. A future training opt-out cannot retrospectively supply the judgment that should have preceded publication. Better controls improve that judgment's implementation; they do not take over the judgment.

The Week That Was

The week kept returning to one gap: naming a problem is not the same as stopping it. New York's Education Department named 38 products, then said it was working with companies to find out whether the offending features could be disabled at all (Chalkbeat, 23 October). The UK's AI Security Institute described an agent that often said an attack would be harmful in the very situations where it went ahead (AISI, 23 October). Gemini's immersive explorations reached the student hub in Education editions with the line "There is no admin control for this feature" (Google, 28 October).

Since Friday's brief: whole-deck editing with Gemini in Slides, which schools get through the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, carries the same line (Google, 30 October). For schools, that makes the add-on licence the only switch. OpenAI said ChatGPT will restrict practical weapons information for teens even without signs of violent intent, end chats that seem fixated on violence, and may pause accounts after repeated ones, telling parents when a teen's is paused. Rollout starts within weeks (Reuters via AOL, 30 October).

What Does It All Mean?

Every story above assumes a lever somewhere. So the question worth a Sunday: when a school cannot switch a feature off, what is its AI rule actually made of?

Start with the lever schools think they have. Among American teens who use AI for schoolwork, Common Sense Media found in August that 44% had had an AI tool blocked on a school network or device, and 59% of those simply moved to a personal device, where there is even less support (Common Sense Media, 18 August). A network block is a detour, not a wall.

Assessment researchers have been making this case for a while. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu split institutional responses into discursive changes, which tell students what is allowed, and structural ones, which change how the task itself works. Traffic-light permission schemes, they argue, borrow the look of a real enforcement system without its power, and leaning on rules like these creates what they call an "enforcement illusion" (Corbin, Dawson and Liu, 2025).

The University of Sydney, where Liu works, had already written the idea into policy: from 2025, AI allowed by default except in exams and in-semester tests, then two lanes (ACS Information Age, December 2024). Secured, supervised tasks leave AI to the examiner; everything else is open, with students guided in its use. Its FAQ is blunt that AI use cannot be restricted in assessments not secured face to face, nor reliably detected (Teaching@Sydney, updated April 2026).

Even the AI Assessment Scale's own authors agree. Leon Furze, one of them, writes that assessment security is not what the scale aims at; it is a framework to support assessment design, and a colourful handout plus "a hopeful expectation" secures nothing (Furze, September 2025).

That would be bleak if rules kept students honest. They never did. A follow-up study published in February, surveying 4,354 students at six US high schools about eighteen months after ChatGPT's release, found an overall cheating rate of 72.06%, which its authors call consistent with historical baselines; AI availability does not appear to have changed overall prevalence (Chen et al., February 2026). The tool changed the method, not the rate. Whatever sets the rate, whether engagement, pressure or a task that feels worth doing, sits upstream of any switch.

So the working answer: an AI rule rests on three layers, in falling order of reliability. First, what is structurally true of the task: done in the room, staged, defended aloud. Second, what the school can actually switch, which is coarse and covers products more than features. Third, what it tells students, which is invaluable for conversation and useless as security.

For a school running an AI use scale, the practical move is small. The scale stays as the shared language. But every task pitched at the no-AI or low-AI end needs a one-line answer to "what makes this true in the room?" If the honest answer is that the student promised, the task is open and should be marked as one.

Behind You! Behind You!

New York will treat unverified chatbot users as children. Governor Hochul signed S.9051B/A.10379C on 28 October. In the coming months, companies may not offer under-18s, or any user not confirmed as an adult, features such as posing as human, claiming a relationship, asking unprompted emotional questions or using a child's private details from earlier chats. The attorney general can seek up to $25,000 per violation (New York State, 28 October). The state's Business Council had argued that educational tools were swept in (TechTimes, 29 October). Watch whether vendors build that default once and ship it everywhere.

States want proof and can't get it. SETDA's 2026 State EdTech Trends report, published 29 October from 73 survey responses, found 79% of state leaders naming AI a top technology priority. Yet 52% said their state collects no data on the use or effectiveness of ed-tech products, and 68% said independently verifying vendor claims about AI products is challenging (GovTech, 29 October). If American states with procurement offices struggle, an international school is checking alone.

Half of the US schools and districts surveyed had a cyber incident last year. The Center for Internet Security's 2026 K-12 report puts it at 51% in the past 12 months, and 66% of districts leave cybersecurity to one or two people (CIS, 27 October). Phishing or email compromise ranked among the top incidents for 87% of leaders, and the report warns of more convincing AI-enabled phishing (K-12 Dive, 27 October). The weak point is staff inboxes, not servers.

The ed-tech backlash is on the ballot, and it isn't partisan. Ahead of Tuesday's US midterms, Chalkbeat found a Republican candidate for California governor promising to ban AI in schools on day one. In Texas, the Republican governor pledges less classroom screen time and his Democratic challenger opposes AI for elementary students. Seven of eight San Francisco school board candidates back limits on classroom technology (Chalkbeat, 26 October). International schools enrolling families from those systems should expect the mood to travel with them.

Sceptic's Corner

Next time someone says the research shows AI improves learning, ask which research.

Jill Barshay reports 39 meta-analyses on AI in education, every one finding substantial benefits. Patrick O'Neill, a community college professor, audited 14 of them and found serious conceptual and statistical flaws, from hallucinated references to underlying studies citing generative-AI papers published before the technology was public. One, published in a Nature journal in May 2025, has since been retracted (Hechinger Report, 26 October).

When O'Neill pooled 44 well-designed experiments, outcomes ranged from very helpful to very harmful, and his preliminary calculations put the average at roughly zero. Susanna Loeb's team gathered almost 900 studies and judged only 20 good enough for its evidence summary. O'Neill's audit is itself a preprint and his numbers are preliminary, so hold him to the same standard.

Two things follow. A literature that is uniformly positive is a warning, not a comfort; real interventions produce null results. An average of roughly zero is not a verdict of harmlessness. It is what you get when good designs and bad ones cancel out, which is why the question is always which use, for whom, checked how.

The line for a staff meeting: there is no effect size for "AI", only for particular uses of it. Anyone quoting one number is quoting a brochure.
