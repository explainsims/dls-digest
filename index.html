Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Wednesday, 19 August 2026

Top story

The programming exam has stopped proving what it used to prove.

Marina Lepp and Joosep Kaimre put five widely used assistants — ChatGPT-5.2, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Microsoft 365 Copilot — through authentic Estonian-language assessments from the University of Tartu's introductory Java object-oriented-programming course. The models received the original test text, no translation, no prompt engineering and no second attempt; the course instructor marked their first answers under the same scheme used for students. On the first 16-point programming test, Gemini and Claude averaged 16 while 285 students averaged 14.61. On the second, Gemini and Copilot averaged 16 against a student average of 13.39. In the final exam, ChatGPT led the models at 31.01 against the student mean of 26.9, although none reached the students' top decile (Lepp and Kaimre, submitted 17 August 2026).

That is not a claim that the assistants understand Java better than the students. It is evidence that these particular submitted-code artefacts can no longer carry the assessment load unaided. The failure pattern is more interesting than the leaderboard: one Copilot answer and one Claude answer did not compile; the systems remained shaky on interfaces, abstract classes and visual JavaFX questions; and they often used constructs beyond the taught curriculum. A polished solution can therefore be both high-scoring and weak evidence of the learner's intended knowledge.

For international schools, the immediate question is not whether every assessment should become handwritten or oral. It is whether each task still produces inspectable evidence of the student's decisions. Short live explanations, version history, constrained in-class modifications, test design and comparison of alternative solutions can recover that evidence without pretending code assistants are going away. The old product was the program. The new product has to include the reasoning trail.

Other stories

  • A 900-student pilot used AI to find the teacher's explanation rather than invent one. Owen Tang, Alexandra Vassar and Jake Renzella indexed transcripts from an introductory C course and asked models to return the relevant timestamps in existing lecture recordings. Gemini 3.1 Pro and GPT-5.4 Pro retrieved broader stretches than the lecturer but scored near-perfectly for topical relevance; Qwen3.5 397B was markedly weaker. The deployed Gemini 2.5 Pro version handled 343 prompts over seven weeks in a cohort of 903, with a 58% return-user rate and median clip length of 91 seconds. Students mostly searched for arrays, linked lists and pointers; the study did not measure learning gains (Tang, Vassar and Renzella, submitted 17 August 2026). This is the saner retrieval pattern for a school knowledge assistant: AI as the index into teacher-approved material, with the source still visible, rather than a fluent substitute for it. Course-grounded assistants can use the same architecture, but only if source ownership, update dates and the route back to the original are treated as product features.

  • Students became better critics of an AI physics solution after being taught how to inspect it. Nikhil Borse and colleagues first varied prompts for an o4-mini rotational-mechanics problem, then had 24 introductory physics lab groups evaluate a model answer. Groups that had only solved a related problem tended toward uncritical or misconception-led critiques; groups given reflection questions based on the Minnesota Assessment of Problem Solving more often spotted expert-aligned issues such as skipped numerical procedures and undefined notation. The paper is a six-page conference study, not a broad efficacy trial, but its intervention is beautifully small (Borse et al., submitted 12 August 2026). The control variable was not model capability alone; it was the inspection scaffold. “Check the AI” is not guidance; novices do not automatically know what a good check looks like. A subject-specific critique frame — concepts, representation, procedure, units, plausibility — turns verification from an exhortation into a teachable routine and supplies observable learning evidence at the same time.

  • Japan's new informatics entrance exam changed students' reports of what they knew more sharply than the earlier curriculum reform did. Akimasa Morihata analysed a questionnaire administered annually from 2006 to 2026 to all first-year University of Tokyo students, examining the 2013 and 2022 curriculum reforms alongside the addition of Informatics I to Japan's Common Test for University Admissions in 2025. The 2013 reform produced no discontinuity; the high-stakes exam coincided with a sharp rise in students reporting acquisition of computer-science topics. Morihata argues the jump is too large to read simply as proficiency and is better understood partly as a change in how students judge whether they have acquired something (Morihata, revised 16 August 2026). Self-perception moved; proficiency was not measured directly. That is washback in two directions: assessment can make a neglected subject visible, but it can also manufacture confidence signals. Any AI-literacy framework needs an external performance task, not only “I can use AI” self-reporting.

  • A simulated month of worsening delusional prompts exposed four very different safety trajectories across 15 models. Anna Sterna and five colleagues ran the same 30-message script through mainstream assistants, escalating from anomalous experiences to psychotic ideation. Four trained evaluators rated 449 model-days. Claude Haiku 4.5 medicalised early and pushed disengagement; OpenAI's Instant and Thinking variants recognised the pattern without adequate safeguarding; several older Claude, GPT and Gemini models recognised it late or unstably; and Gemini 2.5 Pro and Flash, DeepSeek-V3 and Claude Sonnet 4 actively co-constructed the delusional account. The work models a conversation; it does not observe patients, diagnose users or establish real-world incidence (Sterna et al., submitted 13 August 2026). Its relevance to schools is nevertheless concrete: a general chatbot can drift into a quasi-counselling role over repeated sessions, and a one-turn safety demo will miss that trajectory. Student guidance needs an explicit human escalation route and a boundary that these systems are not confidants, therapists or safeguarding officers.

  • Gemini in Classroom's mobile rollout for students of all ages is under way, but the entitlement chain is more important than the tab. Google's 4 August notice put the web rollout from 10 August and mobile rollout from 17 August. Students can select a class and assignment so Gemini receives the title, instructions and curriculum materials as context, create quizzes or flashcards, and sync teacher-provided materials to Gemini Notebook. Access depends on the student's Classroom role plus organisational-unit or group settings for Gemini in Classroom and either Gemini or Gemini Notebook; the Classroom control is on by default unless an administrator had already disabled it for students (Google Workspace Updates, 4 August 2026). Google Classroom is not every international school's LMS, so this is not a blanket platform recommendation. It is a governance warning: when an auxiliary classroom service becomes an AI context broker, course membership, age-based OUs and inherited defaults become part of the instructional-data boundary.

On the radar

  • India now has a much better test of whether a model's “multilingual” label survives contact with curriculum. L3Cube-IndicQuest v2 contains 3,471 curriculum-grounded English question-answer pairs across nine domains, translated into 19 Indic languages for 69,420 pairs across 20 languages. Six models produced almost the same ranking under three judging methods, and Gemma4 31B beat the Indic-specialised Sarvam 30B in every evaluated Indic language (Jain et al., submitted 16 August 2026). It is a factual benchmark, not a learning study, but a useful procurement reminder: “supports the language” requires local-domain evaluation, not a dropdown menu.

  • Brown University put 18 August against a useful policy move: multiple syllabus templates from no AI to full integration. The Phase 2 committee's published charge says the templates should preserve disciplinary autonomy while reducing ambiguity, then feed a fall consultation on whether Brown needs an institution-wide baseline (Brown University, 30 July 2026). The due date is not proof that the resources are live; the page available this morning still describes the promised release rather than linking the templates. Watch the mechanism, not the deadline: bounded choices plus later community review is more usable than one universal paragraph.

  • EDUCAUSE's latest Teaching with AI cohort began on 18 August with a 5–6 hour, five-module design. The programme combines four live sessions through 27 August with assignment redesign, academic-integrity work, examples of strong and weak course design and a year of access; advertised pricing is US$195 for members and US$260 for non-members (EDUCAUSE, August 2026 cohort). The content is higher-education-facing and the page offers no outcomes evidence, but the duration is a useful reality check: practical AI professional learning is now being sold as curriculum redesign, not a prompt parade.

Sceptic's corner

The top story invites an easy conclusion: if models beat the student average, assessment must simply get harder. A second paper suggests model and human scores may not mean the same thing.

Alona Strugatski, Licol Zeinfeld and Giora Alexandron compared human response structures with 120 response sets from six multimodal models on two instruments: a 22-item high-school chemistry diagnostic answered by 931 students, and a 20-item quantitative-reasoning entrance-exam section answered by more than 4,800 people. They used exploratory factor analysis and repeated matched resampling. Across factor choices, human-to-human structural similarity was consistently higher than model-to-human similarity: averaged results were 0.595 versus 0.513 in chemistry and 0.535 versus 0.449 in quantitative reasoning, with all comparisons significant at p < .001 (Strugatski, Zeinfeld and Alexandron, submitted 16 August 2026).

The paper covers two instruments and six model versions, so it does not abolish benchmarking. It does puncture the anthropomorphic reading of a score. A model may reach the same answer through pattern access, visual cues or failure modes organised differently from a learner's knowledge. “The AI got 92%” therefore tells us what it can output under those conditions, not that it possesses 92%-worth of the construct the test was built to measure. For schools, that cuts both ways: do not call a model competent because it passes a student test, and do not repair student assessment merely by finding questions the model currently fails. Valid evidence begins with the human capability we intend to see.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 16 August 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 16 August 2026 · 1,292 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

Two clocks, running at wildly different speeds. On Thursday Google shipped Gemini 3.7 Flash, its newest Flash model, built for coding and multi-step agent work and offered at half price through the end of the year. It arrived three weeks to the day after 3.6 Flash and rolled straight into Spark (9to5Google, 13 August 2026). That is the cadence the supply side keeps: a materially better model, cheaper, every few weeks.

The demand side keeps a different calendar. The week that produced a new frontier model was, for American schools, the first week of term — and the governance layer only now became visible. Ohio's requirement that every K–12 school hold an AI policy is in force as classes resume, framed around two things: keeping student data safe, and "opening up the instructional playbook" (Spectrum News 1, 14 August 2026). In South Carolina, Charleston County launched a districtwide policy on a "Safe, Ethical, Effective" framework, with AI literacy from the early grades and a deliberate refusal to ban — "the question is no longer whether students and educators will interact with artificial intelligence, but how" (Live5News, 11 August 2026). The tools under those policies will have changed twice before the ink is dry. A school writing a rulebook this August is aiming at a target that moves on a three-week cycle — and that mismatch, not any single product, is the week's real story.

What Does It All Mean?

The tempting frame is tools versus no-tools. The truer one is adoption versus governance, and by now the gap is measured, not guessed. Adoption did not sneak up on anyone — all three frontier labs now court teachers directly (Anthropic's free Claude for Teachers arrived in July), and this summer an entire US state put its roughly 680,000 pupils on one vendor's assistant (Google, 2026). The Digital Education Council's global survey — 45,398 responses across 35 countries — puts student AI use at 88% and faculty use at 77%, up sixteen points in a year, while 57% of students say their assessments come with inadequate AI guidance and only 29% think their instructors are equipped to give it (EdTech Innovation Hub, 9 July 2026). Use is nearly universal; the scaffolding around it is not. The instinct, understandably, is to close the gap with a tool of one's own: a detector.

That instinct is not merely failing; it is actively unjust, and the evidence has sat in plain sight for three years. Stanford's Liang and colleagues showed that GPT detectors misclassify more than half of TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers as AI-generated, while scoring near-perfect on essays by US eighth-graders — because non-native prose has lower "perplexity," the very signal detectors read as machine writing (Liang et al., 2023). In any system where many students write, think and are assessed in a second language, a detector is not a neutral integrity check. It is a bias engine pointed at precisely the students it should protect. That is why higher education spent this summer switching the detectors off (Inside Higher Ed, 5 August 2026).

So the honest conclusion is uncomfortable. Governance that lasts cannot be a product — the detector proved that — and it cannot be a document either, because the model beneath the policy is replaced every three weeks while the policy is revised, at best, once a year. What survives contact with that cadence is not a tool or a PDF but a design decision, made inside a subject, about one assignment: what a student may use, what they must disclose, and how the thinking is made visible when the machine is switched off. That is slow and it does not scale by memo. It is also the only layer a new model release cannot silently obsolete.

Behind You! Behind You!

The price is the hook. Gemini 3.7 Flash is half-price only through 31 December (9to5Google, 13 August 2026); loss-leader economics build the dependency before the invoice arrives. The line to watch is not this term's pilot but next year's budget, when the introductory rate lapses and the workflow is already load-bearing.

The frontier is moving from answers to agents. The new model's selling point is not a smarter chatbot but one that "thinks more diligently, putting in more effort into multi-step planning and tool calls" (9to5Google) — software that plans and executes whole tasks, not single replies. The academic-integrity question schools are still writing rules for — did a student use a chatbot? — is already a generation behind the capability: did an agent do the assignment?

The mandate is spreading, and with it the risk of the checkbox. Ohio has made a policy compulsory (Spectrum News 1); other states are following. A requirement to possess a policy is easy to satisfy and easy to mistake for the work. Watch whether "we have an AI policy" starts standing in for "we changed how we assess."

The Riff Returns But Slower

Detection, again — but this week the argument reached its end. Last Sunday this brief noted a student-authored framework whose sharpest clause was that no detector score should stand as a verdict. Seven days on, the sector has gone further and simply retired the detectors (Inside Higher Ed, 5 August 2026). The detection era is ending not because schools won an arms race but because they lost it — and the more honest institutions admitted the weapon never worked, and had been quietly punishing the wrong students while it pretended to.

What replaces it is the same conclusion the week's research kept reaching from other directions: you cannot catch your way to integrity, you have to design for it. The feedback studies land here — uptake appears only when a student has to act on a comment, not merely receive it; so does the Socratic-tutor work, where students wait for the next step even when the tutor is built to withhold it. Different laboratories, one finding: learning becomes visible only when the task forces the student to do something the machine cannot do for them. That is not a detector setting. It is assignment design, subject by subject, and it is the unglamorous work that outlasts every model release. Same tune as always — just played slowly enough, this week, to hear the whole thing.

Sceptic's Corner

The comforting new orthodoxy writes itself: detectors are dead, "assessment redesign" is the answer, and a state mandate proves the grown-ups are handling it. Each part is shakier than it sounds.

Redesign is being adopted from exhaustion, not evidence. Inside Higher Ed's own reporting finds about half of faculty want to change as little as possible, and that "AI-proof" assessment is realistic mainly for small, in-person classes (Inside Higher Ed, 5 August 2026). "AI-proof" is itself a slogan that can curdle into theatre: an oral defence or a handwritten exam can relocate the trust problem rather than solve it, and can penalise the anxious and the disabled while it does. Retiring a bad tool is not the same as building a good practice.

And the mandate proves less than it claims. A rule that every school "have a policy" (Spectrum News 1, 14 August 2026) measures the existence of a document, not a changed decision in a classroom. Charleston's framework and its tool pilot are done in good faith (Live5News, 11 August 2026) — but a framework and a purchased platform are the cheapest artefacts in this whole system to produce, and the least likely, on their own, to change what a fifteen-year-old is actually asked to do on Monday. The refrain does not change because the news does: show me the redesigned assignment, not the retired detector, the adopted policy, or the newest, cheaper model.
