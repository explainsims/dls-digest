Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Friday, 20 November 2026

Top story

An AI maths tutor helped adults practise fixing their own mistakes.

In a 15 November Carnegie Mellon preprint, Michael Asher, Meng Cao and Paulo Carvalho randomly assigned 400 adults who had missed a screening question on setting up a constraint equation to one of four kinds of feedback. After a wrong answer, they saw the correct answer, got a generic explanation, got a personalised AI explanation, or worked through the error with a tutor that asked follow-up questions. In the last group, participants corrected 131 of 206 errors during the dialogue. On post-test questions aligned with the skills they had practised, that group scored nine percentage points higher (0.24 standard deviations, p = .048) than the average of the two explanation groups. The researchers found no reliable advantage on the other post-test questions. The coached practice also took about 13 minutes, against about seven for the other conditions.

A 13 November experiment in the British Journal of Educational Technology put a different kind of AI support in front of 16- to 18-year-olds at an English sixth-form college. Megan Ennion and Ros McLellan analysed 149 students assigned to Khanmigo Lite, an in-person maths tutor or unguided web search for deliberately above-curriculum problems. In the second set of problems, the AI group reported more effort and greater difficulty than the human-tutored group. The human-tutored group attempted the most questions in both sets, with a statistically significant gap versus AI in the first set only. The authors treat the section-level findings as exploratory. An attempt counted when a student started a problem, so this study tells us about engagement with difficulty and leaves learning gains unmeasured.

For international schools, the two papers give a maths department a sharper question than whether to permit an AI tutor. Does the activity require a first attempt, help the learner locate a specific error and get them to try again? A polished explanation can sit in the chat while the student moves on. A corrected line of working followed by a fresh problem gives the teacher something they can actually inspect.

Other stories

  • Brightspace made teacher instructions take precedence in AI feedback. Minnesota State scheduled the 20.26.11 Brightspace update for 19 November. In the release notes, with the Lumi Feedback changes added on 5 November, D2L says teacher-level instructions now take priority over administrator-level instructions in its paid feedback add-on. Automated assessment uses its own custom instructions. Regenerating feedback builds on the earlier output and leaves the grade in place unless the submission or rubric changes. The same release adds support for HEIC and HEIF submissions, a mundane change with an audience in any school where students photograph their working on Apple devices. The hierarchy is the bigger story. A school-wide default and a rule that teachers cannot override are two distinct product capabilities. Separate settings for feedback and automated assessment also mean a single test cannot tell you how both behave. A chemistry teacher may need feedback that reflects the discipline and leaves room for a second attempt. A school may also need a rule that stops feedback from disclosing a model answer. For Ed Tech Coaches working across learning platforms, this is a concrete scenario for an LMS walkthrough. A demo with all the instructions pointing in the same direction will never show who wins when they conflict.

  • The EU put the curriculum and the assessment on the same AI agenda. On 19 November the Council of the EU approved conclusions on AI skills and competences. The adopted text invites member states to draw on existing resources, including DigComp 3.0 and the primary and secondary AI literacy framework, when updating curricula, learning programmes and assessment. It couples that work with initial and continuing teacher education, age-appropriate whole-school policy and evaluation of effects on existing inequalities. It also asks the Commission to build on the school-age framework for other stages of life. The assessment language matters because the same syllabus can require confident use of a tool in one task and reliable independent performance in another. The tension worth taking into a curriculum meeting is between practising with AI and seeing what students can do independently. A class can reasonably have students ask an assistant to critique a claim, then ask them to explain which criticism they accepted and why under conditions that make their own reasoning visible. Buying the same assistant across departments cannot decide where each of those assessments belongs.

  • Nigeria put a readiness check before the AI deployment. Nigeria's communications ministry reported the unveiling of the national EdTech strategy on 17 November. The same launch introduced AskFME, a public information chatbot, and a public portal for the national education data infrastructure. The strategy's AI governance annex sets out proportionate checks on infrastructure, teacher capacity, data, governance and technical support. For AI-enabled products, it sets out an assurance path from establishing an educational need through impact and privacy assessments, a pilot and independent evaluation before scaling. It also keeps human judgement in high-impact decisions, including learner assessment and disciplinary action, with routes to challenge and correct AI-influenced decisions. The strategy explicitly gives a literacy activity or controlled pilot a different readiness threshold from an advanced service that relies on substantial data. That's a sensible distinction for an international school. A lesson on how training examples influence a classifier can run without feeding a system anything about the students in the room. A tool recommending who needs intervention is a much heavier proposition. Putting both through one generic AI approval form would conceal most of the work attached to the second.

  • Anthropic's first trade put a price on expectations. Anthropic priced 980 million shares at US$83 each on 18 November. It was selling about 956 million of those itself, which implies roughly US$79.4 billion gross to the company at the offer price, before costs and assuming completion. The offering was scheduled to close on 20 November subject to the customary conditions. Nasdaq reported that shares began trading on 19 November and opened at US$111.50. The numbers describe expectations about the company as a whole. For a school buying a service built on Claude, the more interesting bit of paper is the contract with the service provider. What happens to a year of teacher-created materials if the quota changes, the vendor swaps models or the school moves to another product? Two classroom products can call the same model and offer very different answers. A usable export and a commitment on price and access for the term tell procurement more about continuity than the first trade does.

On the radar

  • Sorsogon has a December AI literacy activity for Grades 4–12. A 19 November memo from the Philippine Department of Education's Sorsogon division relays a voluntary DEVCON Kids programme for the 7–18 December end-of-term block. The facilitator plan offers pair work and a single-projector option. One exercise has learners train a classifier to distinguish fish from rubbish, then examine mistakes. A 19 November UNESCO Santiago teacher workshop was scheduled around the same activity, with adaptations for different subjects and limited connectivity. The thing to look for in December is what learners can explain about a misclassification after the exercise. A completed activity and an explanation of the model's error tell a teacher different things.

  • The EU's child-safety proposal is due for its first ministerial policy debate. The Council's 16 November background brief lays out the draft EU KIDS Act ahead of the 20 November agenda. The draft includes safety-by-design duties for AI chatbots and companions as well as other services. Its staged account ages of 13 and 15 concern social media and video-sharing platforms. The Irish presidency is due to present a progress report, and negotiations are ongoing. For schools comparing the same product on a managed account and a home account, watch which protections attach to the service itself and which depend on how a young user signs in.

Sceptic's corner

The tempting inference from the Carnegie Mellon result is that an AI tutor becomes effective once it learns to ask questions. Look at what was bundled into that condition. The adults got additional attempts, prompts tied to their immediate errors and nearly twice the practice time. The comparison groups got feedback and moved on to the next problem. In the tutoring group, the system also had a stopping rule. If the learner still had no correct equation after six tutor replies, it provided and explained the answer. In the authors' results, the advantage appeared on questions closely related to what the adults had practised, and personalising an explanation by itself did not reliably improve performance. The study supports the package of guided correction as tested. It leaves open how much of the gain a teacher could reproduce with a pencil, a carefully chosen hint and six more minutes.

There is a straightforward way to see the difference at school. Give both groups the same time and a plausible way to get help. Let one revise with a prepared hint and the other use the tutor. Watch whether they can identify what went wrong and fix it. Give both groups a new problem without help a few days later, including an item that needs the same idea in a different setting. The busy chat, the quick fix and the delayed answer each report something different. The first two can tell a teacher where to look. The last is where a claim about learning has to earn its keep.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 15 November 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 15 November 2026 · 1,387 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

A week of rulebooks. South Korea confirmed four cases of cheating with AI glasses in school assessments (Ministry of Education, 9 November). Stanford's SCALE review grew from 20 causal papers to 52, and its verdict stayed conditional (SCALE, 10 November). England opened a consultation on safety certification for edtech and AI (DfE, 10 November). Brazil approved a framework that rules out AI grading of interpretive work and sanctions based only on a detector (Secom, 10 November). Google shipped skills, a "Wait until" step and standalone canvas links.

Since Friday's brief: NPR asked why Google won't let schools switch off AI in Search, and put a former Google education lead's antitrust theory on the record (NPR, 13 November). It frames the centrepiece. On Saturday, The 74 carried Chalkbeat's account of Wednesday's Chicago rally; it leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

Google's position on Search is now plain. AI Overview and AI Mode are "deeply integrated" into it, so a school cannot keep Search and switch them off (New York Times via The Star, 10 November). Senator Ed Markey's 9 November letter calls that "an ultimatum to effectively force schools to enable generative AI" (same report). So the Sunday question: if a school cannot remove the AI box from the results page, what is left for it to govern?

First, why the box won't come off. Tracy Pizzo, who spent more than a decade at Google, much of it on education, told NPR the answer may lie in court rather than in classrooms. In September, Judge Amit Mehta dismissed publishers' antitrust suits; Penske Media's tying claim failed because Search and AI Overviews were not plausibly separate products; reports quote the ruling's phrase "an integrated search experience" (MIXED, 4 October). A schools-only off switch, Pizzo argued, would be evidence that the two can be separated. The antitrust experts NPR asked were not entirely convinced, and Google did not answer (NPR, 13 November). Treat it as a theory. It does explain why "off by default" stops at the Search bar.

Second, what the box does to reading. Pew tracked the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025. When an AI summary appeared, they clicked a traditional result on 8% of visits, against 15% without one, and clicked a link inside the summary on 1% (Pew Research Center, 22 July 2025). Common Sense Media found that "AI Mode completed 100% of the homework assignments we gave it", and that neither feature can be switched off by parents or schools (Common Sense Media, July 2026).

Third, what schools have been trying to teach. The Stanford History Education Group found that professional fact-checkers beat historians and undergraduates at judging websites because they read laterally: they leave the page and check the source elsewhere. In a randomised trial across six high schools, six 50-minute lessons made students twice as likely to spot questionable sites, though they still earned only half the available points (Stanford GSE, 19 April 2022). Google.org funded the study.

Put those together. The habit information literacy builds is leaving the page. The interface that now greets every query is built so that most people don't.

For an international school on Google, that moves the question from the admin console to the task. Whatever a school decides about Gemini for students, every student who searches will meet an Overview. A school's AI-use scale tells students what they may use; it cannot treat Search as an AI-free zone. A research task can ask for the move the Overview skips: name two sources outside it, say what each adds, and mark where the summary was wrong or thin. A ninth-grader who can do that has a skill the box cannot take away.

Behind You! Behind You!

Chicago took the argument to Google's door. About 30 people, mostly parents and educators, rallied outside Google's West Loop office on Wednesday, alongside a protest in Cambridge, Massachusetts (Chalkbeat via The 74, 14 November). Chicago Public Schools, which has blocked student access to generative AI tools, said it is "actively exploring all options to independently suppress" the Overview, and failing that will look at other search tools. Watch whether a district that size actually leaves.

Anthropic signed the teachers' unions' privacy standard. The AFT and its New York affiliate, the UFT, announced on 10 November a legally binding agreement covering Claude for Teachers and Anthropic's existing district agreements (K-12 Dive, 10 November). Data may be used only for authorised educational purposes, and schools control how long it is kept and when it is deleted. Microsoft's equivalent took effect on 1 November. A school outside the unions' reach can still ask any vendor to match those terms in writing.

Ho Chi Minh City began planning its AI periods. The city's Department of Education and Training held a workshop on 12 November at Le Hong Phong High School for the Gifted on bringing the national AI education framework into secondary schools this year: 12 periods per class per school year, as topics or woven into subjects (SGGP, 12 November). The bottlenecks named included teacher capacity, funding, student data safety and formalistic assessment. Vietnam's largest city is working the same problems as the international schools around it, on a national timetable.

Gemini in Gmail reached every adult Workspace account, on by default. From 10 November, Google is extending thread summaries and non-personalised Help me write to "all Workspace users 18 years and older" (Google, 10 November). The features are on by default; users can disable them and admins can switch them off. The post does not name Education editions. For a school, that means every staff inbox and any student account set as adult. The decision is better made before the habit forms.

  • Overdue: Vault API support for Gemini exports, due within 14 days of the 22 October rollout; still no update note (Google, 22 October).
  • Slow burn, missed last Sunday: Under a plan reported after Australia's education ministers met, primary students would be blocked from generative AI in classrooms and secondary students restricted from using it in assessments; the ministers agreed a Digital Detox Taskforce to advise government by mid-2027 (CathNews, 5 November). No start date yet. The assessment half is the one to follow.

Sceptic's Corner

The protest slogan wants Search without the AI. The strongest case against it is that the old results page was never the gateway campaigners remember.

In clickstream data that mostly predates AI Overviews, SparkToro and Datos found that "58.5% of American Google searches resulted in zero clicks", and that for every 1,000 US searches just 360 clicks reached a site Google doesn't own (SparkToro, 2 July 2024). Answer boxes, panels and the habit of stopping at the top all came before the chatbot. The Overview made an old pattern fluent.

That doesn't make the Overview harmless. A box that writes the homework on request is a fair thing to want gone for younger students, and Common Sense's evidence gives campaigners a real case. But switching it off would not restore a habit of checking sources that most users never had. Worse, the fight over the switch lets everyone treat the problem as solved once the switch is found.

The Stanford trial is the better guide. Six lessons doubled students' ability to spot a dubious site and still left them on half marks. That is the work, with or without the box.

The line for the staffroom: the AI box isn't the first time Google answered for students; it's the first time it answered in paragraphs. Teach them to leave the page.
