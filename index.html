Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Thursday, 19 November 2026

Top story

A large phone-pouch study found a small learning gain.

Harvard's Center for Education Policy Research announced the findings on 17 November. The researchers followed about 3.3 million U.S. students at roughly 4,600 schools between 2021–22 and 2025–26, using fall-to-spring NWEA MAP Growth scores and comparing schools that introduced Yondr pouches with similar schools. The main estimate, pooling maths and reading, was 0.0087 standard deviations of extra annual growth, or about 0.01. The authors translate the magnitude into the equivalent of one to two weeks of instruction in a year. They calculated that comparison from typical test-score growth. The study measured test scores and pouch adoption, so the weeks figure tells readers the scale of the effect, in familiar units. Research report, including Table 2.

The study's stronger comparison is at school level. The researchers looked for differences between adopting and comparison schools before the rules changed and found no clear difference in their earlier growth trends. Schools still chose whether to adopt. The estimate is less precise when looking at either subject on its own, and the authors had relatively few schools with a long history of using pouches. Their separate analysis of statewide restrictions produced mixed results and the groups were already on different trajectories, which makes those estimates harder to interpret.

For international schools, the distinction worth carrying into a phone discussion is between the written rule and what happens between classes. A school can have a strict policy on paper and still leave teachers negotiating it six times a day. A school with an effective routine might already have much of the benefit available locally. Start with the outcome the school has reason to care about, such as interruptions during reading or the time lost getting a lesson underway. Then ask what a pouch would change about that routine and who would be doing the extra work.

Other stories

  • Gemini Notebook turns source material into interactive sites. Google's 18 November Workspace update says the web rollout started on 16 November, can take up to 15 days across both Workspace release tracks and is limited to users aged 18 and older. Administrators can control access to the Notebook service, with no separate control for generating sites. The sharing instructions give the public-link route to consumer-account owners and editors, and say link viewers need access to the full notebook. Google documents a separate download route. On shared sites, each user can see their own inputs, including when the user is the owner, so a checklist completed by several people would yield separate views. A teacher could generate an explorable history timeline from public sources and check whether the dates, labels and controls survive the move from notebook to site. For Ed Tech Coaches, seeing it work in the author's browser answers only the first question. Before promising a class resource, check who can actually open it and what else that access would give them.

  • Scotland gives teachers time to work through its draft curriculum. In a 17 November letter to the Scottish Parliament, Education Secretary Màiri McAllan said two extra in-service days had been agreed for 2026–27 to support the curriculum revision. Local authorities must seek an exemption to arrange the closures, and the letter acknowledges the cost to families. A sample of practitioners is testing draft materials, with an advanced draft scheduled for January 2027, final materials in spring and implementation in August 2028. That sequence gives teachers something concrete to do with the time. Put actual lessons and pieces of pupil work alongside the draft and see where the proposed sequence diverges from what a department currently teaches and assesses. An international school changing its curriculum or assessment reporting can borrow the sequence even if it cannot borrow the two days. It is far cheaper to hear that three teams interpret a standard differently while the document is open for revision than after families have seen three incompatible reports.

  • A science museum puts careers, families and AI in the same programme. The New York Hall of Science's 17 November announcement scheduled a 50-student, three-hour mentored hackathon with Playlab for the 18 November public launch of Exploring AI Together. An evening programme was designed for 14- to 24-year-olds and their educators and caregivers. The museum's family advisory committee was involved before the launch, with an October session about how AI affects decisions in fields such as housing and transport. Its research team plans to evaluate the initiative. The useful design choice is that adult participants get questions of their own. In a school careers event, pupils might work out what information a system would need to suggest an opportunity, while a parent or visiting professional asks how it would recognise missing evidence or handle an exception. Both can compare the suggestion with a real listing or policy. A successful session would leave the family with a way to interrogate the next recommendation they encounter, even after the prototype is forgotten.

  • America's national assessment makes the hardware contingency explicit. A 17 November U.S. Department of Education notice seeks comment on the proposed data collection for the 2028 National Assessment of Educational Progress through 19 January 2027. The plan includes multistage testing studies in reading and maths at grades 4 and 8. It envisages testing on school devices, with Chromebooks provided to public or private schools whose devices fall below the minimum specification. The assessment reports results for populations and groups, with no individual student or school scores. For schools planning their own device-based assessment, the fallback is the instructive part. A backup device that merely opens the test can still alter a pupil's experience if the keyboard, text size or navigation differs. Rehearsing an ordinary device change before test day makes the handoff visible. Try a sample task all the way through on the spare with the accommodations that a pupil normally uses, then check what happens to work already entered. The aim is for a hardware problem to stay a hardware problem.

  • An Erasmus+ funding call asks school partnerships to show what travels. The European Commission's 18 November call announcement gives a 2027 budget of over €5.3 billion across education, training, youth and sport. The budget also supports the new package's priorities on basic skills, teachers and digital tools including AI. The school development partnerships scheme is one specific part, with €400,000 per selected 36-month project. Its criteria ask for a baseline and measurable learning targets in a basic skill, plus plans for other schools to use successful approaches. A school outside the programme can still take something from that spec. In a cross-school reading project, for example, look for a response both partners can assess and agree what counts as improvement. Then have the receiving school try the materials without a visiting specialist to explain them. That handover says more about whether a practice can travel than a polished account written by the team that invented it.

On the radar

  • An inclusion pilot will make some forms of disclosure optional. In a 16 November preview, Montgomery County Public Schools in Maryland scheduled a 19 November unveiling for a voluntary initiative supporting students with disabilities that may be hard for others to see. Selected groups are due to start in early 2027, with educational resources, self-advocacy opportunities and optional identification tools. The details to watch are where the identification would be used, who would see it and how a student who chooses to keep a disability private can get the same support. A good test of the eventual design is what happens when a student asks for help without using the tool.

  • A school partnership needs a task that works across timetables. The British Council's 19 November teacher session, with Leeds City of Languages, puts collaborative school projects on the International Education Week programme. The promising signal would be a worked example in which pupils respond to one another's ideas and teachers can reuse the sequence. A live call can be memorable, but pupils at either end also need time to prepare and reply, especially across time zones or languages. A question recorded by one class, answered with local evidence by another, and followed up after both have had time to listen is a small enough exchange to repeat.

Sceptic's corner

The Harvard release's historical claim deserves more scrutiny than its estimate of pouch effects. It suggests that phones in school may explain about half the annual slowdown in U.S. achievement growth after 2013. The arithmetic compares the size of a recent estimate for schools adopting lockable pouches with the size of a longer national decline. That comparison makes a small number intelligible. Establishing how much of a national trend phones caused would take evidence about that earlier period, including how phone use changed and what else changed alongside it. Harvard release.

The earlier U.S. pouch study, revised in September, found average test score effects close to zero, with modest positive estimates for high school, especially in maths. The new report's discussion says the uncertainty intervals overlap and points out that one study measured score levels while the other measured within-year growth. Both studies leave room for small academic benefits. The precision suggested by an annual promise of recovered weeks goes further than either can deliver for a particular school.

A school that wants fewer phone interruptions already has an outcome it can inspect. It can ask whether teachers spend less time intervening, whether students settle into sustained work and whether pupils with approved access can use it without being singled out. Those questions can return different answers, and each has a different remedy. Protect the quiet needed for a lesson and judge the policy first on whether it makes that quiet easier to sustain.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 15 November 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 15 November 2026 · 1,387 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

A week of rulebooks. South Korea confirmed four cases of cheating with AI glasses in school assessments (Ministry of Education, 9 November). Stanford's SCALE review grew from 20 causal papers to 52, and its verdict stayed conditional (SCALE, 10 November). England opened a consultation on safety certification for edtech and AI (DfE, 10 November). Brazil approved a framework that rules out AI grading of interpretive work and sanctions based only on a detector (Secom, 10 November). Google shipped skills, a "Wait until" step and standalone canvas links.

Since Friday's brief: NPR asked why Google won't let schools switch off AI in Search, and put a former Google education lead's antitrust theory on the record (NPR, 13 November). It frames the centrepiece. On Saturday, The 74 carried Chalkbeat's account of Wednesday's Chicago rally; it leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

Google's position on Search is now plain. AI Overview and AI Mode are "deeply integrated" into it, so a school cannot keep Search and switch them off (New York Times via The Star, 10 November). Senator Ed Markey's 9 November letter calls that "an ultimatum to effectively force schools to enable generative AI" (same report). So the Sunday question: if a school cannot remove the AI box from the results page, what is left for it to govern?

First, why the box won't come off. Tracy Pizzo, who spent more than a decade at Google, much of it on education, told NPR the answer may lie in court rather than in classrooms. In September, Judge Amit Mehta dismissed publishers' antitrust suits; Penske Media's tying claim failed because Search and AI Overviews were not plausibly separate products; reports quote the ruling's phrase "an integrated search experience" (MIXED, 4 October). A schools-only off switch, Pizzo argued, would be evidence that the two can be separated. The antitrust experts NPR asked were not entirely convinced, and Google did not answer (NPR, 13 November). Treat it as a theory. It does explain why "off by default" stops at the Search bar.

Second, what the box does to reading. Pew tracked the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025. When an AI summary appeared, they clicked a traditional result on 8% of visits, against 15% without one, and clicked a link inside the summary on 1% (Pew Research Center, 22 July 2025). Common Sense Media found that "AI Mode completed 100% of the homework assignments we gave it", and that neither feature can be switched off by parents or schools (Common Sense Media, July 2026).

Third, what schools have been trying to teach. The Stanford History Education Group found that professional fact-checkers beat historians and undergraduates at judging websites because they read laterally: they leave the page and check the source elsewhere. In a randomised trial across six high schools, six 50-minute lessons made students twice as likely to spot questionable sites, though they still earned only half the available points (Stanford GSE, 19 April 2022). Google.org funded the study.

Put those together. The habit information literacy builds is leaving the page. The interface that now greets every query is built so that most people don't.

For an international school on Google, that moves the question from the admin console to the task. Whatever a school decides about Gemini for students, every student who searches will meet an Overview. A school's AI-use scale tells students what they may use; it cannot treat Search as an AI-free zone. A research task can ask for the move the Overview skips: name two sources outside it, say what each adds, and mark where the summary was wrong or thin. A ninth-grader who can do that has a skill the box cannot take away.

Behind You! Behind You!

Chicago took the argument to Google's door. About 30 people, mostly parents and educators, rallied outside Google's West Loop office on Wednesday, alongside a protest in Cambridge, Massachusetts (Chalkbeat via The 74, 14 November). Chicago Public Schools, which has blocked student access to generative AI tools, said it is "actively exploring all options to independently suppress" the Overview, and failing that will look at other search tools. Watch whether a district that size actually leaves.

Anthropic signed the teachers' unions' privacy standard. The AFT and its New York affiliate, the UFT, announced on 10 November a legally binding agreement covering Claude for Teachers and Anthropic's existing district agreements (K-12 Dive, 10 November). Data may be used only for authorised educational purposes, and schools control how long it is kept and when it is deleted. Microsoft's equivalent took effect on 1 November. A school outside the unions' reach can still ask any vendor to match those terms in writing.

Ho Chi Minh City began planning its AI periods. The city's Department of Education and Training held a workshop on 12 November at Le Hong Phong High School for the Gifted on bringing the national AI education framework into secondary schools this year: 12 periods per class per school year, as topics or woven into subjects (SGGP, 12 November). The bottlenecks named included teacher capacity, funding, student data safety and formalistic assessment. Vietnam's largest city is working the same problems as the international schools around it, on a national timetable.

Gemini in Gmail reached every adult Workspace account, on by default. From 10 November, Google is extending thread summaries and non-personalised Help me write to "all Workspace users 18 years and older" (Google, 10 November). The features are on by default; users can disable them and admins can switch them off. The post does not name Education editions. For a school, that means every staff inbox and any student account set as adult. The decision is better made before the habit forms.

  • Overdue: Vault API support for Gemini exports, due within 14 days of the 22 October rollout; still no update note (Google, 22 October).
  • Slow burn, missed last Sunday: Under a plan reported after Australia's education ministers met, primary students would be blocked from generative AI in classrooms and secondary students restricted from using it in assessments; the ministers agreed a Digital Detox Taskforce to advise government by mid-2027 (CathNews, 5 November). No start date yet. The assessment half is the one to follow.

Sceptic's Corner

The protest slogan wants Search without the AI. The strongest case against it is that the old results page was never the gateway campaigners remember.

In clickstream data that mostly predates AI Overviews, SparkToro and Datos found that "58.5% of American Google searches resulted in zero clicks", and that for every 1,000 US searches just 360 clicks reached a site Google doesn't own (SparkToro, 2 July 2024). Answer boxes, panels and the habit of stopping at the top all came before the chatbot. The Overview made an old pattern fluent.

That doesn't make the Overview harmless. A box that writes the homework on request is a fair thing to want gone for younger students, and Common Sense's evidence gives campaigners a real case. But switching it off would not restore a habit of checking sources that most users never had. Worse, the fight over the switch lets everyone treat the problem as solved once the switch is found.

The Stanford trial is the better guide. Six lessons doubled students' ability to spot a dubious site and still left them on half marks. That is the work, with or without the box.

The line for the staffroom: the AI box isn't the first time Google answered for students; it's the first time it answered in paragraphs. Teach them to leave the page.
