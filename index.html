Filed Sunday, 10 May 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 10 May 2026

The LMS conversation moved from features to governance posture in five working days, and the field will act on it — on the field’s clock, not the news cycle’s.

The Week That Was

The week’s centre of gravity was Instructure. Three breaches in eight months — the third after the public crisis-response patches, which is the worst possible signal a vendor can send mid-contract-cycle. Leak deadline shifted from 8 May to 12 May; school login pages got defaced; Canvas spent half a day offline during finals at a long list of US universities.

The other half of the story was the AI-inside-suite race converging — Google’s free Gemini 3 Pro for educators, Microsoft’s Teach module global, Anthropic’s Microsoft 365 add-ins, Toddle’s five AI surfaces. BETT week is doing its usual job of making everyone’s roadmap look symmetrical.

Since Friday’s brief: quiet weekend on the breach. Canvas fully restored by 8 May; no new leak yet, the deadline is Tuesday. No new framework releases either. Sunday, 10 May: the silence is itself the news — leak or no leak, Tuesday is the read-out.

What Does It All Mean?

The week’s events all pointed at a procurement-posture pivot. Is the field actually going to act, or will inertia and switching-cost win?

The honest precedent is PowerSchool, December 2024. Sixty-two million students’ data — names, addresses, social security numbers, medical info — taken in a worst-case-scenario breach. Then the attackers came back and extorted individual districts after PowerSchool had paid. EdWeek called it “close to a worst-case scenario.” Class-actions, state AG suits, a $17.25M Naviance settlement, a Bain Capital ratification ruling kept alive in SDCal in March.

And the market-share number eighteen months later? PowerSchool retains roughly 75% of the K-12 SIS market. (Civic IQ, January 2026) Districts kept renewing through 2025. Most demanded “enhanced security provisions” in the new contracts, but they signed. One state moved to Infinite Campus citing cybersecurity; many didn’t.

That’s the historical baseline. A breach that leaked SSNs and medical data on 62 million minors shifted procurement language a notch and barely budged market share. The Canvas breach leaks names, school emails, IDs and message contents — serious, but a less explosive payload. If PowerSchool is the comparator, the realistic 18-month shape is renewals get harder, RFPs get won less often, price gives, not Canvas collapses.

Two reasons this cycle could be different. First, three breaches in eight months, with the third post-remediation, is structurally not the same as PowerSchool’s one-incident-then-extortion-tail. PowerSchool’s failure was a credentials-and-monitoring story. Instructure’s is now a remediation-cycle story — they patched, the attackers walked back in, the patches didn’t hold. That’s a harder thing to recover from in front of a committee that knows how to read a pen-test report.

Second, the Bain Capital ruling. In re PowerSchool, March 2026 in SDCal — Bain stayed in on a “ratified and conditioned cost-cuts” theory. Plausibility, not merits — but a survivable plaintiff theory pointed at the disclaimer-of-control template that protects PE-owned vendors. Instructure is KKR-owned; before that, Thoma Bravo. The same shape is in the room for any future Instructure litigation. (Womble Bond Dickinson)

The bigger LMS history is worth holding. Anthology — Blackboard’s owner — declared bankruptcy late last year. Canvas overtook Blackboard in 2019; Anthology Blackboard now sits at 12% of US/Canada higher-ed enrolment share, with Canvas at 50%. (On EdTech) That migration took roughly a decade, and it happened without a Blackboard breach event — it was about feature-set, UX, and faculty preference. Switching costs are real, and so is the time-base of LMS migration.

The honest answer: the field will act on it, but on the field’s clock, not the news cycle’s. RFP rubrics get rewritten first — most current rubrics treat security as yes/no checkboxes that produce the same answer for one contained incident and three incidents in eight months including one post-remediation. That’s the gap that closes first. Renewal conversations will be harder for Instructure through the next two cycles. Procurement language changes inside an 18-month window; market share moves on a 5-to-10-year clock. The PowerSchool data is the floor. The remediation-failure pattern is what could pull this case off the floor — but in quarters, not weeks.

Most ed-tech press treats it as a footnote, but the migration shape that follows a vendor going bankrupt isn’t the shape that follows a vendor having three breaches — and international schools with Blackboard contracts may need to make a 12-month call faster than the Canvas-Toddle conversation does. Sceptic’s Corner

The “Canvas is finished” read overshoots; so does the “Toddle is the safe pick” read. PowerSchool’s 75% retention through a worse breach is the brake on the first. Toddle’s absence of disclosed breaches, paired with a 2,500-school footprint vs Canvas’s 9,000+, is the brake on the second — attack-surface math isn’t symmetrical, and the AISB parent-satisfaction floor (75%+ unsatisfied in one peer transition) is real friction that doesn’t show up in vendor decks. The procurement-honest framing: two different bets on what to optimise for — Canvas for swappability via LTI/MCP, Toddle for data isolation via dual-cloud-hidden architecture. The Sceptic’s question for either: what’s your plan for the day this vendor has its incident?

The AI-literacy-framework “answer” needs the Williamson/Watters/Narayanan-Kapoor caveat. Once AILit becomes the foundation of PISA 2029, schools teach to that test four years in advance — the Williamson critique, and the load-bearing objection in the field. A framework whose AI competencies are only skill-acquisition (prompt, evaluate, create) and not also critical orientation (whose interests does this AI serve, when does it function as snake oil, when should I refuse to use it) ages badly. Most candidate frameworks pass two of four. None pass all four cleanly.

The Bain ruling is plaintiff-theory survival, not precedent. Plausibility standard, not merits. PE-firm liability in portfolio-company breach cases is new legal weather, not settled law. Enough to shift rubric weighting. Not enough to disqualify by ownership type alone.

Sceptic’s corner

Two corrections to earlier framing — and one to the wider narrative.

First: Wednesday’s incident was called Instructure’s second breach in eight months. With yesterday’s re-entry, it’s the third, and the third happened after the patches went in. That changes the rhetorical weight of the procurement argument from “this vendor has a problem” to “this vendor’s remediation cycle did not work” — a different and harder thing for Instructure to recover from in the next earnings cycle.

Second: ShinyHunters’ “275 million users” claim still hasn’t been independently verified, and the 8,809-institution victim list has had publicly-disputed entries (some named institutions told local press they had no Canvas tenancy at all). Treat scale claims as ceiling, not floor; the practical risk to any individual student is determined by what the leak sample actually contains, which is still unknown.

Third — to the wider narrative: BETT week always produces a vendor-arms-race read. Google’s free Gemini 3 Pro for educators, Microsoft’s Teach module, Anthropic’s M365 deep-integration and Toddle’s AI Tutor Awards all landed within ten days of each other. The temptation is to read this as “the AI-in-LMS war is on” and rush into a stack decision. The honest read is the inverse: every major vendor now has roughly the same surface-area assistant baked into their own suite. The decision schools are actually making in 2026-27 is whose surface area sits closest to the gradebook, and that’s a procurement question, not an AI-feature question. The Canvas breach week happens to be highlighting exactly that — what’s at stake when the AI-inside-LMS gets the wrong vendor.

8 May 2026 · Scoping — the digital-fluency framework landscape: what’s out there, what holds up, which two or three to read first

Lede The “digital natives” line has been falsified for a decade in the literature, and it’s now collapsing in a second way: the student-teacher AI-tool gap isn’t a generational head-start, it’s a trajectory divergence. RAND 2025 — teachers 1.8× more likely than students to use ChatGPT weekly, but students lead in tutoring (64%) and college/career advice (49%); over 80% of US students say teachers haven’t taught them how to use AI for schoolwork. Schools that want to shape that trajectory rather than catch up to it need a digital-fluency framework underneath whatever AI policy they’re shipping. What it is Stage-1 scoping piece mapping three framework families: (1) established digital-fluency / digital-citizenship — ISTE Standards v4.02 (2024), DigComp 2.2 (2022), Common Sense K-12 Digital Citizenship Curriculum, Australia’s Digital Technologies; (2) AI-native frameworks — UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students (Sept 2024), OECD/EC AILit Framework (May 2025), AI4K12 Five Big Ideas, MIT RAISE, Stanford CRAFT; (3) academic / theoretical articulations — HDLF / AIEDF papers describing the layering architecture cleanly without being adoptable. National-level: Estonia, Singapore, Australia. Peer international schools have shipped almost nothing public; the 2025-26 EARCOS Global AI Readiness Study is the most promising regional signal. What schools could borrow The chassis-shape — AI as a layer on top of digital fluency, not parallel with it — is largely missing from the field. Common Sense’s stack and DigComp 2.2’s update method come closest in widely-adopted material; the academic HDLF/AIEDF paper articulates the architecture cleanly but isn’t adoptable. A school doing the layering work is doing original work, not picking it off a shelf. Two-to-three frameworks for an incoming pedagogy lead to read first: (1) OECD/EC AILit (May 2025) — longest shadow because PISA 2029 is being built on it, read with Williamson’s “performing AI literacy” critique in the other hand; (2) UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students (Sept 2024) — most internally coherent, leads with ethics and human-centredness; (3) ISTE v4.02 paired with Common Sense K-12 Digital Citizenship as the chassis under whichever AI-layer framework gets chosen. Honourable mentions: AI4K12 Five Big Ideas (if a CS strand anchors it); DigComp 2.2 (for the architectural pattern of AI-examples-inside-existing-frame). Sceptic Williamson’s performing AI literacy critique is the most serious objection in the field — once a competence is going to be tested at international scale (PISA 2029), schools “teach to the test” four years in advance, and the framework risks becoming “AI literacy-as-numbers.” Adoption-not-implementation is the canonical ISTE failure mode. The August 2026 Common Sense 9-12 refresh is the moment to re-read. Frameworks shape what gets taught, what gets assessed, and what gets defended at parent meetings; picking one without reading the field is how schools end up with a poster on the wall and a policy nobody operationalises.
