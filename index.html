Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Thursday, 30 July 2026

Top story

The largest school-based survey yet of how teenagers actually use AI found the dominant pattern is emotional, not academic.

The Jed Foundation published preliminary findings on 23 July from more than 5,500 middle and high school students across 21 US schools and districts, collected between October 2025 and April 2026. Four in five students had used some form of AI and more than half used it for schoolwork — but nearly three in five said they turned to AI when they felt sad, stressed or lonely, nearly a third used it for relationship or problem-solving advice, and more than one in ten used it for companionship. Students who used AI for emotional or mental-health support, problem-solving advice, during difficult emotions, or for companionship "were more likely to have poorer mental health, were more lonely, and had a greater history of suicidal thoughts and behaviors" (The Jed Foundation, 23 July 2026). JED's vice president of research and evaluation, Kayla Anderson, framed that as an association, not a cause (WJLA, 24 July 2026).

The demographic splits run in opposite directions: high school and rural students were more likely to use AI at all, while middle school and urban students were more likely to reach for it during difficult emotions — so the youngest cohort in the sample is the one using it closest to distress. And the sample is not a representative frame but schools already enrolled in JED's own programmes, with the full report not due until the autumn (EdTech Innovation Hub, 30 July 2026). Zainab Okolo, JED's senior vice president of policy, advocacy and government relations, drew the line at substitution, saying AI should not take the place of in-person interactions with peers and trusted adults (WJLA, 24 July 2026).

Almost every school AI policy in draft right now — including the district framework below — is an academic-integrity document governing what a student submits. This data says the modal adolescent interaction with a chatbot is not a homework shortcut but a private conversation at an hour when nobody else is available. The counselling pathway and the AI policy are being written by different people, in different rooms, and rarely read side by side.

Other stories

  • Fifteen US states now have chatbot-specific laws on the books, and the drafting is converging on minors. A Future of Privacy Forum tracker, updated weekly and confined to bills that have passed at least one chamber, counted 98 chatbot-specific bills across 34 states plus three congressional proposals as of July; fifteen states have signed legislation into law (Stacker, 24 July 2026). The provisions are unusually specific about age. Several states, including Colorado, Idaho, Iowa and Nebraska, require platforms to give parents tools to oversee or control a minor's account; Connecticut mandates an AI-disclosure reminder every hour for users under 18, against every three hours for adults. The same analysis cites KFF polling from March 2026: one in six US adults had used AI for mental health advice in the previous year, rising to 28% among 18-to-29-year-olds. Set against the JED figures, this is a legislature responding to a measured behaviour rather than a hypothetical one — and the specificity travels: a vendor selling into a US district now ships parental controls and session-level disclosure by default, a feature list any school anywhere can ask to have switched on.

  • A large Texas district wrote its AI framework by starting with where AI is not allowed. Katy ISD's new AI framework, presented to its board days before 28 July, bars generative AI chat tools — Google Gemini named among them — for elementary students, permits limited, supervised AI there for reading and personalised learning, and allows district-approved generative AI in high school with teacher permission; unreviewed platforms, and any falling outside federal education-law compliance, are excluded outright. Assistant superintendent Sanée Bell put it plainly: "Students will learn how to use AI...And also when it is and is not appropriate." University of Houston professor Jaclyn Ocumpaugh named the precondition — "I think we want to make sure that our teachers really understand how these things work before we turn them loose in the classroom" — and the misconception behind it: "There are a lot of people that think this is basically Google on steroids, and it's not" (ABC13, 28 July 2026). Most district frameworks this summer are adoption plans with caveats. This one runs the other way — a prohibition floor with graduated release upward — and whatever one makes of where the age line falls, a framework whose first question is what a nine-year-old should not be doing with a chatbot starts somewhere very different from one asking which licence to buy.

  • ASEAN's education ministers made AI one of three regional priorities for the next five years, and Singapore now chairs it. The ASEAN Five-Year Work Plan on Education 2026-2030 was launched on 22 July during the 14th ASEAN Education Ministers Meeting in Singapore, with artificial intelligence in education, STEM, and holistic development as its three focus areas; Singapore's education minister Desmond Lee framed it as "taking practical steps to prepare our people for the future" (Bernama, 22 July 2026). Singapore holds the education-sector chairmanship for 2026-2027 and will run signature programmes on AI in education and STEM, including a forum drawing in China, Japan, South Korea and dialogue partners (Xinhua, 23 July 2026). Vietnam's deputy minister of education and training Le Quan attended (VietnamPlus, 23 July 2026). Regional work plans rarely bind an international school, but they shape the professional-development market, the conference circuit and the vocabulary ministries use — and a school in Ho Chi Minh City now sits inside a bloc whose declared education agenda names AI first, a nearer frame for a board conversation than an imported European or American one.

  • More than a thousand people who build frontier models asked Washington to help build the machinery for slowing them down. A statement titled "Pacing the Frontier" circulated on 28 July carrying signatures from employees at OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta, backed by the nonprofits Guidelight AI Standards and Encode AI. Reported counts moved as it stayed open — 1,171 in one account, 1,178 in another — and the signatories are senior: Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei and several co-founders, plus OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, with Sam Altman not among them (Trending Topics, 29 July 2026); OpenAI chief research officer Mark Chen and Google AI-safety VP Anca Dragan also appear on the list. The ask is not a pause. Trending Topics renders it as Washington being asked to "support an international effort to develop the technical and governance instruments needed to deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development"; the letter is deliberately non-prescriptive about what those instruments are, with compute transparency, shared evaluation protocols and export controls coming from commentary rather than the text. Both OpenAI and Anthropic endorsed it at company level within hours (explainx.ai). It changes no school's timetable. What it changes is the credibility cost of one particular staffroom line: "the people building this aren't worried" has stopped being a defensible thing to say.

  • The largest open-weight model ever released came out of China on Sunday, and anyone can run it on their own hardware. Moonshot AI published the weights for Kimi K3 on 26 July, a day ahead of its stated target: 2.8 trillion total parameters with 104 billion active — 16 of 896 experts selected per token across 93 layers — a 1,048,576-token context window, and native text, image and video handling via a 401-million-parameter vision encoder, under a custom Kimi K3 licence (Hugging Face model card). Nathan Lambert calls it "the strongest open model ever released" and reckons that "either the open-to-closed or American-to-Chinese model performance gap has been reduced from the debated 6-9 months to something shorter, say 3-5 months" — pointedly declining to say which of the two moved (Interconnects). It is emphatically not a school tool: self-hosting starts at eight H100 80GB GPUs, a data-centre purchase, not a server-room one (explainx.ai). The reason to track it anyway is that open weights remain the only architecture under which an institution with a hard data-residency constraint can run a near-frontier model without a vendor contract at all — and the distance between that option and the frontier just halved.

On the radar

  • Microsoft is paying eighteen university labs to find what its own red team misses. The External Red Team Alliance, announced on 27 July, pairs a global academic network with an operational specialist network, giving unrestricted gifts — no deliverables, no product requirements — to labs on six continents, among them Carnegie Mellon, Harvard, IIT Madras, KAIST, UCL, Pretoria and São Paulo. The stated problem is that safety testing inside a single organisation leaves failure modes needing domain expertise, multilingual understanding and regional knowledge undetected (Microsoft Security Blog, 27 July 2026). Worth watching whether independent capacity survives being funded by the thing it audits.

  • A paper submitted this month puts a number on what a red-team pass can actually prove. Bandana Kaur's "What AI Red-Team Evaluations Can and Cannot Prove", submitted 23 July, derives an "evidential ceiling" — a measurable limit on how far any single evaluation result can move confidence in a safety claim under a fixed budget — and argues the boundary "is calculable rather than merely a matter of judgment". The regimes diverge sharply: modest benchmarks can certify high-frequency harms at a stated standard, while for rare catastrophic harms current benchmarks fall "several orders of magnitude short". The method extends to adaptive and automated red-teaming (arXiv, 23 July 2026).

  • Meta funded thirty smart-glasses projects, and the education ones are about learning in the flow of the day. Nearly $2 million went to 30 US organisations selected from almost 500 applications across 18 states, split between Accelerator grants for groups already deploying AI glasses and Catalyst grants for developers building on the Wearables Device Access Toolkit. Immerse Inc. is doing hands-free real-time language tutoring for more than 200,000 learners; Easter Seals Greater Houston's BridgingApps programme is piloting with people who have low vision, people with intellectual and developmental disabilities, and people experiencing cognitive aging; United Spinal Association is documenting accessibility barriers (EdTech Innovation Hub, 30 July 2026).

Sceptic's corner

The line already forming around the JED numbers is causal: chatbots are making adolescents lonelier and more depressed. It is an easy inference and the research does not support it. The sample comes from schools already enrolled in JED's own programmes rather than a representative frame (EdTech Innovation Hub, 30 July 2026), and Anderson herself presented the relationship as an association rather than a cause (WJLA, 24 July 2026).

The competing explanation is at least as plausible and considerably duller. A student who is already distressed at one in the morning has a narrow menu: wake a parent, message a friend who is asleep, wait until a counsellor has a free period on Thursday, or open an application that answers instantly and never looks tired. Selection into AI use by distress would produce precisely this correlation with no causal contribution from the technology. The middle-school-and-urban skew in the emotional-use finding is, if anything, mild evidence for the selection reading — those are the cohorts with the least autonomy over their own time.

None of which is a reason to file the study away — only to read it as measuring something other than AI. What it measures is friction: a pastoral pathway with a response time counted in days and a social cost counted in being seen walking into an office, losing to something with neither. That is actionable without settling the causal question. Whether or not the chatbot is doing harm, it is winning on availability — and availability is a design variable a school controls.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 14 June 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

A frontier model vanished from every classroom in 72 hours — the lesson is to build on task design, not the tool.

Week ending Sunday, 14 June 2026 · 1,540 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

The weekend handed us the story of the week, after Friday's brief had filed. On 12 June the US Commerce Department ordered Anthropic to suspend Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 — and because the company says it cannot reliably separate foreign nationals from anyone else in real time, it pulled both models for every user on Earth, paying customers and its own staff included (Anthropic, 12 June; Bloomberg, 13 June). Secretary Howard Lutnick's letter to Dario Amodei cited national security and an export-control directive barring access "by any foreign national," reportedly triggered by a jailbreak demonstrated against Mythos 5. Fable 5 launched 9 June as the most capable model the company had shipped; three days later it was a global blackout by government fiat. Anthropic is complying but disputing — it says the vulnerability was minor, previously known, and present in rival frontier models too (Washington Post, 13 June).

The rest, briefly: Apple's WWDC turned the managed device into an enforcement surface (lockable browser, multi-app mode, harder assessment configs), and OpenAI's rewritten ChatGPT memory began reaching free accounts. The other genuinely-fresh item: Inside Higher Ed and Generation Lab published a Student Voice survey on 11 June — 1,038 students, 203 institutions. Only 7% call themselves "all in" on AI; 40% worry about dependence; just 10% think their institution is handling it well. Students are more sceptical than the staffroom assumes.

What Does It All Mean?

Here is the question the week quietly posed: if a school's whole AI policy rests on disclosure — "tell me which tool you used, and what for" — is that instrument still usable? Three threads from the dailies converged on the same uncomfortable answer.

Thread one, Wednesday: Anthropic's Fable 5 silently routed most biology and chemistry requests to Claude Opus 4.8, so a student often could not know which model answered them (Anthropic). (The model is now suspended — see above — but the legibility point it illustrated stands, and indeed sharpens.) Thread two, Thursday: OpenAI's new memory writes a persistent profile the user cannot fully see, that survives a deleted chat, and that the company admits the summary page does not completely list (implicator.ai, 4 June). Thread three, all week: the EU AI Act's chatbot-transparency obligations land 2 August, leaning hard on disclosure as the safeguard.

Now two voices from outside the headlines. A 2025 paper on the AI Act, Manipulation and the Danger of Mirrors, argues that a label reading "this is AI" does not neutralise an interface engineered to feel reciprocal — knowing it is a machine does not stop the social response. Disclosure assumes a rational reader the architecture is built to bypass. In the other direction, the detection literature keeps hardening: a January 2026 review finds AI detectors generate more false accusations than correct identifications, with documented racial skew against non-native writers. So the school cannot reliably confirm a disclosure that was made, nor catch one that was withheld.

Put the three threads and the two voices together and the conclusion is not "disclosure is worthless." It is narrower and more useful: disclosure is a weak primitive to build on, and a worse one every quarter. The tools are becoming less legible (silent fallbacks, invisible memory) while the regulators lean harder on legibility, and verification fails in both directions.

The shutdown adds a second lesson pointing the same way. A school cannot even see which model answered — and now learns it cannot count on the model being there at all. Fable 5 was available to students on Tuesday and gone by government order on Friday, for reasons with nothing to do with the school. That is an argument against building any classroom routine on a single vendor's top model — the same conclusion the disclosure problem reaches from the other side. Legibility says you cannot fully know what the tool is doing; continuity says you cannot assume the tool will still exist. Both push the weight off the tool and onto the design around it.

This is where last week's three-mode contract earns a second look — not as the answer, but with a correction. Baseline / scaffold / open mode works because it shifts the burden off the student's honesty and onto the task design: a baseline task is built so that AI cannot quietly do the work, regardless of what anyone discloses — or which model is up this week. The honest upgrade is to stop treating "students disclose consequential uses" as the load-bearing control in open mode. Treat disclosure as a teaching habit worth cultivating — and assume it will be partial, sometimes impossible, and never the thing that makes an inference valid. The structural choice carries the weight; the label, and the model, are courtesies.

Behind You! Behind You!

The WWDC education story has an IT half the dailies underplayed. Jamf's 11 June education recap reads the keynote for administrators: expanded Platform SSO and offline authentication, QR-code and guest-access onboarding on iPad, and declarative controls for binaries, app config, and Safari permissions. Its theme — "identity is becoming foundational, management more autonomous" — is the unglamorous plumbing behind every device-mode policy. Worth a read before any Apple-side rollout conversation.

Students want course design, not more AI modules. Buried in that 11 June Student Voice survey: 24% of students said the thing they most want is authentic learning preserved through better assignment design, and 60% want AI offered as personalised support rather than bolted on (Inside Higher Ed). It is higher-ed and self-report, but it points the same way as the teacher data: the demand is for better tasks, not louder tools.

The Riff Returns But Slower

  • Live — Fable 5 / Mythos 5 restoration. When access returns is the open question; as of this weekend both models remain fully suspended, with no court order restoring them, while Anthropic disputes the directive and says it is working to restore access (Anthropic, 12 June). The wider Anthropic–government relationship is already adversarial — the DOJ is contesting a separate, earlier order tied to the winter federal-agency ban — so expect this fought, not quietly resolved. The durable signal is the precedent: a frontier model can now be export-controlled out of every classroom at once.
  • 2026-06-28 to 07-01 — ISTELive/ASCD. As ever, the value arrives after the stagecraft: released session resources and case studies, not keynote adjectives (ISTE).
  • 2026-06-30 — Colorado's AI Act takes effect, covering high-risk systems including those affecting educational access — the first concrete compliance date in this wave.
  • 2026-08-02 — EU AI Act transparency and high-risk education obligations. An Omnibus delay has been discussed but is not law until published; until then the date stands for anyone selling grading or proctoring AI into the EU.
  • Slow burn — Utah's actual district uptake of Gemini for Education; whether consumer-tier memory controls get more auditable under August's regulatory pressure; the steadily growing — but still thin — base of rigorous causal evidence behind all of it.

Sceptic's Corner

The hype to squeeze this week is the quiet assumption underneath every "AI governance is maturing" deck: that transparency is the mature control — that once a system tells you it is AI, once memory has a settings page, once disclosure is required by law, the responsible-use problem is largely architected.

The week argued the opposite, gently and from multiple directions. The page that lists your ChatGPT memories is admitted to be incomplete. The model on the label is not always the model that answered. The detector that would verify a student's disclosure is, per the January 2026 review, more likely to be wrong than right and biased against the students least able to defend themselves. And the regulatory instrument arriving in August leans on exactly the disclosure mechanism a 2025 AI Act paper says users' own psychology is built to ignore.

And the week's own headline made the point sharper: the most transparent thing imaginable — a model with a card and published safety warnings — was pulled offline because of a published safety finding. The lesson from the shutdown is not transparency. It is continuity: a tool you can fully inspect is no use to a class on the morning a government turns it off.

None of that is an argument for cynicism, and certainly not for AI-detector theatre — the detectors are the worst offender here, not the cure. It is an argument for putting the weight somewhere it can bear load. A disclosure norm is a fine thing to teach and a poor thing to enforce; a single best model is a fine thing to use and a poor thing to depend on. The validity of what a student hands in should rest on how the task was built, what evidence of thinking it requires, and what data it keeps off the open web — none of which depend on a label being accurate, or a model still being switched on. Transparency was never going to be the safeguard, and this week it looked less like one than usual.
