Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Tuesday, 17 November 2026

Top story

More educators have had AI training, and the follow-up is thin.

In its 16 November reporting on a nationally representative US survey, Education Week says about two-thirds of 852 teachers, school administrators and district leaders surveyed from 26 August to 6 October reported receiving at least one session on using AI in their work. That is up 11 percentage points from the previous year's 56%. In interviews, educators singled out familiar problems: too little time to work on their own materials, too little feedback while they were trying things, and too little room to talk about drawbacks, including what to do about suspected misuse in assessed work.

A separate survey of 268 US teacher educators, reported in the same 16 November package, shows how uneven the preparation can be before a teacher even starts. Sixty per cent said decisions about whether to include an AI focus were left to individual instructors. Fourteen per cent reported ongoing training with follow-up from their institution. Thirty-four per cent wanted that format and another 34% wanted more than one session without ongoing follow-up. These percentages describe the teacher educators themselves. Neither survey tests whether a participant can judge an AI output or whether a different format improves teaching.

For international schools, the useful design question is what staff can bring back to a second session. An English teacher could bring a rewritten discussion question and the evidence from the text that made them discard the first draft. A maths teacher could compare two explanations of the same wrong answer and show where one skipped the student's reasoning. Both can work from invented examples in the training room. A colleague should be able to reproduce the judgement in a fresh example a fortnight later. An attendance figure can tell a leader whom the launch reached. I would give the second session the job of showing what travelled back to a classroom.

Other stories

  • Gemini separates chat history from model training. In a 16 November announcement, Google said the Gemini app will let users retain a chat history while opting out of generative AI training. It says chats started with the new History setting off will still be retained for around two months so it can respond, process feedback and protect users and its services. The company plans to start a global rollout over the coming weeks. The Workspace privacy documentation sets out the terms for eligible managed accounts, including its commitment that Google does not have people review content or use it to train generative AI models outside the domain without permission. This is a useful distinction for a teacher who opens the same app under two accounts. In a training screenshot, identify the signed-in account and specify what the control governs: whether a conversation appears in history, how long it is retained, and whether it can go into model training are three separate questions. The account is the first thing to check before using the setting to make a decision about school data.

  • An Asia-Pacific AI training fund backs local delivery. On 11 November, AVPN announced 28 implementing partners across 19 markets for the third phase of its Google.org- and Asian Development Bank-backed fund. India's Pratham Education Foundation plans to adapt existing material for critical classroom use, including when to set AI aside. JA Korea plans to pair training with formal certification for public school educators and outreach to rural teachers. The Raspberry Pi Foundation is joining as a strategic partner to help local organisations adapt Experience AI content. AVPN also announced a learning platform in 16 languages that uses a short assessment to recommend a pathway. The numbers describe the scope of a programme whose next phase is getting underway. The detail to watch is who changes the material. A translated example can still assume class sizes, connectivity or tool access that local teachers rarely have. A local partner with licence to change the task, the tools and the assumptions can make a more meaningful adaptation. That is worth checking before adopting a catalogue's ready-made pathway.

  • A physicist shows where an AI contribution fits in a proof. Nathaniel Craig's 15 November first-person account describes the collaboration behind his 6 October preprint, which proposes a candidate mathematical definition of the Standard Model on a spacetime grid. Craig says his strategy was to represent the same force fields on successively finer grids. GPT‑6 Astra suggested how to carry those fields onto finer grids, showed that the comparisons would converge and took the lead on the detailed proof. Craig names three lattice theorists who inspected the argument and describes work still to do, including checking a condition at finite grid spacing that helps connect the construction to a quantum theory with conserved probabilities. The news this week is the researcher's account, which gives a science class an unusually concrete example of AI disclosure. A student could use the same four headings on a much smaller investigation: the method I brought, the step the model contributed, the check somebody else can reproduce, and the limitation that still matters. Each heading asks for a different kind of evidence.

  • An accessibility study asks which parts of a task should survive an adaptation. In a 13 November exploratory study, Gao and colleagues examined 63 digital products, collected 158 stakeholder questionnaire responses, and used five of eight interviews for their core analysis. The stakeholder work considered possible activities for autistic children in China. The lion-dance scenario captures the design question. A lion can appear as artwork during any generic exercise, or two learners can take complementary roles and coordinate movements. Lowering the sound level could make the second design easier to enter while keeping coordination as the task. Removing the need to respond to a partner changes what the task teaches. The study enrolled and observed no autistic children and measured no effects on engagement or learning. Its useful product is a question for school teams to take into a review with learners: which demand is part of the learning goal, and which makes it awkward to show what you can do? The answer may differ even for pupils working on the same activity.

  • Ofcom wants stronger protections built into more platforms. On 16 November the UK regulator set out additional online safety measures. They include stronger detection duties for certain services and banning users who generate, upload or share child sexual exploitation and abuse content. On anti-grooming rules, Ofcom says services already in scope must use highly effective age checks to determine which accounts need protections or extend those protections to all users. It expects the new duties to come into force from early 2027, subject to parliamentary process. The practical school question for an app with messaging or sharing is what a student account can do on the first day, before anybody has had a chance to find a menu or make a report. A vendor can talk through its safety features from an administrator account and leave that question unanswered. A demonstration with a student account can show who can contact it, where a report goes and which protections require a school administrator to turn them on.

On the radar

  • ISTE+ASCD briefs teams on the 2027 AI Innovator Student Challenge. ISTE+ASCD's 16 November kickoff webinar followed the 3 November opening of submissions. Teams are educator-guided, identify a community problem tied to a UN Sustainable Development Goal, and design a solution that uses AI. Entries close on 7 February. The contest might turn up useful examples of pupils testing whether AI adds anything to a design. A local problem such as confusing recycling labels admits a printed prototype that a team can test alongside any AI idea. That comparison would say more about the project's value than the fact students got a model to run.

  • An external safety assessor will say what it was allowed to inspect. OpenAI announced its first phase of embedded assessments on 16 November. In its own account that day, Transluce describes a six-week pilot, due to start in early December, examining selected post-training alignment evaluations. It plans to report what extra access it requested, what it received and how restrictions shaped its conclusions. Its agreement limits public findings to released models. For school buyers who see independent testing invoked in a pitch, the report's eventual account of its scope may offer questions worth borrowing. Which product and settings were tested, and how much of the result can a school actually read?

Sceptic's corner

A device dashboard makes it tempting to put a number next to the quality of a lesson. Minutes are easy to report. In a 16 November Stanford interview, Hari Subramonyam asks what intellectual work the device allowed and what it displaced. He gives the example of a magazine project: selecting material, organising it and deciding how to reach readers are part of the learning. A design tool might free up time to make and revise those decisions. A generator can also make the decisions on a student's behalf. The dashboard will record activity in either case.

The case for managing time is real. If a class routinely loses ten minutes to notifications or switching tabs, a routine that gets those minutes back can help. An aggregate limit can also double as a cap on access. In the University of Sydney's 16 November response to Australia's digital detox debate, Catherine Smyth points to text-to-speech and other assistive uses that let students do the assigned work. Count them without context and a successful adjustment can look like a classroom that needs to cut back.

Give the clock the question it can answer. For the learning question, ask students to show a choice the task required them to make. A magazine team can show two versions of a page and explain what changed for the reader. That takes a few minutes and gives a teacher something a usage report cannot supply.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 15 November 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 15 November 2026 · 1,387 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

A week of rulebooks. South Korea confirmed four cases of cheating with AI glasses in school assessments (Ministry of Education, 9 November). Stanford's SCALE review grew from 20 causal papers to 52, and its verdict stayed conditional (SCALE, 10 November). England opened a consultation on safety certification for edtech and AI (DfE, 10 November). Brazil approved a framework that rules out AI grading of interpretive work and sanctions based only on a detector (Secom, 10 November). Google shipped skills, a "Wait until" step and standalone canvas links.

Since Friday's brief: NPR asked why Google won't let schools switch off AI in Search, and put a former Google education lead's antitrust theory on the record (NPR, 13 November). It frames the centrepiece. On Saturday, The 74 carried Chalkbeat's account of Wednesday's Chicago rally; it leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

Google's position on Search is now plain. AI Overview and AI Mode are "deeply integrated" into it, so a school cannot keep Search and switch them off (New York Times via The Star, 10 November). Senator Ed Markey's 9 November letter calls that "an ultimatum to effectively force schools to enable generative AI" (same report). So the Sunday question: if a school cannot remove the AI box from the results page, what is left for it to govern?

First, why the box won't come off. Tracy Pizzo, who spent more than a decade at Google, much of it on education, told NPR the answer may lie in court rather than in classrooms. In September, Judge Amit Mehta dismissed publishers' antitrust suits; Penske Media's tying claim failed because Search and AI Overviews were not plausibly separate products; reports quote the ruling's phrase "an integrated search experience" (MIXED, 4 October). A schools-only off switch, Pizzo argued, would be evidence that the two can be separated. The antitrust experts NPR asked were not entirely convinced, and Google did not answer (NPR, 13 November). Treat it as a theory. It does explain why "off by default" stops at the Search bar.

Second, what the box does to reading. Pew tracked the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025. When an AI summary appeared, they clicked a traditional result on 8% of visits, against 15% without one, and clicked a link inside the summary on 1% (Pew Research Center, 22 July 2025). Common Sense Media found that "AI Mode completed 100% of the homework assignments we gave it", and that neither feature can be switched off by parents or schools (Common Sense Media, July 2026).

Third, what schools have been trying to teach. The Stanford History Education Group found that professional fact-checkers beat historians and undergraduates at judging websites because they read laterally: they leave the page and check the source elsewhere. In a randomised trial across six high schools, six 50-minute lessons made students twice as likely to spot questionable sites, though they still earned only half the available points (Stanford GSE, 19 April 2022). Google.org funded the study.

Put those together. The habit information literacy builds is leaving the page. The interface that now greets every query is built so that most people don't.

For an international school on Google, that moves the question from the admin console to the task. Whatever a school decides about Gemini for students, every student who searches will meet an Overview. A school's AI-use scale tells students what they may use; it cannot treat Search as an AI-free zone. A research task can ask for the move the Overview skips: name two sources outside it, say what each adds, and mark where the summary was wrong or thin. A ninth-grader who can do that has a skill the box cannot take away.

Behind You! Behind You!

Chicago took the argument to Google's door. About 30 people, mostly parents and educators, rallied outside Google's West Loop office on Wednesday, alongside a protest in Cambridge, Massachusetts (Chalkbeat via The 74, 14 November). Chicago Public Schools, which has blocked student access to generative AI tools, said it is "actively exploring all options to independently suppress" the Overview, and failing that will look at other search tools. Watch whether a district that size actually leaves.

Anthropic signed the teachers' unions' privacy standard. The AFT and its New York affiliate, the UFT, announced on 10 November a legally binding agreement covering Claude for Teachers and Anthropic's existing district agreements (K-12 Dive, 10 November). Data may be used only for authorised educational purposes, and schools control how long it is kept and when it is deleted. Microsoft's equivalent took effect on 1 November. A school outside the unions' reach can still ask any vendor to match those terms in writing.

Ho Chi Minh City began planning its AI periods. The city's Department of Education and Training held a workshop on 12 November at Le Hong Phong High School for the Gifted on bringing the national AI education framework into secondary schools this year: 12 periods per class per school year, as topics or woven into subjects (SGGP, 12 November). The bottlenecks named included teacher capacity, funding, student data safety and formalistic assessment. Vietnam's largest city is working the same problems as the international schools around it, on a national timetable.

Gemini in Gmail reached every adult Workspace account, on by default. From 10 November, Google is extending thread summaries and non-personalised Help me write to "all Workspace users 18 years and older" (Google, 10 November). The features are on by default; users can disable them and admins can switch them off. The post does not name Education editions. For a school, that means every staff inbox and any student account set as adult. The decision is better made before the habit forms.

No start date yet. The assessment half is the one to follow.

Sceptic's Corner

The protest slogan wants Search without the AI. The strongest case against it is that the old results page was never the gateway campaigners remember.

In clickstream data that mostly predates AI Overviews, SparkToro and Datos found that "58.5% of American Google searches resulted in zero clicks", and that for every 1,000 US searches just 360 clicks reached a site Google doesn't own (SparkToro, 2 July 2024). Answer boxes, panels and the habit of stopping at the top all came before the chatbot. The Overview made an old pattern fluent.

That doesn't make the Overview harmless. A box that writes the homework on request is a fair thing to want gone for younger students, and Common Sense's evidence gives campaigners a real case. But switching it off would not restore a habit of checking sources that most users never had. Worse, the fight over the switch lets everyone treat the problem as solved once the switch is found.

The Stanford trial is the better guide. Six lessons doubled students' ability to spot a dubious site and still left them on half marks. That is the work, with or without the box.

The line for the staffroom: the AI box isn't the first time Google answered for students; it's the first time it answered in paragraphs. Teach them to leave the page.
