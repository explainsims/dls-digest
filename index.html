Tuesday, 3 November 2026

Top story

A teacher-feedback agent improved practice, with a boundary worth keeping.

A study published on 2 November in Educational Technology & Society reports a semester-long randomised trial with 30 secondary teachers and 240 classroom videos. An agent combining a language model with retrieved material supplied formative feedback on how faithfully teachers implemented their instructional designs. The abstract reports greater gains in implementation fidelity and overall teaching performance than in the comparison group. Exploratory associations were clearer for classroom management and a supportive climate than for cognitive activation: the work of getting pupils to think deeply (Du and colleagues, 2 November).

That distinction deserves more attention than the headline improvement. Feedback about whether a lesson followed its design can be specific, frequent and useful. It can help a teacher notice a missing step or make participation more orderly. But a lesson can be beautifully executed while asking pupils to do very little intellectual work. A system that checks the script and a colleague who challenges the script are offering different kinds of help. The useful professional-development question is which kind a teacher needs at that moment, rather than whether one source of feedback can replace the other.

For international schools, the practical reading is to separate a coaching tool's claims before evaluating it. Does it recognise an observable routine accurately? Does its advice improve the next lesson? Does that lesson leave pupils better able to explain, transfer or challenge an idea? Those are successive questions, and success on the first does not answer the third. Classroom video also creates its own consent and data-handling questions; a promising abstract does not settle them.

This is a small trial, and this account is limited to the published abstract, not an independent examination of its full methods or effects. The journal assigns the article to an October 2027 issue but explicitly records publication yesterday. Its value today is a sharper boundary for an interesting result: feedback that improves enactment still needs a human conversation about what is worth enacting.

Other stories

  • McGraw Hill is taking licensed course material into Copilot, with a limited beta still ahead. The publisher's 29 October announcement says customers with Microsoft 365 Copilot licences will be able to summarise, translate and adapt the McGraw Hill material they license, producing study aids and lesson resources through a plugin using Model Context Protocol. A limited beta begins in early 2027; this is an announced route to access, not a classroom feature available today (McGraw Hill, 29 October). The strategic move is more interesting than another assistant button: the publisher wants its paid library to travel into the interface where teachers already work, while keeping a commercial boundary around the material. For international schools, that adds a question to the textbook comparison. Which useful functions come with the content licence, and which depend on a second platform's subscription? Grounding an answer in a licensed book can make its provenance easier to inspect; it does not guarantee that the adaptation preserves the intended difficulty or assessment demand. A teacher still needs to compare the generated resource with the original and with the learner's task. The content may have a respected publisher behind it. The particular worksheet has still just been made.

  • Google's new African learning hub treats connectivity as part of the teaching design. Launched on 2 November, Skilling Africa with Google brings together more than 15 training initiatives, with a target of reaching three million people by 2030. Google describes short lessons that can be downloaded and completed offline, a low-bandwidth mobile design, and SCOUT, an AI study companion offering answers and study guidance. The service is free; the reach figure is a target, not a completed training outcome (Google Africa, 2 November). The useful design lesson travels further than the launch market. An online course that assumes continuous, comfortable access has already selected who can finish it. An international school may have excellent campus Wi-Fi while its families still face uneven home connectivity, shared devices or awkward mobile-only tasks. That makes a small downloadable activity a substantive accessibility choice. The companion is the part that will attract the demonstration; the ability to keep learning when it is unavailable may matter more. Nothing in the announcement establishes the quality of SCOUT's feedback or the effect of its streaks and rewards on learning. Those need their own evaluation. Access, sustained participation and worthwhile learning are three separate achievements, even when a product page puts them in one paragraph.

  • Microsoft is testing workplace assistants against invented organisations rather than borrowed customer records. Its 30 October research account describes synthetic environments for pharmaceuticals, capital markets and software work, built using public signals from occupational data, job advertisements and company filings. The environments include evolving communications and projects, allowing questions about changing decisions rather than isolated documents. Microsoft reports a 39% improvement in an internal measure of synthetic-data quality compared with its initial generation approach. That is not a 39% improvement in Copilot's accuracy, and the account is a vendor description of its evaluation infrastructure (Microsoft Research, 30 October). Schools can borrow the principle without imitating the scale. A fictional pupil's record can contain an outdated accommodation, a later correction and two similarly named activities; an assistant then has to find the authoritative current answer. A clean demonstration with one immaculate document never asks that question. Synthetic data keeps real children out of an early experiment, but it is useful only if the invented case contains the awkwardness the system must handle. Otherwise the exercise measures how well an assistant navigates the world its designers wish they had. The valuable test is a realistic conflict with a known answer, followed by a clear account of what the assistant got wrong.

  • A small journalism study offers a better discussion prompt than another declaration that students value critical thinking. Kentucky State University's 2 November account of David Shabazz's preliminary qualitative work describes 17 college students using AI for reporting tasks. Fifteen saw it as an assistant rather than an author, 14 emphasised verification, and 13 stressed human judgment and authorship. The research was presented on 22 September; yesterday's university report is the new publication, not a newly completed experiment (Kentucky State University, 2 November). These are participants' perspectives, not measured proof that they reliably checked their sources. That limit makes the teaching opportunity clearer. Ask a learner to identify one suggestion they rejected, the evidence that changed their mind and what they did next. The answer reveals more about editorial responsibility than agreement with a principle almost nobody would oppose. Journalism also keeps the audience in view: a plausible claim can harm a reader even when the writer had no intention to mislead. That is a useful bridge from classroom citation rules to a public responsibility. Seventeen undergraduates cannot speak for school students generally, but their language gives teachers a concrete starting point for asking what authorship requires after a machine has helped with the draft.

On the radar

  • Chatbot engagement is beginning to be described through the conversation rather than the click count. Another ET&S article published on 2 November examines 29 graduate students using two chatbot activities, one built around open discussion and another around structured tutoring. Its exploratory profiles describe different engagement patterns, not general learner types or evidence that a chatbot caused better learning (Che and colleagues, 2 November). The useful question for schools is whether a learner challenges, revises and explains during an exchange. A long conversation can contain very little of that. The profiles are hypotheses to investigate, not labels to attach permanently to children.

  • EDUtech Asia's immediate value is the questions its demonstrations must survive. The long-advertised Singapore event reaches its workshop day today, 3 November, ahead of the 4–5 November conference and exhibition. The organiser distinguishes the paid conference from the free, registered expo and says sessions will not be recorded (Terrapinn, current event details). Watch for evidence that travels beyond a successful demonstration: who was taught, what they could subsequently do, and what staff had to maintain. A regional showcase can expose useful approaches and common implementation problems. A crowded stand is a measure of attention; the learning claim still needs a separate denominator.

Sceptic's corner

The attractive claim is that more feedback must mean better teaching. AI can make feedback abundant, immediate and consistently attached to a rubric. All three properties can help. None establishes that the rubric is asking the most important question.

A teacher may receive precise advice about the visibility of an objective, the sequence of activities and the distribution of participation. Those are observable features with genuine classroom value. The uncomfortable follow-up is whether a pupil encountered an idea difficult enough to change their understanding. A routine can become more fluent while the intellectual demand remains comfortably low. Rewarding what is easy to recognise can gradually narrow what teachers feel permitted to attempt.

Today's trial is interesting precisely because its abstract distinguishes aspects of practice instead of treating a single improvement score as the entire result (Du and colleagues, 2 November). It does not justify dismissing automated feedback. It suggests giving that feedback a bounded job and keeping the wider judgment open.

For coaching, a useful division of labour is to let a tool help identify a pattern, then ask the teacher what the pattern means and what alternative might serve the class better. Sometimes a departure from the plan is the best decision of the lesson. A system trained to reward fidelity needs room for that possibility. The standard should be a more thoughtful next lesson, with pupils doing worthwhile thinking, rather than a teacher becoming easier for a machine to score.

The Week That Was

The week kept returning to one gap: naming a problem is not the same as stopping it. New York's Education Department named 38 products, then said it was working with companies to find out whether the offending features could be disabled at all (Chalkbeat, 23 October). The UK's AI Security Institute described an agent that often said an attack would be harmful in the very situations where it went ahead (AISI, 23 October). Gemini's immersive explorations reached the student hub in Education editions with the line "There is no admin control for this feature" (Google, 28 October).

Since Friday's brief: whole-deck editing with Gemini in Slides, which schools get through the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, carries the same line (Google, 30 October). For schools, that makes the add-on licence the only switch. OpenAI said ChatGPT will restrict practical weapons information for teens even without signs of violent intent, end chats that seem fixated on violence, and may pause accounts after repeated ones, telling parents when a teen's is paused. Rollout starts within weeks (Reuters via AOL, 30 October).

What Does It All Mean?

Every story above assumes a lever somewhere. So the question worth a Sunday: when a school cannot switch a feature off, what is its AI rule actually made of?

Start with the lever schools think they have. Among American teens who use AI for schoolwork, Common Sense Media found in August that 44% had had an AI tool blocked on a school network or device, and 59% of those simply moved to a personal device, where there is even less support (Common Sense Media, 18 August). A network block is a detour, not a wall.

Assessment researchers have been making this case for a while. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu split institutional responses into discursive changes, which tell students what is allowed, and structural ones, which change how the task itself works. Traffic-light permission schemes, they argue, borrow the look of a real enforcement system without its power, and leaning on rules like these creates what they call an "enforcement illusion" (Corbin, Dawson and Liu, 2025).

The University of Sydney, where Liu works, had already written the idea into policy: from 2025, AI allowed by default except in exams and in-semester tests, then two lanes (ACS Information Age, December 2024). Secured, supervised tasks leave AI to the examiner; everything else is open, with students guided in its use. Its FAQ is blunt that AI use cannot be restricted in assessments not secured face to face, nor reliably detected (Teaching@Sydney, updated April 2026).

Even the AI Assessment Scale's own authors agree. Leon Furze, one of them, writes that assessment security is not what the scale aims at; it is a framework to support assessment design, and a colourful handout plus "a hopeful expectation" secures nothing (Furze, September 2025).

That would be bleak if rules kept students honest. They never did. A follow-up study published in February, surveying 4,354 students at six US high schools about eighteen months after ChatGPT's release, found an overall cheating rate of 72.06%, which its authors call consistent with historical baselines; AI availability does not appear to have changed overall prevalence (Chen et al., February 2026). The tool changed the method, not the rate. Whatever sets the rate, whether engagement, pressure or a task that feels worth doing, sits upstream of any switch.

So the working answer: an AI rule rests on three layers, in falling order of reliability. First, what is structurally true of the task: done in the room, staged, defended aloud. Second, what the school can actually switch, which is coarse and covers products more than features. Third, what it tells students, which is invaluable for conversation and useless as security.

For a school running an AI use scale, the practical move is small. The scale stays as the shared language. But every task pitched at the no-AI or low-AI end needs a one-line answer to "what makes this true in the room?" If the honest answer is that the student promised, the task is open and should be marked as one.

Behind You! Behind You!

New York will treat unverified chatbot users as children. Governor Hochul signed S.9051B/A.10379C on 28 October. In the coming months, companies may not offer under-18s, or any user not confirmed as an adult, features such as posing as human, claiming a relationship, asking unprompted emotional questions or using a child's private details from earlier chats. The attorney general can seek up to $25,000 per violation (New York State, 28 October). The state's Business Council had argued that educational tools were swept in (TechTimes, 29 October). Watch whether vendors build that default once and ship it everywhere.

States want proof and can't get it. SETDA's 2026 State EdTech Trends report, published 29 October from 73 survey responses, found 79% of state leaders naming AI a top technology priority. Yet 52% said their state collects no data on the use or effectiveness of ed-tech products, and 68% said independently verifying vendor claims about AI products is challenging (GovTech, 29 October). If American states with procurement offices struggle, an international school is checking alone.

Half of the US schools and districts surveyed had a cyber incident last year. The Center for Internet Security's 2026 K-12 report puts it at 51% in the past 12 months, and 66% of districts leave cybersecurity to one or two people (CIS, 27 October). Phishing or email compromise ranked among the top incidents for 87% of leaders, and the report warns of more convincing AI-enabled phishing (K-12 Dive, 27 October). The weak point is staff inboxes, not servers.

The ed-tech backlash is on the ballot, and it isn't partisan. Ahead of Tuesday's US midterms, Chalkbeat found a Republican candidate for California governor promising to ban AI in schools on day one. In Texas, the Republican governor pledges less classroom screen time and his Democratic challenger opposes AI for elementary students. Seven of eight San Francisco school board candidates back limits on classroom technology (Chalkbeat, 26 October). International schools enrolling families from those systems should expect the mood to travel with them.

Sceptic's Corner

Next time someone says the research shows AI improves learning, ask which research.

Jill Barshay reports 39 meta-analyses on AI in education, every one finding substantial benefits. Patrick O'Neill, a community college professor, audited 14 of them and found serious conceptual and statistical flaws, from hallucinated references to underlying studies citing generative-AI papers published before the technology was public. One, published in a Nature journal in May 2025, has since been retracted (Hechinger Report, 26 October).

When O'Neill pooled 44 well-designed experiments, outcomes ranged from very helpful to very harmful, and his preliminary calculations put the average at roughly zero. Susanna Loeb's team gathered almost 900 studies and judged only 20 good enough for its evidence summary. O'Neill's audit is itself a preprint and his numbers are preliminary, so hold him to the same standard.

Two things follow. A literature that is uniformly positive is a warning, not a comfort; real interventions produce null results. An average of roughly zero is not a verdict of harmlessness. It is what you get when good designs and bad ones cancel out, which is why the question is always which use, for whom, checked how.

The line for a staff meeting: there is no effect size for "AI", only for particular uses of it. Anyone quoting one number is quoting a brochure.
