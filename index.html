Friday, 15 May 2026

Top story

Toddle's PYP Refresh webinar is not today. Toddle's own page now lists it for Saturday, 16 May 2026, and the framing is unambiguously AI-product-shaped: "PYP Refresh: How Toddle AI Supports the 2025 Updates."

The current Toddle page gives the date as Saturday, May 16, 2026, describes the event as a 90-minute hands-on workshop, and says participants will see how PYP coordinators and school leaders use Curriculum Design Assistant, Toddle's AI planning tool, to revise and strengthen units against the 2025 PYP updates. It also says registrants get free access to the tool during breakout work. (Toddle event page).

The tell is not merely that Toddle is using AI in the title. It is that Toddle is positioning AI as curriculum-realignment infrastructure: update the POI, revise units, unpack the new subject guides, clean up concept terminology, and do it faster "without losing depth or intention." For international schools, that is directly adjacent to LMS-evaluation work because it turns the vendor from a workflow system into a planning partner. The security question from the Canvas week still belongs in the room, but the most natural version is not "what is your ransom policy?" as a cold opener. It is: when Toddle AI is used to revise unit plans, what student / teacher / curriculum data enters the model workflow, where is it processed, and what contractual controls govern retention, training use, export, and incident notification? If Toddle answers that cleanly, it becomes a stronger post-Canvas competitor. If the answer is product-demo fog, that is the datum.

Other stories

  • Instructure's 14 May incident update now names the Free-for-Teacher vector and gives the cleanest vendor-source account so far. Instructure says the 29 April unauthorized access and 7 May defacement both used one of its Free-for-Teacher accounts; the 7 May activity was detected and disabled approximately 10 minutes after it began; no additional data was accessed or exfiltrated in the second attack; and Free-for-Teacher is temporarily shut down while the company works on a safer return path. It also says Parchment was not affected and that CrowdStrike found no evidence of lateral movement into other products. This is the first official account that makes the platform-risk shape legible: a free teacher-facing product became the path into the paid institutional platform. (Instructure incident page).

  • Google AI Educator Series launched on 13 May, still US-first, with NotebookLM/Gemini as training currency rather than global entitlement. Google's April education update said the free ISTE+ASCD-backed AI training would launch 13 May for US K-12 and higher-ed educators, with new modules monthly. The same post is the stronger source for what Google is bundling around the training: expanded NotebookLM limits for Education Plus / Teaching and Learning add-on users, Gemini as an official AI provider for Moodle placements, and Gemini LTI support for Moodle starting in May. For international schools outside the US, the practical read is: Google's AI fluency materials will become useful reference currency, but the entitlement story remains uneven for non-US Workspace tenants. (Google education update).

  • OpenAI's Edu admin surface is maturing: Workspace analytics is now live for Enterprise and Edu. The ChatGPT Enterprise/Edu release notes say the refreshed Workspace analytics experience is available, replacing User analytics with workspace-level adoption and engagement views. The same release stream keeps workspace agents off by default and routes agent-building, publishing, Slack usage, and app actions through admin controls. This matters less as "new feature" and more as procurement posture: OpenAI is building the admin evidence layer schools will ask for when AI use moves from enthusiast adoption to institutionally governed service. (OpenAI release notes).

  • The European School Education Platform published updated ethical AI/data guidelines for teachers on 12 May. The update explicitly connects classroom AI use to the EU AI Act and GDPR and adds guiding questions, scenarios, legal context, definitions, and reference frameworks for primary and secondary educators. It is not an enforcement document, but it is a teacher-facing policy artefact in the register international schools actually reuse: practical scenarios plus legal framing without pretending every teacher is a lawyer. (European School Education Platform).

  • US Department of Education student-privacy webinars land late May, and the titles are exactly on this week's fault line. SPPO's May series includes FERPA 201 and Transparency on 20 May, then Incident Response and Vetting Educational Technology on 27 May. The latter promises a simulated data breach and ed-tech vetting for privacy protections and FERPA compliance. Even if US-specific, the artefact list that follows will be useful as comparable-jurisdiction language when rewriting vendor questions after Canvas. (Protecting Student Privacy).

Sceptic's Corner

The LMS story is shifting from "where do assignments live?" to "where does curriculum judgment get processed?" Canvas this week is the breach side of that shift; Toddle's Saturday webinar is the pedagogy-product side. A vendor that only stores submissions has one risk profile. A vendor whose AI assistant rewrites unit plans, maps terminology to new subject guides, and guides coordinators through curriculum realignment has another. The data entering the system may not be student grades, but it can still be institutionally sensitive: curriculum maps, teacher-authored materials, internal planning decisions, assessment language, and adaptation notes. Procurement rubrics often treat those as lower-risk than student records. That is too simple. Curriculum artefacts reveal school priorities and teacher practice; once AI tools become planning infrastructure, they deserve their own data-classification line.

Google and OpenAI are converging on opposite halves of the same school problem. Google is moving teacher fluency and Workspace-native learning tools: Gemini, NotebookLM, Moodle placements, LTI, educator training. OpenAI is moving admin governance: workspace agents, analytics, app/action controls, scheduled workflows. The naive comparison is "which model is better?" The school-facing comparison is more useful: which vendor gives the clearer path from individual teacher experimentation to governed institutional use? Right now Google has the classroom trust channel; OpenAI has the admin-control story; Toddle has the curriculum-workflow intimacy. None of the three is the whole answer.

The Instructure Free-for-Teacher vector is the quiet design lesson. Free or low-friction teacher entry points are pedagogically attractive because they let innovation happen before procurement catches up. They are also a security boundary problem when they sit near the same brand, identity fabric, support systems, or trust perimeter as the institutional product. The post-Canvas question for every ed-tech vendor is not "do you offer free teacher accounts?" It is: what prevents a free-teacher surface from becoming the route into the paid-school environment? If the answer is "different product" or "different tenant" or "different infrastructure," good. If the answer is a vibe, keep walking.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 17 May 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 17 May 2026

Classify artefacts before classifying tools: student records, teacher-authored curriculum, operational workflows, and public communications are not the same risk surface.

The Week That Was

The week began with the Canvas breach still on the clock and ended with a cleaner, odder shape: Instructure paid ShinyHunters, Congress asked for a briefing, class actions split across Utah and New York, and Instructure's own incident page finally named the Free-for-Teacher route into Canvas. The Saturday Toddle webinar then made the adjacent point without meaning to: LMS vendors are no longer just storing assignments. They are putting AI into curriculum planning, reporting, communications, and governance work. (Instructure, Toddle)

The other thread was quieter but more durable. Google launched AI educator training for US teachers; OpenAI kept hardening the admin layer for ChatGPT Edu agents; the EU Council adopted teacher-centred AI conclusions. The market is now building both the classroom-facing fluency layer and the institutional-control layer at the same time. (Google, OpenAI, EU Council)

What Does It All Mean?

The useful question this week is not "which vendor is safer?" That is too flat. The better question is: when AI becomes part of school infrastructure, what kind of school artefact is it allowed to touch?

Canvas exposed the obvious class of artefact: names, emails, course enrolments, message contents. That is traditional student-data territory, and the vocabulary around it is reasonably mature: breach notification, FERPA, state AG reporting, incident response, third-party processor, remediation. The US Department of Education's late-May privacy webinars are almost comically well-timed here: one session on transparency, one on incident response and vetting educational technology. (Protecting Student Privacy)

Toddle points at the less mature class of artefact. Its PYP Refresh workshop framed Toddle AI as a curriculum-realignment assistant: revise units, unpack 2025 PYP updates, strengthen planning, use the Curriculum Design Assistant in breakout work. That is not student-grade data, but it is not nothing. Curriculum maps, teacher-authored units, assessment language, intervention notes, school-home communication templates, and local adaptation decisions all reveal how a school thinks. They are institutional knowledge, not just files.

This is where the weekly needs to leave the breach story and do the slower work. The OECD's PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy project defines the coming assessment domain around students' ability to act critically and ethically in AI-mediated environments. The EU Council's conclusions put teachers at the centre: AI should preserve teacher autonomy, learner well-being, data protection, accountability, and risk awareness. Those two policy moves are not about picking Gemini over Claude over ChatGPT. They are about whether schools can still name the human judgement in a system once the workflow has been partially handed to software. (OECD PISA, EU Council)

That is also why OpenAI's admin controls matter more than the demo videos. Workspace agents in ChatGPT Edu can connect tools, schedule runs, ask approval for sensitive steps, expose analytics, and give admins visibility through a compliance API. This is the boring layer that turns AI from teacher enthusiasm into governed service. Google's AI Educator Series is the other half: broad professional fluency, currently US-first, with Gemini and NotebookLM as training currency. One side teaches people what to do. The other side lets institutions see and constrain what was done. Neither is sufficient alone.

For international schools, the framing to carry is simple: classify artefacts before classifying tools. Student records are one class. Teacher-authored curriculum is another. Operational workflows are a third. Public-facing communications are a fourth. Once those classes exist, the vendor questions become sharper: which artefacts enter the model workflow; where are they processed; can they train a model; who can export them; what logs exist; what happens when a free-teacher surface touches the paid-school environment. That is a better rubric than "AI feature: yes/no."

Behind You! Behind You!

The retracted ChatGPT-learning paper is the research story that will keep mattering after the breach news cools. Ars Technica covered the retraction of an influential paper that had claimed strong learning benefits from ChatGPT. Ben Williamson's caveat is the one to remember: the headline finding may persist in policy and vendor decks even after the paper is gone. (Ars Technica)

The EU Council conclusions landed in the exact register international schools borrow from. Teacher autonomy, well-being, inclusion, data protection, accountability, risk awareness: not flashy, but that is policy language with legs. It gives school leaders a non-vendor vocabulary for saying "AI, yes, but not as automation theatre." (EU Council)

OpenAI's compliance story is becoming a governance asset. The agent launch is not just "make a bot." The useful bit is admin visibility into configurations, updates, runs, connected tools, approvals, and suspension. Schools will ask for exactly this layer once agents move from pilots to department routines. (OpenAI)

Instructure's Free-for-Teacher shutdown deserves its own post-mortem slot. The vendor says both the April 29 and May 7 activity used a Free-for-Teacher account, and that the product is temporarily shut down while a safer return path is designed. Free teacher entry points are innovation channels. They are also trust-boundary tests. (Instructure)

Sceptic's Corner

"Human-centred AI" can become wallpaper if schools don't operationalise it. The EU wording is good. So is OECD's critical-literacy direction. But the phrase only earns its place if a school can point to concrete constraints: approval gates, data-classification rules, curriculum-artifact handling, parent-facing disclosure, teacher opt-out boundaries, and logs that a human can actually read. Otherwise "teacher at the centre" becomes the new "personalised learning" — true in the slide deck, foggy in the room.

Vendor fluency training is not the same as institutional judgement. Google's AI Educator Series may be useful; it will almost certainly become common reference currency. But training people to use Gemini and NotebookLM is not the same as teaching them when to refuse a tool, how to inspect data flows, or how to recognise a retracted evidence claim when it reappears in a sales deck.

The safest-sounding product may be the one that touches the most sensitive non-student data. A curriculum assistant looks calmer than a gradebook breach because no one says "social security number." But curriculum is a school's intellectual infrastructure. Once AI systems help rewrite it, schools need governance language for teacher-authored and institution-authored knowledge, not just student records. That is the blind spot this week exposed.
