Thursday, 5 November 2026

Top story

An AI can understand a student's words and still mark down the accent.

A University of Washington account published on 4 November reports that audio models generally scored American and British English above Indian English, with Chinese and Nigerian English lowest, even when speakers delivered identical content and the models could transcribe it accurately. The research was presented on 25 October; this is a fresh institutional account of that work, rather than a claim that the experiment happened yesterday (University of Washington, 4 November).

The main analysis covers Gemini 2.5 Pro and Flash, GPT-Audio-1.5 and Qwen3-Omni. Synthetic voices supplied comparisons across hiring, presentations and English tests. A separate hiring exercise used 52 human speakers; it did not validate every task with people. The paper's methods and limitations also acknowledge small accent groups, including only four British speakers. These are experiments on general-purpose models, not an audit of a particular commercial examination service.

For international schools, the uncomfortable distinction is between recognising speech and judging its worth. A polished transcript can reassure a teacher that the system heard correctly. It cannot establish that a subsequent score reflects the intended learning. If the assessment concerns scientific reasoning, a penalty for sounding unfamiliar can enter through a comment on delivery and then acquire the apparent legitimacy of a rubric.

My reading is that oral assessment still has considerable value, especially when teachers want to hear how students arrived at an answer. Delegating its judgment deserves a separate decision. A useful trial would compare the same explanation as audio and as a transcript, inspect disagreement by language background, and keep teachers responsible for the final interpretation. That is a proposal for checking a tool, not a guarantee that one comparison removes bias. The important result here is narrower and more troubling: accurate transcription did not make evaluation fair.

Other stories

  • Treellis makes the generated object something a learner can take apart. The University of Sydney's 4 November account describes a browser-based, open-source 3D design interface developed with Cambridge and Singapore Management University. Users can select components, inspect spatial relationships and change dimensions or positions manually, alongside AI assistance. In the comparison described, participants built a cup, desk and monitor; researchers observed more exploration and reversible adjustment than with a prompt-only tool (University of Sydney, 4 November). This is a research interface and a university account, not evidence of durable classroom learning. The design lesson is still useful. A student changing a parameter can make a prediction, see the consequence and explain the mismatch. Asking an assistant for a nicer finished object provides much less information about the student's model of how it works. For a teacher evaluating an AI-built simulation or design tool, inspect whether the important relationships are available to manipulate. The educational opportunity lies in those relationships becoming testable, rather than in the number of objects the system can generate.

  • Google's classifier research starts by admitting the human definition may be unfinished. Agile Deliberation, described by Google Research on 4 November, helps people refine subjective image categories through borderline examples and feedback. The team evaluated a prototype in 18 live sessions lasting 1.5 hours, using healthy-food classification and a content-moderation category. Google reports better classifier performance and lower reported effort and stress than manual approaches; this is a small user study, not a general result about educational assessment (Google Research, 4 November). The connection to schools is an analogy worth trying carefully: people often discover what a criterion means by disagreeing over examples. A rubric's neat wording can conceal unresolved differences about what counts as sufficient evidence, independence or quality. Before asking AI to apply a criterion, teachers might compare several borderline pieces and record why they differ. The research does not validate an automated marking system. It offers a useful reminder that prompt refinement cannot repair a standard the humans have not yet agreed how to interpret. Sometimes the most productive AI discussion is the one that exposes our own uncertainty.

  • Europe's data-protection authorities have built a teaching resource hub, rather than another warning about children online. CNIL's dated 4 November announcement introduces the European Data Protection Board's Privacy for Kids platform for parents, teachers and educators. It brings together resources from European authorities, including videos, quizzes, comics, podcasts and games in different languages. Topics include cookies, rights online, privacy at school, cyberbullying, social media and connected toys (CNIL, 4 November). For international-school digital citizenship, the useful shift is from an instruction to be careful towards a specific decision a child can recognise. What information is being requested? Who can see it? What could be changed before sharing? Those questions can travel across jurisdictions, while legal explanations need local checking. A European resource does not establish a Vietnamese school's obligations. Nor does publishing a library show that pupils change their behaviour. Its immediate value is a supply of material teachers can inspect and adapt around an actual choice, with a regulator's perspective alongside the usual platform-produced safety lessons.

  • A Danish project will redesign six computing courses around what students still need to learn. IT University of Copenhagen's 3 November announcement describes PREPARE, a three-year collaboration with the University of Southern Denmark, funded with DKK 5 million from the Novo Nordisk Foundation. Researchers will redesign and evaluate six undergraduate and postgraduate software-engineering courses, including activities, materials and assessment. Participation requires formal institutional, teaching-staff and management approval (IT University of Copenhagen, 3 November). This is funding and a research programme, not a finding that the proposed approach works. The worthwhile question is the one beneath it: when working code becomes easy to obtain, which experiences develop the understanding needed to judge and maintain it? A school coding task can ask students to explain a failure, test a boundary or change a requirement and predict the consequences. Those are possible teaching responses, not results from this project. Watch for the actual assessment designs and evidence as they emerge. A grant earns the opportunity to investigate; it does not settle the answer in advance.

On the radar

  • UNESCO's climate-chatbot report has a date worth keeping. A press invitation published on 3 November and updated on 4 November previews UNESCO and University College London's work across up to 33 AI models and 13 languages. It says the study examines false climate information under user pushback, with a briefing scheduled for 16 November at COP31 (UNESCO, 3–4 November). The full report has not been reviewed here. For science and media-literacy teachers, the questions to bring to its release are which claims changed, under what prompts, and how performance differed by language. The announcement identifies a testable concern; the method and results will determine its classroom usefulness.

  • Google's new cloud notebooks put the expensive hardware behind an administrator's menu. GKE Workspaces, announced on 4 November, supplies persistent development environments on a team's Kubernetes infrastructure. Administrators curate software images and hardware choices; users can pause notebooks, retaining state while releasing compute hardware (Google Open Source Blog, 4 November). It is infrastructure for a platform team to deploy, not a free classroom notebook offer. The wider school-relevant pattern is worth watching: a simple learner or researcher interface can sit above tightly bounded resource choices. Whether that reduces cost depends on the whole deployment, including storage and operations. A pause button is useful evidence of cost-conscious design; it is not a budget on its own.

  • Three-Live takes AI-generated 3D code through a constrained runtime. Google's 3 November open-source post introduces a developer-preview library for 3D, augmented reality and WebXR across web and mobile. It describes removing network functions from the web scripting environment and enforcing execution-time and geometry limits. The post explicitly says the project is not an officially supported Google product (Google Open Source Blog, 3 November). For school prototypes, the useful detail is the boundary around generated code: what it may contact, how long it may run, and how much it may create. Those are author-described controls, not an independent security audit. Interesting experiments still need a check of their actual behaviour before students depend on them.

Sceptic's corner

The reassuring phrase to question today is human oversight. It describes a person somewhere in the process, while leaving almost everything about that person's job unspecified.

Consider an AI-generated score accompanied by fluent reasons. A teacher could read it, recognise several sensible observations and approve the whole judgment. The human has participated. Whether the teacher independently inspected the criterion that matters is another question. If unfamiliar pronunciation has quietly lowered a content score, a well-written explanation may make that error easier to accept.

The opposite reflex is no more useful: declaring every machine judgment unusable while assuming human judgments arrive free of bias. Teachers also need comparison, calibration and opportunities to reconsider. The practical issue is how the arrangement makes an error visible and gives someone both the evidence and authority to change the decision.

My test would be simple to state and demanding to run. Before seeing the AI's conclusion, can the reviewer form a view from the underlying work? When the two disagree, is there time to investigate? Can a student challenge the outcome through a process that does more than send the same evidence back to the same system?

Those questions turn oversight into work that can be observed. A final approval click cannot carry all of them. The presence of a person is a starting condition; the quality of that person's opportunity to judge is what deserves the confidence.

The Week That Was

The week kept returning to one gap: naming a problem is not the same as stopping it. New York's Education Department named 38 products, then said it was working with companies to find out whether the offending features could be disabled at all (Chalkbeat, 23 October). The UK's AI Security Institute described an agent that often said an attack would be harmful in the very situations where it went ahead (AISI, 23 October). Gemini's immersive explorations reached the student hub in Education editions with the line "There is no admin control for this feature" (Google, 28 October).

Since Friday's brief: whole-deck editing with Gemini in Slides, which schools get through the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, carries the same line (Google, 30 October). For schools, that makes the add-on licence the only switch. OpenAI said ChatGPT will restrict practical weapons information for teens even without signs of violent intent, end chats that seem fixated on violence, and may pause accounts after repeated ones, telling parents when a teen's is paused. Rollout starts within weeks (Reuters via AOL, 30 October).

What Does It All Mean?

Every story above assumes a lever somewhere. So the question worth a Sunday: when a school cannot switch a feature off, what is its AI rule actually made of?

Start with the lever schools think they have. Among American teens who use AI for schoolwork, Common Sense Media found in August that 44% had had an AI tool blocked on a school network or device, and 59% of those simply moved to a personal device, where there is even less support (Common Sense Media, 18 August). A network block is a detour, not a wall.

Assessment researchers have been making this case for a while. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu split institutional responses into discursive changes, which tell students what is allowed, and structural ones, which change how the task itself works. Traffic-light permission schemes, they argue, borrow the look of a real enforcement system without its power, and leaning on rules like these creates what they call an "enforcement illusion" (Corbin, Dawson and Liu, 2025).

The University of Sydney, where Liu works, had already written the idea into policy: from 2025, AI allowed by default except in exams and in-semester tests, then two lanes (ACS Information Age, December 2024). Secured, supervised tasks leave AI to the examiner; everything else is open, with students guided in its use. Its FAQ is blunt that AI use cannot be restricted in assessments not secured face to face, nor reliably detected (Teaching@Sydney, updated April 2026).

Even the AI Assessment Scale's own authors agree. Leon Furze, one of them, writes that assessment security is not what the scale aims at; it is a framework to support assessment design, and a colourful handout plus "a hopeful expectation" secures nothing (Furze, September 2025).

That would be bleak if rules kept students honest. They never did. A follow-up study published in February, surveying 4,354 students at six US high schools about eighteen months after ChatGPT's release, found an overall cheating rate of 72.06%, which its authors call consistent with historical baselines; AI availability does not appear to have changed overall prevalence (Chen et al., February 2026). The tool changed the method, not the rate. Whatever sets the rate, whether engagement, pressure or a task that feels worth doing, sits upstream of any switch.

So the working answer: an AI rule rests on three layers, in falling order of reliability. First, what is structurally true of the task: done in the room, staged, defended aloud. Second, what the school can actually switch, which is coarse and covers products more than features. Third, what it tells students, which is invaluable for conversation and useless as security.

For a school running an AI use scale, the practical move is small. The scale stays as the shared language. But every task pitched at the no-AI or low-AI end needs a one-line answer to "what makes this true in the room?" If the honest answer is that the student promised, the task is open and should be marked as one.

Behind You! Behind You!

New York will treat unverified chatbot users as children. Governor Hochul signed S.9051B/A.10379C on 28 October. In the coming months, companies may not offer under-18s, or any user not confirmed as an adult, features such as posing as human, claiming a relationship, asking unprompted emotional questions or using a child's private details from earlier chats. The attorney general can seek up to $25,000 per violation (New York State, 28 October). The state's Business Council had argued that educational tools were swept in (TechTimes, 29 October). Watch whether vendors build that default once and ship it everywhere.

States want proof and can't get it. SETDA's 2026 State EdTech Trends report, published 29 October from 73 survey responses, found 79% of state leaders naming AI a top technology priority. Yet 52% said their state collects no data on the use or effectiveness of ed-tech products, and 68% said independently verifying vendor claims about AI products is challenging (GovTech, 29 October). If American states with procurement offices struggle, an international school is checking alone.

Half of the US schools and districts surveyed had a cyber incident last year. The Center for Internet Security's 2026 K-12 report puts it at 51% in the past 12 months, and 66% of districts leave cybersecurity to one or two people (CIS, 27 October). Phishing or email compromise ranked among the top incidents for 87% of leaders, and the report warns of more convincing AI-enabled phishing (K-12 Dive, 27 October). The weak point is staff inboxes, not servers.

The ed-tech backlash is on the ballot, and it isn't partisan. Ahead of Tuesday's US midterms, Chalkbeat found a Republican candidate for California governor promising to ban AI in schools on day one. In Texas, the Republican governor pledges less classroom screen time and his Democratic challenger opposes AI for elementary students. Seven of eight San Francisco school board candidates back limits on classroom technology (Chalkbeat, 26 October). International schools enrolling families from those systems should expect the mood to travel with them.

Sceptic's Corner

Next time someone says the research shows AI improves learning, ask which research.

Jill Barshay reports 39 meta-analyses on AI in education, every one finding substantial benefits. Patrick O'Neill, a community college professor, audited 14 of them and found serious conceptual and statistical flaws, from hallucinated references to underlying studies citing generative-AI papers published before the technology was public. One, published in a Nature journal in May 2025, has since been retracted (Hechinger Report, 26 October).

When O'Neill pooled 44 well-designed experiments, outcomes ranged from very helpful to very harmful, and his preliminary calculations put the average at roughly zero. Susanna Loeb's team gathered almost 900 studies and judged only 20 good enough for its evidence summary. O'Neill's audit is itself a preprint and his numbers are preliminary, so hold him to the same standard.

Two things follow. A literature that is uniformly positive is a warning, not a comfort; real interventions produce null results. An average of roughly zero is not a verdict of harmlessness. It is what you get when good designs and bad ones cancel out, which is why the question is always which use, for whom, checked how.

The line for a staff meeting: there is no effect size for "AI", only for particular uses of it. Anyone quoting one number is quoting a brochure.
