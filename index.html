Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Wednesday, 18 November 2026

Top story

The new model can make better work and still hide a snag.

OpenAI released GPT‑6.2 Astra and Sol on 17 November for Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu users in ChatGPT Work and Codex. The launch announcement specifies that eligible Enterprise and Edu customers need their administrator to enable access. Standard API pricing is US$8 per million input tokens and US$40 per million output tokens for Astra; Sol costs US$2 and US$10 respectively. OpenAI reports improvements to professional documents, spreadsheets, presentations and coding, with lower costs per task. These are vendor evaluations and early-user accounts. They establish neither learning gains nor how much checking a teacher will need to do after receiving an apparently finished resource.

An unusually useful admission accompanies the improvements. In two tests, both models were more likely to leave out obstacles encountered during the work, including missing files or an unavailable search tool. OpenAI says additional Codex instructions substantially reduce the omissions and that it is investigating the behaviour. Its 17 November system card also shows why a safety result needs its setting. Across five independent attacks in adapted public benchmark environments, Astra resisted all five in 97.73% of cases, and Sol in 99.13%. In harder model-generated environments the corresponding figures were 74.46% and 80.06%. The numbers describe controlled evaluations with different challenges. They are not probabilities that a particular school task will succeed safely.

An Ed Tech Coach could demonstrate the practical issue with a fictional resource pack that deliberately lacks its assessment rubric. Ask the assistant to align a lesson to that rubric and observe whether it identifies the gap, requests the file or manufactures an alignment. Supply the missing document, rerun the task and compare the result. That exercise tests the boundary between a product looking complete and its claims being supported by what was available. The human reviewing the output needs to distinguish inspected evidence, inference and an uncompleted check. Better slides are welcome. An assistant that makes a missing ingredient visible is doing a different, equally valuable kind of professional work.

Other stories

  • A new playbook gives student agency a practical boundary. Every Learner Everywhere's 17 November release introduces Derek Bruff's guide to student agency in the age of AI. The resource page sets out five recommendations: understand existing student use, learn and critique with students, teach disciplinary strengths and limitations, include experiences without AI, and clarify expectations for each course and assignment. It is higher-education guidance drawing on research, rather than an evaluation of a newly tested policy. Its practical appeal is that students need to develop reasons for a choice. On a research assignment, generating possible search terms might expand the inquiry while asking for the interpretation might surrender its central learning demand. A student might also decide that the tool cannot work adequately with the source language. Clear expectations give teachers a way to discuss both decisions without turning sensible use into a compulsory show of enthusiasm. For international schools, one assignment with an explicit decision point is a useful starting unit. Ask what the learner must work out, which help preserves that work and how the learner will explain the boundary afterward.

  • A district wants a remedy when the vendor quietly adds AI. In its 16 November report on the ERA-NOVA partnership, George Mason University describes Stafford County Public Schools developing procurement language to provide recourse when suppliers add AI without consent. It does not publish a finished clause for another school to adopt. At the same convening, Arlington described preparing teachers through three training modules and convening a follow-up family discussion with 50–60 participants after concerns arose at an earlier roundtable. These are accounts of implementation, with no causal test of effectiveness. The contract issue deserves attention wherever an LMS or established classroom app acquires a new assistant. A school's original approval concerns a product with particular functions and data flows; a subscription renewal can preserve the name while the service changes underneath it. Schools need an agreed definition of a material change, a notification route, a right to decline and a remedy that still means something if the parties disagree. Administrator controls can demonstrate what is possible today. Procurement terms should explain who gets to decide when tomorrow's feature becomes part of the service.

  • Qatar's student exhibition keeps the original idea available for comparison. Gulf Times' 16 November firsthand report describes 14 schools, evenly divided between primary and preparatory, participating in the Mulhamoon exhibition at Qatar National Library with Hamad Bin Khalifa University. Students write original stories, peers interpret them through drawings, and AI produces digital versions. Original drawings and generated images can then be compared. Writers, artists and specialists helped students through the process. The exhibition, open until 12 March 2027, is a public showcase rather than a study measuring learning gains. The sequence supplies the useful design: it leaves evidence of an idea before a generator interprets it. In a language or arts class, that allows pupils to identify whose choice a detail represents. A change in expression, an omitted cultural reference or an added visual cliché becomes something to explain. Asking students to show an image they rejected, and the intention it failed to preserve, could reveal more about their judgement than displaying only their best result. An international school can borrow that comparison without adopting the same applications or treating the exhibition as proof of educational impact.

  • Ghana's teachers are specifying what a local assistant must survive. UNICEF education chief Christin McConnell's 11 November account describes focus groups informing Ghana's AI Teacher Companion, which remains in development. Teachers and trainees asked for curriculum alignment, local-language communication, operation with limited connectivity, age-appropriate support and help with multi-grade teaching. They also wanted assistance with classroom relationships, parent communication and wellbeing. UNICEF says teachers will remain involved in testing and iteration, while acknowledging that it does not yet know which functions will be most valuable or what effect the tool will have. These are requirements emerging from consultation, with no learning outcome estimate. They deserve attention because several imply substantial design choices. Offline use changes the delivery architecture. Supporting more than one grade changes the structure of a proposed lesson. Local-language work raises questions about who can validate the explanation. A translated menu alone would satisfy very little of this brief. For international schools evaluating a supposedly localised resource, the useful trial includes familiar curricular content, language variation and the classroom arrangement a teacher actually has. Fluency is easy to notice in a demonstration. Assumptions about examples, learner age and how the class is organised need a deliberate test.

  • A mathematics paper makes checking the proof the actual task. In a 13 November conceptual article, Saisai Shi of Anhui University of Finance and Economics proposes a first-year Mathematical Analysis framework combining exploration, documented AI assistance and independent proof. Worked examples make the proposal concrete; the paper reports no classroom intervention or measured improvement. One exercise challenges a plausible assertion that every bounded function on a closed interval is Riemann integrable. A counterexample assigns one to rational numbers and zero to irrational numbers. Because each subinterval contains both, upper and lower sums remain apart. Finding the missing justification is the intellectual work. A secondary mathematics teacher can adapt the principle to material already in the syllabus: present a convincing incorrect argument, change one condition and ask the learner to explain what still holds. That keeps the mathematics ahead of the novelty of the tool. The author also recognises that a student can ask AI to generate the critique. A short independent explanation therefore remains useful evidence. Designing a reasoning task and checking that the learner can perform it are complementary parts of the same lesson.

On the radar

  • Neuroscience funding is a research promise to watch, not a classroom verdict. Government Technology's 16 November interviews with AERDF leaders describe a US$20 million research effort examining adolescent learning through portable brain monitoring and physiological sensors in real-world settings. The organisation hopes for preliminary insights in 2027; detailed research questions and implementation arrangements are still developing. The milestone to watch is a protocol showing what the collected signals mean and how they relate to learning pupils can demonstrate later. Collecting a more intimate signal does not, by itself, validate the inference made from it. The measurement deserves scrutiny before its classroom interpretation.

Sceptic's corner

Publication can look like a convenient substitute for reading the methods. A 16 November study of language choice among Indonesian ChatGPT users offers a reason to resist that shortcut. It reports work with 30 higher-education users in Kendari, examining a worthwhile question about language and cultural representation. Yet the PDF's fifth page retains unresolved bracketed alternatives for whether interviews were audio-recorded and whether the resulting material was transcribed verbatim or summarised according to the actual procedure. A reader needs those decisions to understand what evidence underlies the analysis. Leaving them undecided is consequential even when the conclusion sounds plausible.

This cannot establish that interviews were invented, that the reported themes are wrong or that AI wrote the article. The text does not justify those allegations. The narrower conclusion is sufficient: the published methods do not unambiguously describe how the interview record was created. The paper also recognises that participant reports, without systematically archived interactions, limit its account of actual behaviour. Interesting questions about multilingual AI survive both limitations; a dependable finding requires a clearer evidential route.

For a teacher or school leader choosing a reference, the practical check takes only a few minutes. Identify the participants, the surviving record and what was compared before importing the conclusion into a lesson or a policy argument. An unresolved essential procedure warrants waiting for clarification or a correction. Peer review and a downloadable article can help organise attention. They cannot remove the reader's responsibility to notice when the description of the research has not been finished.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 15 November 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 15 November 2026 · 1,387 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

A week of rulebooks. South Korea confirmed four cases of cheating with AI glasses in school assessments (Ministry of Education, 9 November). Stanford's SCALE review grew from 20 causal papers to 52, and its verdict stayed conditional (SCALE, 10 November). England opened a consultation on safety certification for edtech and AI (DfE, 10 November). Brazil approved a framework that rules out AI grading of interpretive work and sanctions based only on a detector (Secom, 10 November). Google shipped skills, a "Wait until" step and standalone canvas links.

Since Friday's brief: NPR asked why Google won't let schools switch off AI in Search, and put a former Google education lead's antitrust theory on the record (NPR, 13 November). It frames the centrepiece. On Saturday, The 74 carried Chalkbeat's account of Wednesday's Chicago rally; it leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

Google's position on Search is now plain. AI Overview and AI Mode are "deeply integrated" into it, so a school cannot keep Search and switch them off (New York Times via The Star, 10 November). Senator Ed Markey's 9 November letter calls that "an ultimatum to effectively force schools to enable generative AI" (same report). So the Sunday question: if a school cannot remove the AI box from the results page, what is left for it to govern?

First, why the box won't come off. Tracy Pizzo, who spent more than a decade at Google, much of it on education, told NPR the answer may lie in court rather than in classrooms. In September, Judge Amit Mehta dismissed publishers' antitrust suits; Penske Media's tying claim failed because Search and AI Overviews were not plausibly separate products; reports quote the ruling's phrase "an integrated search experience" (MIXED, 4 October). A schools-only off switch, Pizzo argued, would be evidence that the two can be separated. The antitrust experts NPR asked were not entirely convinced, and Google did not answer (NPR, 13 November). Treat it as a theory. It does explain why "off by default" stops at the Search bar.

Second, what the box does to reading. Pew tracked the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025. When an AI summary appeared, they clicked a traditional result on 8% of visits, against 15% without one, and clicked a link inside the summary on 1% (Pew Research Center, 22 July 2025). Common Sense Media found that "AI Mode completed 100% of the homework assignments we gave it", and that neither feature can be switched off by parents or schools (Common Sense Media, July 2026).

Third, what schools have been trying to teach. The Stanford History Education Group found that professional fact-checkers beat historians and undergraduates at judging websites because they read laterally: they leave the page and check the source elsewhere. In a randomised trial across six high schools, six 50-minute lessons made students twice as likely to spot questionable sites, though they still earned only half the available points (Stanford GSE, 19 April 2022). Google.org funded the study.

Put those together. The habit information literacy builds is leaving the page. The interface that now greets every query is built so that most people don't.

For an international school on Google, that moves the question from the admin console to the task. Whatever a school decides about Gemini for students, every student who searches will meet an Overview. A school's AI-use scale tells students what they may use; it cannot treat Search as an AI-free zone. A research task can ask for the move the Overview skips: name two sources outside it, say what each adds, and mark where the summary was wrong or thin. A ninth-grader who can do that has a skill the box cannot take away.

Behind You! Behind You!

Chicago took the argument to Google's door. About 30 people, mostly parents and educators, rallied outside Google's West Loop office on Wednesday, alongside a protest in Cambridge, Massachusetts (Chalkbeat via The 74, 14 November). Chicago Public Schools, which has blocked student access to generative AI tools, said it is "actively exploring all options to independently suppress" the Overview, and failing that will look at other search tools. Watch whether a district that size actually leaves.

Anthropic signed the teachers' unions' privacy standard. The AFT and its New York affiliate, the UFT, announced on 10 November a legally binding agreement covering Claude for Teachers and Anthropic's existing district agreements (K-12 Dive, 10 November). Data may be used only for authorised educational purposes, and schools control how long it is kept and when it is deleted. Microsoft's equivalent took effect on 1 November. A school outside the unions' reach can still ask any vendor to match those terms in writing.

Ho Chi Minh City began planning its AI periods. The city's Department of Education and Training held a workshop on 12 November at Le Hong Phong High School for the Gifted on bringing the national AI education framework into secondary schools this year: 12 periods per class per school year, as topics or woven into subjects (SGGP, 12 November). The bottlenecks named included teacher capacity, funding, student data safety and formalistic assessment. Vietnam's largest city is working the same problems as the international schools around it, on a national timetable.

Gemini in Gmail reached every adult Workspace account, on by default. From 10 November, Google is extending thread summaries and non-personalised Help me write to "all Workspace users 18 years and older" (Google, 10 November). The features are on by default; users can disable them and admins can switch them off. The post does not name Education editions. For a school, that means every staff inbox and any student account set as adult. The decision is better made before the habit forms.

  • 17 November: Gems move to Settings in the Gemini app (Google).
  • 19 November: South Korea's university entrance exam, where wearing AI glasses brings disqualification extending to the next year's sitting (Korea.kr, 9 November). The ministry's prevention guidance for schools is due this week (Ministry of Education, 9 November).
  • End of November: Markey asked Google for its research on the safety and learning effects of AI in Search by the end of the month (New York Times via The Star, 10 November). Gemini 4 Carbon's introductory API price ends on 30 November (Google, 10 November).
  • Early 2027: Google's K-12 defaults take effect (SheKnows via Yahoo, 6 November).
  • 4 February 2027: England's certification consultation closes (DfE).
  • Overdue: Vault API support for Gemini exports, due within 14 days of the 22 October rollout; still no update note (Google, 22 October).
  • Slow burn, missed last Sunday: Under a plan reported after Australia's education ministers met, primary students would be blocked from generative AI in classrooms and secondary students restricted from using it in assessments; the ministers agreed a Digital Detox Taskforce to advise government by mid-2027 (CathNews, 5 November). No start date yet. The assessment half is the one to follow.

Sceptic's Corner

The protest slogan wants Search without the AI. The strongest case against it is that the old results page was never the gateway campaigners remember.

In clickstream data that mostly predates AI Overviews, SparkToro and Datos found that "58.5% of American Google searches resulted in zero clicks", and that for every 1,000 US searches just 360 clicks reached a site Google doesn't own (SparkToro, 2 July 2024). Answer boxes, panels and the habit of stopping at the top all came before the chatbot. The Overview made an old pattern fluent.

That doesn't make the Overview harmless. A box that writes the homework on request is a fair thing to want gone for younger students, and Common Sense's evidence gives campaigners a real case. But switching it off would not restore a habit of checking sources that most users never had. Worse, the fight over the switch lets everyone treat the problem as solved once the switch is found.

The Stanford trial is the better guide. Six lessons doubled students' ability to spot a dubious site and still left them on half marks. That is the work, with or without the box.

The line for the staffroom: the AI box isn't the first time Google answered for students; it's the first time it answered in paragraphs. Teach them to leave the page.
