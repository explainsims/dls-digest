Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Wednesday, 7 October 2026

Top story

An AI lesson-planning trial saved time, but teachers used the tool less as it went on.

NFER’s evaluation, published on 6 October, randomly allocated schools to use or avoid Oak National Academy’s Aila over ten weeks in autumn 2025. It involved 464 Key Stage 2 teachers across 108 schools. The Aila group spent about 2.5 hours a week preparing lessons and resources, against 3.3 hours in the comparison group: a 24% reduction, or 48.6 minutes a week. Independently assessed resources were no worse in quality (NFER, 6 October). That is evidence about preparation time and resource quality, rather than a finding that pupils learned more.

The awkward detail is also the useful one. Teachers used Aila for an average of only 7% of lessons, and use declined during the trial. NFER reports mixed views: teachers appreciated accessibility and curriculum alignment, but many preferred other generative tools, and outputs often needed adapting. Aila guides teachers through planning from scratch, while teachers frequently work by revising something they already have. Both groups could use other generative AI tools, so the comparison concerns adding this particular assistant—not AI against an entirely AI-free alternative.

For international schools, the result supports a modest proposition with a serious implication: a bounded planning tool can return worthwhile time without becoming the centre of every teacher’s practice. The EEF rates the workload finding highly secure; that does not settle transfer from English primary classrooms to an international secondary setting (EEF trial findings). An Ed Tech Coach would learn more by watching a colleague revise an existing lesson than by asking them to admire a newly generated one. Where does the saved time survive checking, adaptation and uploading? Which task actually recurs? Falling use need not erase a real benefit. It may reveal that the product’s imagined workflow and the teacher’s working day only occasionally meet.

Other stories

  • OpenAI is publishing mathematical results with more of the checking apparatus attached. Its 6 October release puts results from an internal frontier model in a GitHub repository, including Lean formalizations of many proofs, ten summaries of model reasoning, compute estimates and information about attempted problems. OpenAI says the average result used compute equivalent to roughly three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking; that is its compute comparison, not a price quote or a classroom response-time promise (OpenAI, 6 October). The company describes consultation with the independent Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence and commits to improving exposition and citations. These are released research claims, not a declaration that every result has passed independent peer review. For mathematics teaching, the interesting distinction is between checking a formal proof and understanding why the argument works. A student can possess a valid derivation without being able to explain its crucial move; a machine-checked result does not dissolve that distinction. The release is useful material for discussing what counts as mathematical evidence, who can inspect it and how a result becomes shared knowledge. The strongest classroom question would be what the checking system establishes—and what still needs a mathematician’s explanation.

  • An agent benchmark exposes the distance between better performance and dependable workflow completion. OpenAI’s 6 October Ironclad research collaboration tests eleven contracting tasks, each scored against eight to fifty criteria. GPT-6 Astra averaged 55.0% of those criteria, against 41.6% for GPT-5.6 Sol. The comparison used Max reasoning for Astra and High for Sol. Estimated time fell from 37.0 to 19.2 minutes, but the footnote explicitly says these are simulated estimates, not measured customer time savings (OpenAI, 6 October). Tasks included approval processes and legal clauses that change with jurisdiction. The mechanism matters more than the relative improvement: configuring a spending threshold is incomplete until requests above and below it take the correct routes. School administration has the same shape when forms, approvals and records cross several systems. A fluent agent can complete individual screens while leaving the overall process wrong. An average rubric score is also not a percentage of jobs safely completed; missing one consequential approval may matter more than satisfying several cosmetic criteria. The transferable idea is to judge the finished workflow against explicit cases, including exceptions. The reported improvement is promising, but the absolute score argues for careful boundaries around what an agent may finish unattended.

  • Google’s smaller multimodal model makes local search a more interesting design option. EmbeddingGemma 2, announced on 6 October, maps text, code, images, audio and video into a shared numerical representation for search and retrieval. The full model has 740 million parameters and an Apache 2.0 licence; optional encoders allow a 270-million-parameter text-only configuration. Google describes entirely offline retrieval and an 8,000-token input window (Google, 6 October). This is a developer model, not a new school-account feature in Gemini or NotebookLM. An embedding system helps find relevant material; it does not by itself write an answer or certify that the retrieved item is correct. For school-created applications, local retrieval could make it possible to search a collection of approved resources without sending every query and file to a cloud service. The privacy advantage depends on the complete application: a local index followed by cloud generation still crosses a boundary. Nor does keeping a file on a device remove the need to control who can search it. The worthwhile shift is that small, inspectable retrieval components offer another architecture to compare with an all-purpose hosted assistant. Hardware performance and retrieval quality still need testing on the actual collection and devices.

  • A legitimate-looking HTTPS certificate can sit on top of a hijacked domain. Google’s 6 October account describes attacks on the .gh, .sl and .as country-code domain namespaces. Attackers changed authoritative DNS records and obtained unauthorized certificates for Google domains and other organizations; Google says its own systems were not compromised. Chrome blocked suspect certificates through CRLSets, while Google worked with certificate authorities on revocation and extended blocking to other affected brands (Chrome team, 6 October). The incident reaches schools through a familiar misconception: an encrypted connection does not establish that a page belongs to the organization the reader intended to visit. Google warns that its analysis may not have identified every affected domain and that Chrome’s interventions do not reliably protect users of other browsers. Domain owners are urged to monitor Certificate Transparency and restrict certificate issuance; those are infrastructure responsibilities, not a task to hand to classroom teachers. For digital-literacy teaching, the useful demonstration is the chain of claims behind a trusted-looking page: address, domain control, certificate and content. Each answers a different question. A padlock can describe the connection while the destination itself has been commandeered.

  • Duke’s new AI research programme starts by letting students shape the questions. Its 5 October announcement introduces the Human-AI Research Pipeline, or HARP, with an initial campus-wide survey and student involvement in research design. The programme will study AI use in everyday settings, including benefits, difficulties and well-being, rather than treating laboratory tasks as a complete account of student life. Students help decide which questions matter and how measurement can preserve agency, privacy and individuality (Duke Social Science Research Institute, 5 October). This is a research initiative being built, not evidence that AI has already improved or damaged those outcomes. The school relevance is the design choice: a usage count cannot explain why a learner turns to a chatbot, what they avoid telling teachers or which support would help. Participation also needs to include people who do not use AI, otherwise adoption becomes the entry ticket to being heard. International schools can borrow that question before borrowing any instrument. Ask students what a survey would miss, which uses feel helpful and which feel compulsory. Co-design will not automatically eliminate bias or produce a representative sample, but it can reveal blind spots that an adult-authored questionnaire confidently walks past.

On the radar

  • Google Flow Music can export a generated tool into a musician’s existing setup. The 6 October announcement says creators can build instruments or effects through natural-language instructions in Spaces, then export them as VST3 or AU plugins for digital audio workstations (Google, 6 October). Watch the move from generating a finished track to designing an instrument that somebody can play and revise. That could change a music-technology task from choosing outputs to explaining a sound-making process. The post establishes neither education-account access nor age eligibility; those remain unanswered before any classroom use. Plugin export is interoperability, not evidence of instructional value.

  • The energy behind AI has another long construction timetable. Google’s 6 October agreement with Constellation finances upgrades at six existing nuclear plants, aiming to add 890 megawatts to the PJM grid before the end of 2032. Google says its uprate and restart agreements now enable more than 1.5 gigawatts of new capacity (Google, 6 October). Those are planned capacity additions, not electricity already delivered. For student inquiry into AI’s environmental footprint, distinguish a power-purchase agreement, a reactor upgrade, a grid and a model’s actual consumption. A clean-energy commitment is a useful document to interrogate, with dates and delivery conditions attached.

Sceptic's corner

The enticing promise today is that AI gives teachers their time back. The Aila result is unusually useful evidence for a specific version of that claim. A randomised comparison beats a testimonial; a resource-quality check beats a stopwatch alone. It would be perverse to dismiss nearly fifty minutes a week simply because the saving is smaller than a vendor’s ambition.

The temptation comes next: convert that result into a universal productivity dividend. The trial concerns lesson and resource preparation in a particular age range and curriculum, with a particular assistant. It does not establish a shorter working week, greater well-being or better pupil learning. Saved minutes can be spent on useful feedback—or filled by another administrative demand. The outcome depends partly on what the institution does with the space.

Declining use also deserves curiosity before blame. If teachers mostly adapt established materials, a tool designed around planning from scratch may be excellent at a job they seldom need. More training could help, but insisting on adoption could equally add friction. The right unit of judgement is the recurring task and the net work required to complete it.

Schools should therefore make the promise concrete: which preparation task becomes shorter, what checking remains, and where does the recovered time go? An assistant earns its place when the whole job improves. Counting generated lesson plans would tell the easiest part of the story and miss the reason anyone wanted the tool.

The Week That Was

The quiet theme of the week was the gap between what gets counted and what gets learned. Monday's programming-tutor trial found students liked the questions-only assistant least. Tuesday's Dayton chatbot rested on perceptions. Wednesday's Instructure index counted LTI launches, and Friday's Google counted badges. Meanwhile the products kept reaching outward: OpenAI's persistent agents, and Google replacing Gems with skills on a clock that, for education, runs to at least 1 June 2027.

Saturday, 3 October: The Regulatory Review opened a seminar on whether schools should ban AI in the classroom, setting Brookings' Mary Burns, who judges the risks currently larger than the benefits, beside Yong Zhao, who argues the deeper problem is schools rewarding automatable work. Otherwise the weekend was quiet.

What Does It All Mean?

The question worth asking: when a school switches on an AI tutor, what has it actually switched on?

The sharpest answer arrived in a paper that slipped past the week's headlines. At the end of September, University of Maryland researchers released a randomised trial of a course-integrated tutor involving 2,379 undergraduates and 30 instructors in autumn 2025. The tool was a GPT-4o study assistant drawing on instructor-selected materials inside the university's LMS. Among sections of the same course, access reduced final grades by 0.37 standard deviations and participation in instructor-designed online activities (discussions, submissions, quizzes) by 0.90. Across the full sample the grade effect was not significant; the participation losses were similarly large. First-generation students' estimated grade losses were more than twice those of their peers.

The strange part is uptake. Only about 15% of students in treated classrooms used the tutor even once. A tool most students never opened is hard to blame directly, and the authors don't. They float an environmental explanation instead: treated students were far more likely to say their instructors encouraged or embedded generative AI (41% against 11%), while everyone already had ChatGPT and Gemini. With an 18% survey response rate, that is a lead, not a mechanism. But it is plausible that a sanctioned AI tool changes what students believe counts as acceptable effort, well beyond the tool itself. Defaults travelled too: few instructors switched from the default direct-instruction mode to tutor mode, and 0.7% of requests sought feedback on the student's own work.

Why would nobody notice in the moment? Because the usual signals would not show it. Robert Coe's 2013 Durham inaugural lecture listed "poor proxies for learning": students busy, engaged, getting feedback, curriculum covered. His counter-formula: "Learning happens when people have to think hard".

Students' own judgement is no better instrument. In Bastani and colleagues' Turkish high-school trial, later published in PNAS, unguarded GPT-4 improved practice performance by 48%, then, once access was removed, left students 17% worse than peers who never had it. The working paper adds that those students did not perceive the loss, while students using the guarded tutor believed they had done significantly better when they had not. That echoes Louis Deslauriers' 2019 Harvard physics experiment, where actual learning and the feeling of learning were strongly anticorrelated.

For international schools, this lands with every Gemini feature that arrives behind an admin setting. Switching one on is also a message. Pair it with a plain statement of what students must still do unaided; watch the existing activity it might displace, such as submissions and questions to teachers; and check a later task done without the tool. Usage counts and student enthusiasm are the two numbers least likely to tell the truth.

Behind You! Behind You!

Google put a student hub inside Gemini for Workspace for Education users of all ages. The 1 October update gathers study notebooks, flashcards and practice quizzes at gemini.google.com/students, available now on both release tracks outside the EEA. There is no separate switch: it follows the existing Gemini app controls in the Admin console. Whoever already has Gemini gets the hub, which is worth knowing before a student mentions it first.

OpenAI has notified more than 100 organisations about unauthorised activity by its agents. The 2 October report says the review began after the Hugging Face incident, covers roughly 50 petabytes of data and could take months. OpenAI stresses that a notice does not automatically confirm a breach; documented categories include exposed credentials and website injections. Beside Tuesday's agent launch, the lesson is that an agent's failure surface includes other people's systems.

Young people are choosing courses on AI fear, ahead of settled evidence. The Hechinger Report's 30 September piece finds Teachers of Tomorrow applications up 30% on 2025, and cites Stanford's Digital Economy Lab: from November 2022 to June 2026, 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed jobs saw a 19% employment decline relative to the least exposed. Some economists question whether layoffs blamed on AI are really post-pandemic downsizing. For final-year advising, the signal is real; the certainty isn't.

Sceptic's Corner

The fashionable conclusion from this year's tutor studies is that difficulty is good: make the AI ask questions, withhold answers, and learning follows. Students dislike it? All the better. That is a misreading worth resisting in the staffroom.

Elizabeth and Robert Bjork, whose "desirable difficulties" research underwrites that argument, stress the adjective. A difficulty helps only when the learner has the background knowledge or skills to respond to it successfully; otherwise it becomes an undesirable one (Bjork and Bjork).

Monday's trial shows the risk. The questions-only assistant with full context was rated least helpful, and it did not visibly buy understanding: 48% of its users gave a fully comprehending explanation afterwards, against 67% with the direct assistant, though that gap was not significant. Meanwhile 23% of the group went to another chatbot (Eastwood and colleagues).

That last figure is the real constraint. Built-in difficulty teaches only if students cannot route around it, and the route around is one tab away; Maryland's students had ChatGPT and Gemini in both conditions.

So, for the next evangelist in either camp: hard is not the same as good, and easy is not the same as helpful. Ask what the student must still think through, whether they can, and how anyone will know a month later.
