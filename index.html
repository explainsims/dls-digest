Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Tuesday, 10 November 2026

Top story

Smart glasses have reached the exam room.

South Korea's education ministry reported on 9 November that a nationwide survey of middle and high schools had confirmed four cases of cheating involving AI-enabled glasses in internal assessments this academic year. One was in middle school and three were in high school. Two had already been reported in the press. All four students received zero for the affected subject exam and the three high schools imposed separate disciplinary measures. The ministry plans to circulate prevention guidance and student materials in the third week of November, before second-semester finals.

A separate 9 November notice for the 19 November national university entrance exam names smart glasses alongside phones, smartwatches and Bluetooth earphones. Wearing AI glasses during the exam carries disqualification extending to the next year's sitting. The same notice allows devices needed for medical or physical reasons, such as hearing aids and continuous glucose monitors, after an invigilator checks them before each session. The survey and the notice answer different questions. One counts the incidents schools could confirm. The other tells candidates and invigilators what to do before a high-stakes sitting.

For a secondary school, the immediate question is whether the instructions at the door match the decision a proctor is expected to make. Four confirmed cases cannot tell us how common the practice is. They are enough to test whether a rule written around phones reaches a device worn on the face. A useful instruction covers devices that can record, receive or display assistance, gives students a confidential route to arrange an approved accommodation, and says who handles an uncertain case. A student should be able to find out in advance what they can wear, and the proctor should have someone to call without pausing the exam for the entire room.

Other stories

  • A study will test what AI can infer from a teacher's account of student thinking. In 9 November reporting by EdSurge, Rowan University's Molly Fisher and University of Kentucky colleagues describe plans to recruit about 100 practising and 50 prospective elementary maths teachers. Expert raters will score how teachers attend to, interpret and respond to students' thinking. Claude, ChatGPT and a third tool still to be selected will score the same de-identified written responses. Teachers will watch classroom video for two of the three assessments. No student footage goes to the AI tools. Teacher data collection is planned for spring 2027. For secondary instructional coaching, the privacy choice has a side benefit. A colleague can discuss a short, de-identified account of a wrong answer without posting footage of the child who gave it. Ask the teacher what the student did, what else might explain it, and what question would distinguish the two explanations. The study is still at the design stage, and that conversation is already available.

  • The Philippines began handing out teacher laptops, with support in the purchase. The Philippine News Agency reported on 9 November that the first handover in Dagupan City delivered 209 MacBook Neo units to 10 public schools. The national allocation for teachers is 231,725 units, comprising 169,008 MacBook Neos and 62,717 Windows laptops. A separate 3,646 MacBook Neos are for administrative staff. The package includes productivity software, delivery, training and a five-year warranty. Suppliers have until March 2027 to complete deliveries, and the department says it will prioritise schools and teachers who have yet to receive government-issued laptops, particularly those leading ed-tech work. For school technology teams, the informative line item is support. A warranty can cover a repair, while a usable rollout still needs an answer for the teacher who has a lesson in ten minutes and a machine that will not start. Spare devices, repair turnaround and the staff time needed to learn the new workflows belong beside the quoted unit price when a school compares bids.

  • Google put a one-click confirmation on automatically labelled files. Its 9 November Workspace update adds separate Accept and Edit buttons to the banner in Drive, Docs, Sheets and Slides when one classification label was applied automatically and the user has permission to change it. When several labels are applied, the banner still sends people to review them. The rollout began on 9 November, with up to 15 days for visibility. There is no new admin control for the confirmation feature. Education Standard and Plus are listed for default classification, while Google's AI classification has separate eligibility, including the Google AI Pro for Education add-on. The school test is a borderline file with familiar-looking contents. Give a reviewer a synthetic timetable, then the same timetable with a column of invented student support notes. If the reviewer accepts the suggested label on both because the banner looks authoritative, a faster click has sped up the wrong part of the workflow. Measure whether staff can recognise when to edit before celebrating how often they accept.

  • Google Vids can now make a longer synthetic shot inside the editor. In its 5 November Workspace notice, Google says Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash offers generated clips from three to 15 seconds, better consistency across reference images and more realistic voices. Its managed education availability list includes Education Plus and the Teaching and Learning and Google AI Pro for Education add-ons. Google lists no admin control specific to this feature. A media assignment can use the extra seconds to make a process visible, but the smoothness of the shot creates its own assessment problem. A generated clip of an experiment can show a plausible apparatus and an entirely invented result in one convincing take. In a student research course, a pupil who uses a generated shot to illustrate a proposed setup could put the source of the design in the caption, mark the scene as synthetic and show any real observation separately. Those three details tell a viewer more than a general AI disclosure at the end.

  • Microsoft put a date on where Claude in Copilot will process data. In its 5 November announcement, Microsoft says eligible organisations in the EU and European Free Trade Association will get processing within its EU Data Boundary for Claude models across Copilot experiences, including Copilot Cowork, by the end of 2026. Existing users of the integration will move automatically once it is available. The option is off by default in the EU and EFTA, and administrators can enable it by user or group. Microsoft already says customer data in the integration is excluded from foundation-model training. The new promise concerns where processing occurs. For schools comparing assistants, ask suppliers to fill in the same small table for each model inside the product. Record where data is stored, where inference happens and whether a different organisation receives or retains any of it. The school signs a product agreement, but staff make decisions using the menu they actually see.

On the radar

  • Fable 5.2's data terms deserve as much attention as its release. Anthropic announced Claude Fable 5.2 on 9 November and added it to its Covered Models list. The default policy keeps prompts and completions for at least 30 days. Safety investigations and legal obligations can delay deletion. Anthropic describes a temporary zero-retention option for eligible internal applications and a private beta that can keep monitoring data in customer-controlled infrastructure. Watch which of those arrangements reaches specific plans and channels. Approving a school account and clearing a particular model in it for sensitive work are separate decisions a tool register needs to capture.

  • Science education researchers have put judgment on the agenda. The NARST programme lists a 12 November AI4STEM Education Center panel with Kent Crippen, Xiaoming Zhai and Okhee Lee. It covers inquiry, science literacy and assessment alongside fairness, privacy and scientific integrity. The part worth following is whether any example specifies the evidence a pupil must handle themselves. A student can ask a model for possible explanations of an anomalous result and still have to identify which observation supports each explanation and what would rule it out. A worked classroom example showing that division of labour would travel further than another list of approved tools.

Sceptic's corner

If the model agrees with the expert, can it coach the teacher? The EdSurge account of the planned study points to a sensible first test. Give expert raters and the model the same written responses, then see where their scores line up. Faster feedback could be valuable if a teacher gets to use it while there is still time to change the next lesson. A model could also pick up recurring omissions across more practice examples than a coach can read.

There is a second question hiding in the score. Suppose a pupil writes that two fractions with different denominators cannot be equal. One teacher describes the misconception neatly. Another gives a messier account but proposes a number-line task that would distinguish confusion about the notation from confusion about the quantity. The two teachers may earn similar marks overall while needing very different feedback. A fluent summary can make either score sound convincing. The useful test is whether the feedback points back to a specific piece of evidence and gives the teacher a better question to ask.

That is a fair standard for a coaching trial. A personnel decision would require evidence about how consistently the system handles different writing styles, subjects and classroom contexts, and a way for a teacher to contest a reading. A rubric can bring rigour to a practice conversation. Putting its output into a staff file raises the evidentiary bar considerably.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 8 November 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 8 November 2026 · 1,434 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

The week belonged to Google, and to the question of who decides. On Tuesday it announced that student Gemini and Search would be off by default, said schools rejecting its AI would lose Search, then reversed within the day; a spokesman confirmed they are "separate controls" (GovTech, 4 November). Search still arrives with its AI features (Bloomberg via Claims Journal, 5 November), which Google says will offer step-by-step guided learning (CNBC, 3 November). On Wednesday Google set out how teachers can attach Guided Learning to Classroom assignments and read each student's chat, rolling out since Tuesday (Google, 4 November).

Since Friday's brief: a quiet weekend. Nothing material surfaced on Saturday or Sunday. The biggest story the dailies missed broke on Thursday, US time; it leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

Two of the week's stories sit on opposite sides of one screen. Guided Learning now gives teachers "individual student chat transcripts" (Google, 4 November). A lawsuit alleges that a 13-year-old discussed suicidal thoughts with Google's AI on a school Chromebook for months before her school alerted her parents, and that Google does not provide chat data on student accounts (Bloomberg via Claims Journal, 5 November). So the Sunday question: should a school read its students' AI conversations, and if it does, what is it reading?

Start with what a transcript is evidence of. Nicholas Soderstrom and Robert Bjork's review of the motor- and verbal-learning research separates performance, what a learner can do during practice, from learning, the change that lasts. The first is often an unreliable index of the second, and learners often mistake one for the other (Soderstrom and Bjork, 2015).

AI supplies a vivid demonstration. In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 Turkish high-school maths students, those given an unguarded GPT-4 tutor did 48% better than the control group in practice, then 17% worse on the exam once it was taken away. A hint-giving version lifted practice by 127% and left exam scores about level (Wharton, August 2024; since published in PNAS, 2025). A smooth transcript records performance with help on hand. It shows where a student got stuck. It cannot show what they will know next month.

Then consider what being read does. Help-seeking is social, and part of AI's appeal is that no one is watching. When researchers surveyed 6,960 students at four Australian universities in 2024, 49% were using AI for feedback, and one wrote that "[AI] allows me to ask stupid questions that I'm too ashamed to ask my teacher" (The Conversation, 27 August 2025). Put the teacher inside the chat and some of that safety leaves.

School monitoring shows where reading drifts. In the Center for Democracy and Technology's research, approximately half of students said they were not comfortable expressing their true thoughts and feelings online when they knew they were monitored. Among teachers at schools using monitoring software, 78% reported students flagged for disciplinary action, against 54% reporting referrals to a counsellor or other adult (CDT, August 2022). Software bought for care slides towards discipline.

The lawsuit shows the opposite failure: a private channel with no adult anywhere near it. So the answer is not to look away. It is to look narrowly, and to say so out loud.

For a school, that means three rules before any course switches on Guided Learning insights. Students hear, at the start of the task and in plain words, who can read the chat. Transcripts are read to find misconceptions, never marked or used for discipline. Safeguarding runs through the trained route the school already has, not a class teacher's dashboard. An AI use scale tells students what they may use. It does not yet tell them who is reading.

Behind You! Behind You!

A family is suing Google over a school Chromebook. Parents of a 13-year-old North Carolina seventh-grader have sued in California state court, alleging she spent months discussing suicidal thoughts with Gemini and Search's AI Mode, and that the warning came from her school, not Google (Bloomberg via Claims Journal, 5 November). Google told the New York Times it is reviewing the claims (SheKnows via Yahoo, 6 November). These are untested allegations. For any Google school, the question is which of its own alerts would fire on a chat.

Deepfakes are outrunning the rulebook. One in three of 852 US educators surveyed by the EdWeek Research Center this summer don't know whether their school has a policy on AI-generated fake images and videos; nine in 10 haven't been trained to detect or respond to deepfakes in the past two years (EdWeek via GovTech, 5 November). The same week Google paused a Vids feature that built an avatar from an uploaded image, a day after announcing it. It had no admin control, listed Education Plus, and said nothing about consent from the person pictured (Google, 2 November, updated 3 November).

Gemini now reads Forms responses. Gemini in Google Forms will analyse responses, find patterns across questions and suggest next steps, writing and running Python code so the counts come from the actual data (Google, 5 November). It comes with Education Plus and the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, reaching Scheduled Release domains from 19 November. Course evaluations and wellbeing check-ins are now one click from a model; the setting the post names is smart features and personalisation.

  • Midterms, read against the backlash: Democrats won in Texas, Georgia, Iowa and Ohio (Chalkbeat, 4 November). Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa campaigned against AI use by elementary students (Chalkbeat, 26 October). On preliminary results, a majority of Chicago's first fully elected school board signed a pledge to ban AI-based tools for students and staff (Chalkbeat, 5 November). The backlash has now won seats.
  • Overdue: Vault API support for Gemini exports, due within 14 days of the 22 October interface rollout; no update note by Sunday (Google, 22 October).
  • 17 November: Gems move to Settings in the Gemini app (Google).
  • Early 2027: Google's K-12 defaults take effect (SheKnows via Yahoo, 6 November), with specifics promised "in the coming months" (News & Observer via GovTech, 6 November).
  • Slow burn: No update surfaced this week on New York City's 38 products (Chalkbeat, 23 October). Search, which schools must take with its AI, is now the bigger test of feature-level control.

Sceptic's Corner

Google launched Guided Learning in Classroom alongside a stack of evidence: a blog promising "1.5x higher learning gains in just a single study session" (Google, 3 November), and a teacher guide calling the tool "proven to improve learning outcomes" (Google, Classroom guide). Follow the numbers home.

The 1.5x compares gains of 8.1 and 5.3 percentage points after half a day of studying statistics at the University of Tokyo. Google's November AI in Education report gives the same trial as a +0.14 standard deviation improvement (Google, November 2026). One framing sounds like half as much again; the other is a modest nudge from one sitting.

"Proven" rests on a pre-registered trial with 1,763 students in 12 schools in Port Loko, Sierra Leone, which DeepMind put at "roughly 1.2 to 1.7 years of typical learning progress" (Google DeepMind, 9 June). The November report rounds that to between one and two years; the guide quotes 1.7, a conversion calibrated to progress in low- and middle-income countries. What was tested was a package of teacher training, paired shared devices, structured lesson plans and technical support, in a design not built to separate the tool from the rest (EdTech Innovation Hub, June 2026). Students who started stronger gained most.

The same week, the Hechinger Report described an October study by Maryland's Jing Liu, who randomly assigned an always-available AI helper to 15 of 30 professors' courses. Students in those courses earned grades 0.11 points lower on a four-point scale, and only 15% used it (Hechinger Report, 2 November). Google's own report concedes that optional "tutor-on-the-side" AI in its ASU Study Hall and Coursera pilots failed to yield meaningful gains, largely through low adoption (Google, November 2026).

The honest reading: teacher-directed AI inside a designed lesson can help. A tab students may open is a different product, and the brochure sells the second on the strength of the first.

The line for the staffroom: "proven" means it worked somewhere, for someone, as part of something. Ask which part you are buying.
