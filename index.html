Monday, 23 November 2026

Top story

South Korea's education minister resigned over the rollout of anti-cheating rules.

Choi Kyo-jin stepped down on 22 November after President Lee Jae Myung formally approved his resignation. Choi took responsibility for changes made just before the 19 November College Scholastic Ability Test, known as the Suneung. In a statement issued by the education ministry, he acknowledged the disruption to candidates and the criticism that his ministry should have prepared earlier. The president directed Prime Minister Han Seong-sook to oversee the follow-up. Yonhap, 22 November.

The ministry's 20 November update counted 111 electronic-device-related cases through the final session, updating its provisional count through the first four sessions. Education ministry tally, 20 November. The next set of exams was already bearing down. On 20 November the education ministry and the Korean Council for University Education reviewed device-cheating precautions with 46 universities that run their own admissions assessments. They set up an emergency group to share prevention and detection information until that admissions cycle's university-run exams end. On Sunday Lee said concerns had spread to university essay tests and internal school exams. He called for a cross-government review of how exams are run, including national qualifications, with changes to the law where needed. Those are directions for follow-up, with implementation still to come. Education ministry, 20 November · South Korean presidency, 22 November.

The immediate lesson for international schools is how a rule gets from a briefing to the exam room. An instruction about what candidates may carry is easy to circulate. Its consequences get more complicated when a student has an approved device accommodation, a substitute invigilator is on duty, or a family has read yesterday's instructions. A workable policy needs a single dated version, a route for accommodation decisions and a way for a proctor to get an answer in time to use it. Sunday's resignation is a reminder that the quality of those arrangements can become part of the fairness of the assessment itself.

Other stories

  • Students revised with three AI tools and scored lower on a new unaided essay. Azzeddine Boudouaia, Yan Li and Jie Gao followed 72 Zhejiang University postgraduates in an eight-week English-writing course. Students drafted three assignments independently, then revised them using ChatGPT, Pigai and Bearly, consulting their teacher when the tools' advice conflicted. Many revisions were coded as successful. On a fresh essay without AI or word-processing tools, the group's mean score for sentence-level and other rule-based features fell from 4.46 to 4.03 out of five (paired Cohen's d = 0.54). The study had one group, and the two essay prompts were not equated for rhetorical demand. Differences between the prompts or other features of the course could explain the change, and the study did not score qualities such as argument and organisation on those tests. The design does give a language department two separate things to look at. Learners may be good at applying advice to one paragraph and still need practice recognising the same problem in their next piece. Both deserve space in a writing unit. Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 21 November.

  • Gemini can now work inside Mac apps on eligible personal accounts. Google announced a rollout in English in the United States for adults with personal Google AI Plus, Pro or Ultra accounts and Apple silicon running macOS 15 or later. Gemini can click, type and navigate menus, with a live preview and a stop control. People can give one-off or standing permission to an app, and Gemini asks for confirmation on sensitive tasks. Its documentation also says that access to an app can expose the accounts already signed in there. This is a consumer rollout. The school question arises where a staff member uses both personal and work services on the same device. A person may open a personal AI account and still be signed into staff mail in a native app. Treating permission as a question about the app's name alone leaves out what is open in it and what it can do. An international school managing Apple devices has a good reason to check how staff and personal sessions are separated before treating these tools as ordinary writing assistants. Google, 19 November · Gemini Apps Help.

  • The University of California widened AI access and drew a faculty governance challenge. On 19 November UC announced a systemwide expansion of its OpenAI deal, with ChatGPT and Codex available to more students, faculty and staff at no charge to individuals. The president cited access, cost and stronger protections than people get through personal accounts. His response says campuses manage deployment locally and faculty retain authority over use in courses. The next day the Academic Council said it had asked for more consultation before the purchase was finalised and learned of the final decision about an hour before the public announcement. It called for consultation before campus deployments. The council letter and the president's reply are published together, which lets readers see when each side thought consultation could still change something. For Ed Tech Coaches, that is worth asking before a platform announcement goes out. Teachers can retain discretion about individual assignments and still have questions about the shared defaults, which student groups get access and how the school describes the service to families. UC Office of the President, 19 November · UC Academic Council, 20 November.

  • A Thai virtual-reality pilot ended at a real computer bench. Puttida Sakulviriyakitkul developed a hand-tracked Meta Quest 3 simulation for trainee computer teachers in Bangkok who had limited access to current hardware. The sequence included website lessons, virtual practice, a session on physical computers and a debrief. The 35 trainees with matched knowledge tests went from 8.20 to 9.09 out of 10 on average. Thirty-six trainees' end-of-sequence hardware performance averaged 4.85 out of five in the instructor's ratings. The tests had a ceiling effect, the instructor who designed the simulation was the sole practical assessor, and the single-group design leaves the effect of the headset unresolved. Generative AI helped the author produce media for the simulation. The useful choice for a school maker programme is that learners ultimately had to perform on the equipment they were training to use. For a workshop short of kits, that suggests a practical split. Rehearsal can take place away from the bench, and bench time can be spent assembling, testing and explaining an actual fault. Journal of Education and Training Studies, 19 November.

On the radar

  • South Korea is running three vendor-led teacher workshops with follow-up. The education ministry announced on 20 November that it and the Korea Foundation for the Advancement of Science and Creativity would offer practical training for about 240 participants, including teachers and education officials. The programme scheduled a Microsoft session for 21 November and puts Google on 28 November and AWS on 5 December. The draft activities range from building an educational web app to creating teaching materials. An 11 December showcase, follow-up training and mentoring offer a chance to see which teacher-made examples are usable in an ordinary classroom after the workshops. Education ministry, 20 November.

  • UNU Macau has named the teams heading to its youth AI final. On 20 November the United Nations University institute announced 15 finalist teams, including 12 selected internationally from 314 applications. Six of the international finalists are in the education track. Teams are due to give a ten-minute presentation and answer judges' questions for five minutes at the 26 November final. The format makes the questions worth watching. Student teams can arrive with a convincing prototype, and a short oral defence gives judges room to ask whose problem it solves, what they tested and where their evidence runs out. UNU Macau, 20 November · Conference programme.

Sceptic's corner

An institution can make a defensible case for providing managed AI access even when teachers disagree on classroom use. The UC president's case is concrete. Students and staff were already using personal accounts outside UC's negotiated terms, and wider managed access could improve privacy and affordability. There is a cost to waiting if the existing pattern persists. There is also a cost to treating the licence as the moment when the consequential choices have already been made. The UC Academic Council's request for consultation before local deployment puts that second point on paper. UC correspondence, 19 and 20 November.

Consider an English learner using a school-provided tool to rehearse an answer. The teacher might welcome vocabulary suggestions during practice and still require a fresh unaided response at the end of a unit. Both parts are coherent if students get the distinction before the task, understand which feedback they are expected to act on and see the same guidance in the assignment and on the platform. The arrangement could leave one student applying automated rewrites to every piece of work just because the tool stays visible. Course-level discretion is easiest to protect when staff can influence how the tools first appear to students. A licence can settle the account question on a particular date. Teachers still have to settle what an accessible tool is for, one task at a time.

The Week That Was

A week of small, conditional evidence. Wharton's Primer team watched simulated characters help students too early (arXiv, 12 November). Harvard's study of about 3.3 million students found that lockable phone pouches bought a small learning gain (Harvard CEPR, 17 November). Carnegie Mellon's tutor helped adults fix their own errors, with nearly twice the practice time bundled in (arXiv, 15 November). OpenAI's new models, released on 17 November, came with the admission that they were more likely to leave out obstacles that blocked their work (OpenAI). On Thursday the EU Council put curriculum and assessment on the same AI agenda (Council of the EU, 19 November).

The same Thursday, unreported in the weekday briefs, South Korea sat its university entrance exam under its tightest AI controls yet; it frames the centrepiece. Since Friday's brief: on Saturday 21 November, Fortune reported that three of America's four largest districts are pushing back on Google's Search terms. It leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

On Thursday South Korea ran what may be the most closely guarded exam in the world. For the College Scholastic Ability Test, 497,526 candidates sat at 1,312 sites with about 83,400 invigilators. Glasses were inspected, Wi-Fi within 100 metres was switched off, and the government sent three national emergency alerts during the day (Kyunghyang Shinmun, 19 November). The preliminary count of 98 covered the first four sessions. The ministry’s updated count after the fifth session was 111 electronic-device cases: 54 detections linked to the alerts, 52 other detections and five cases of device use (Ministry of Education, 20 November).

The week's most notorious case was not caught in an exam room at all. On 15 November, the top-ranked candidate in this year's tax accountant exam admitted to cheating with electronic devices and generative AI (News1 via Financial News, 17 November). Speculation about his near-perfect results came first; the confession followed (Al Jazeera, 17 November).

So the Sunday question: if guarding the room has limits, what should a school change instead?

Read the Korean numbers coldly. Most of what was caught was a device that went off or turned up. Detection counts the careless; whatever the careful managed is, by definition, missing from the count. England's chief regulator has reached the same place. In an 18 November letter, Sir Ian Bauckham said Ofqual would soon propose changes to coursework in A level history and the three English A levels, and told exam boards he expects exam-centre staff, invigilators included, to be trained to spot wearable misuse in time for the summer 2027 series. "But we cannot rely on invigilators alone," he wrote (Ofqual, 18 November).

Research has words for this. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu separate "discursive" changes, which tell students what is allowed, from "structural" ones, which change the task itself so it works whether or not students follow the instructions (Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 2025). Traffic-light rules for AI use are their example of the first kind. They borrow the look of a system with real enforcement and leave the enforcement behind. Korea chose the opposite extreme: maximum structure, applied to a single day.

One AP course already shows a middle path. In AP Computer Science Principles, students build a program of their own, and the supervised May exam asks written questions about that code (College Board). The project can be made anywhere; the explanation happens where nobody can prompt it.

Korea is edging the same way. Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin told the National Assembly the case was a chance to rethink the purpose and format of exams, and essay-type questions are under discussion, undecided (News1 via Financial News, 17 November).

For an international school, an AI-use scale is a discursive instrument, and a useful one: it tells students what each task allows. It cannot make that true. Its structural partner can be cheap. For any task set at the low-AI end of the scale, name the moment, in class and in front of a teacher, where the student explains, defends or extends what they handed in. The College Board does it once a year. A department can do it every unit.

Behind You! Behind You!

Three of America's four largest districts are pushing back on Google. New York City has switched off Gemini and AI Mode on student devices and is talking to Google; Los Angeles has asked for a way to turn the AI summaries off; Chicago says it will look at other search tools if it cannot suppress them (Fortune, 21 November). New Jersey's governor's office called the policy unfair to schools and students (NJ.com via Yahoo, 18 November). Miami-Dade, which has offered Gemini to about 100,000 high schoolers, did not answer questions on how it will adapt. Pushback so far, not departure.

Gemini skills can now be shared, and sharing starts switched on. In a gradual rollout from 12 November, on the web app only for now, Gemini users can share skills with View or Edit access and attach files from Drive, Gemini Notebook and Google Photos (Google, 16 November). Sharing is on by default and admins can turn it off. Skills follow the domain's Drive sharing settings, so external sharing applies to them too, and there is no admin control over attaching files. A well-built rubric skill is worth passing round; check the external setting first.

Gemini can now label sensitive Drive files, for schools on the right licence. On 17 November Google made Gemini-powered classification in Drive generally available. Admins describe a label in plain language, test it, then let Gemini apply it across Drive so labels can drive data-loss and retention rules; it will not overwrite labels people have set (Google, 17 November). In education it needs Google AI Pro for Education. For a school that must show where student records sit, losing the manual training step matters. A rule written in a sentence still needs testing on files whose contents you already know.

Sceptic's Corner

The centrepiece's remedy, change the task rather than guard the room, has a strong case against it. Korea is making it in public.

Redesign does not remove advantage; it moves it. When the National Education Commission's essay-type plans surfaced in August, one parent told Yonhap the family would simply add another academy, and a cram-school official expected demand for test technique to grow in the early years (Yonhap via Daum, 16 August). Grading fairness was the largest single concern in the commission's public comments: 754 of 3,246 (News1 via Financial News, 17 November). The ministry's plan for marking essays at scale is an AI that provisionally scores answers, trained on 1.1 million of them, in every regional education office by 2029 (Money Today, 5 August). The machine leaves the exam hall and returns as the marker.

Conversation-based checks have costs of their own. They reward the fluent and the confident, and a timed in-class write is harder on slow writers and anxious students.

Nor has the motive changed much. Stanford's Challenge Success surveys put the share of high schoolers reporting at least one cheating behaviour in the previous month at 60–70% for years, and the 2023 surveys, which added questions on tools like ChatGPT, found it about the same or slightly lower (Stanford GSE, 31 October 2023). The tool changes; the stakes drive it.

The line for the staffroom: redesigning the task is right, but it is neither free nor neutral. Before calling the new version fairer, check whom it favours.
