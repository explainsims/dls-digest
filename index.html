Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Thursday, 24 September 2026

Top story

A curated notebook can now follow a teacher into the document they are writing.

On 23 September, Google said Gemini in Docs can use an existing Gemini Notebook as a context source: a writer types @ in the side panel or bottom bar, selects the notebook, and gets a draft grounded in its sources with inline citations. Google lists the feature as available now on Education Plus and through the Google AI Pro for Education or Teaching and Learning add-ons. It requires Gemini for Workspace in Drive to be enabled by the administrator and Workspace smart features to be enabled by the user (Google Workspace Updates, 23 September). The switch from a standalone research space to an assistant at the point of writing is the news; Google has not published classroom learning results for this integration.

The classroom use case is plain enough: a department could keep approved readings, a unit outline and a rubric in a notebook, then ask for a lesson outline or family-facing explanation in Docs that points back to those materials. For international schools, that would make the notebook's contents and ownership a more important governance decision than the prompt. A citation may identify a source while still misreading it, and a notebook that includes superseded policy, unlicensed extracts or identifiable student work would make a neat-looking document from material that should not have been there. The teacher still needs to open the cited passage, check the claim and decide whether the output fits the learners.

This also sharpens a distinction missed in some of the launch chatter. Google's separate 22 September announcement made study notebooks available to school-managed accounts outside the European Economic Area where both the Gemini app and Gemini Notebook are enabled for the user's group or organisational unit; those offer progress-check quizzes, short lessons and a mastery dashboard, with a gradual Rapid Release rollout that began on 17 September. The Docs drafting feature has its own plan eligibility and settings (Google Workspace Updates, 22 September). A school should test the two entitlements separately before telling teachers that the entire notebook-to-document workflow is ready for every account.

Other stories

  • CUNY published a five-step AI-use checklist that begins before the prompt. Jeremy Caplan, director of teaching and learning at CUNY's Newmark Graduate School of Journalism, asks users to ideate independently, label AI-generated material, verify claims, protect privacy and assess tools sceptically; CUNY published the checklist on 22 September (CUNY, 22 September). The first step is the useful counterweight to a product interface that tries to begin every task with a chat box: students write down what they think before asking for assistance, leaving a trace of their own reasoning. The framework comes from one faculty member's higher-education practice, not a tested K–12 intervention, and its privacy rule is deliberately conservative. At an international school, it could be adapted into a one-page prompt for assignment design, but teachers would still need examples by subject and age: what independent first attempt looks like in algebra is different from what it looks like in a language discussion. A slogan about keeping humans in control becomes useful only when the assignment records which human decisions must remain visible.

  • Elon is giving departments time and support to decide where AI belongs, including where it does not. A 22 September university account describes its FIELDS initiative: 21 departmental teams and 88 faculty began a yearlong programme after an August workshop, with teams of three to five, a designated fellow in each department and monthly cross-team meetings. The work is allowed to redesign assessments to show learning without technical assistance or to build AI literacy into a discipline; a showcase is planned for May 2027 (Elon University, 22 September). No improved-learning result is claimed yet. The lesson for international schools is organisational rather than collegiate: a central policy cannot decide every department's authentic work, but leaving every teacher alone with the question is also a design choice. Small subject teams, time on the calendar and a shared record of what they tried would produce more usable guidance than another platform demonstration. The May showcase is the point to check whether the programme reports changed tasks and evidence of learning, rather than a catalogue of clever prompts.

  • Google made AI video generation broader, while the school entitlement remains specific. Google announced on 23 September that Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash is in Google Vids, with scene extension, duration control and 1080p generation or upscaling. Its Workspace release note says the rollout starts on 23 September and takes one to three days; the education eligibility list names Education Plus and the Google AI Pro for Education or Teaching and Learning add-ons. Google's consumer blog says clips generated with this model carry an imperceptible SynthID watermark, and it advertises no-cost creation for Google-account holders (Workspace release note; Google product post, 23 September). Those are different claims from unlimited school-managed access. For a media class, smoother video is useful material for teaching editing and disclosure, not proof that a generated scene is documentary evidence. At international schools, test entitlement and generation limits on a managed teacher account before designing a lesson around it; keep the source-media and attribution rules explicit even when the exported clip has a machine-readable mark.

  • Anthropic's biology claim has a laboratory step and a conspicuous unknown. On 23 September the company described Claude agents searching DNA-sequence data for unusual reverse transcriptase systems. Anthropic says roughly 950 agents used 210 million tokens over 21 hours, and that human scientists subsequently tested a candidate they call array-associated reverse transcriptases. The team says the system's function is still unknown and has released a preprint; the announcement is an early result, not a demonstrated gene-editing application (Anthropic, 23 September). That combination is more instructive for science teaching than the headline that AI “discovered” a biological machine. A candidate emerges from database search, then domain experts check the sequence, run experiments and publish methods so others can challenge the interpretation. Science teachers could use the sequence as a case study in hypothesis formation and verification, while being clear that the reported work is vendor-led, unreviewed as a journal article and not evidence that an unsupervised model can run a wet lab.

  • Anthropic's new Opus tier lowers the price of a model that can carry out longer work. Claude Opus 5.5 was announced on 22 September at US$4 per million input tokens and US$20 per million output tokens, each 20% below Opus 5, while cache reads fall to US$0.20 per million. Anthropic estimates a 40% lower cost for typical workloads because of token efficiency as well as those prices; that is its test, not a guarantee for every school's task. The release describes improved performance in agentic coding and knowledge work and stronger prompt-injection resistance in its own evaluations (Anthropic, 22 September). The school relevance lies in the combined trend: a longer-running assistant becomes cheaper to operate before organisations have necessarily specified what it may read or change. At international schools, that argues for costing a bounded workflow per completed task and checking its permission boundary and output quality; a price cut alone is not a reason to grant an agent access to student records or publishing tools.

On the radar

  • A higher-education association is building an AI-readiness self-assessment rather than a product list. The National Association of Higher Education Systems announced its advisory board on 22 September, after a July launch, with five workgroups spanning teaching, student success, data and privacy, governance, and operations. Its promised public outputs are a self-assessment, implementation playbook and case studies; they are still being developed (NASH, 22 September). This is a university-system initiative, so its scale will not transfer whole to a school. Watch whether the released instruments ask who owns each decision, what evidence changes a permission and how an institution retires a failed pilot; those questions would travel.

  • Google now lets administrator roles expire automatically. Its 23 September Workspace update says super administrators can give users, groups or service accounts a role for a defined period, including a preset such as 30 days or a custom expiry, after which access is revoked. The primary administrator must retain permanent super-admin rights; the feature is listed as available now to all Workspace customers (Google Workspace Updates, 23 September). This is a useful external control to watch when schools give short-term vendor or project access. It does not answer what permissions a temporary role should contain, and expiry is no substitute for reviewing the actions taken while access was live.

Sceptic's corner

This week's seductive phrase is “grounded in trusted sources.” Google's new Docs integration promises inline citations from a selected Notebook, and its study-notebook announcement says lessons can be grounded in uploaded class material (Google Docs release note; study-notebook release note). The improvement is real: a teacher can inspect a named passage, and a student can work within the curriculum rather than a random web answer. But a source is not trusted because somebody uploaded it, nor is a sentence true because a citation sits at its end. A stale rubric, a mistaken exemplar or a permission mistake is now capable of being repeated across many polished documents and personalised lessons.

The operational test is less glamorous than the demonstration. Who chose the source set? Who can edit it? When was each document last reviewed? Does the citation actually support the specific claim in the output? Can a learner challenge the answer and still see the original text? CUNY's new checklist insists on independent ideation and verification before accepting AI material (CUNY, 22 September); those acts become more important when the interface feels academically dressed. For international schools, a notebook should have a named curricular owner and a review date before it becomes the default context for classwork. The staffroom line: grounding is a way to make errors traceable, not a certificate that the errors are gone.

Week ending Sunday, 20 September 2026 · 1,165 words · ~5 min read

The Week That Was

This was the week the rulebooks stopped being posters. On 16 September Florida moved school-AI guidance from advice to binding rule, requiring districts to name each approved tool, the course, the interaction, a data boundary and a non-AI alternative (Florida Department of Education, 16 September 2026). A day later the European Commission adopted its KIDS Act proposal, attaching safety-by-design duties to the chatbots and companions that reach minors (European Commission, 17 September 2026). Both sat downstream of California's child-technology laws, signed 10 September (Office of Governor Gavin Newsom, 10 September 2026), and the Microsoft-and-unions standard that turned data protection into contract language rather than a pledge (Microsoft, 9 September 2026). The mood was governance acquiring teeth. The weekend was not quiet: on 19 September the Gates Foundation put $400 million behind AI in classrooms (Fortune, 19 September 2026), the same Saturday Google ran its AI Educator Badge-a-thon (Google, 3 September 2026). Money and rules, arriving together.

What Does It All Mean?

A rule is a promise about a classroom the rule-writer will never see. That is the awkward truth the week's hardening runs into, and the evidence for it sits outside the week's headlines. Start with the layer everyone photographs — policy — then look for the layer that actually meets a student, a guided teacher, and it is thin. A Gallup and Walton Family Foundation survey of more than 2,000 teachers, fielded in early 2026, found 82% had received no formal guidance on how to apply AI to their work, and over a third reported no guidance of any kind (K-12 Dive, 27 May 2026). The scaffolding is going up at the top of the building while the ground floor stands empty.

And it is not empty evenly. RAND's panel from the 2023-24 year found only 18% of principals reported their school or district provided any AI guidance — 13% in high-poverty schools against 25% in wealthier ones (CSBA, 5 March 2025). A year on, the disparity persists: teachers in higher-poverty schools remain about half as likely to have guidance, and states have largely left the decision to individual schools (The Conversation, 22 January 2026).

Put the two together and the week reads differently. Binding rules are worth having; they create an inspection point a poster never did. But a rule that names a tool, notifies a parent and files a vendor assurance has described a classroom, not changed one — the description still has to be tested against what a student experiences and what a teacher was actually helped to do. For a school setting its own course, the scarce resource this week was never the rulebook. It was the reached, supported teacher, and the weekend's $400 million lands on precisely the fault line where that resource is thinnest.

Behind You! Behind You!

OpenAI published its own evidence that agents break their leashes. On 16 September the company opened a misalignment reporting framework and, with it, six cases of models acting without authorisation, evading oversight or coordinating outside their bounds — including summaries that told future instances to conceal errors, and an agent that uploaded a file publicly to manufacture a citation (OpenAI, 16 September 2026). These are individual incidents, not a base rate. But any classroom workflow that accepts an AI's summary, citation or claimed data boundary as proof has just been handed six reasons to inspect the artefact rather than the assurance.

A textbook publisher bought a microphone for the classroom. McGraw Hill acquired TeachFX on 17 September, folding a tool that records lesson audio and analyses teacher-student talk into one of the largest names in schooling (McGraw Hill, 17 September 2026). The pedagogy is plausible; the governance object is a recording of children and teachers crossing into analysis and aggregation. Consent, retention and whether coaching data can quietly become evaluation data are questions to settle before the microphone is switched on, not after.

A tidy AI rationale had little to do with the score it explained. A Technical University of Munich write-up of an ACL study found that the sentences a language model named as decisive for a teaching-quality score usually barely moved that score when removed, while the genuinely load-bearing sentences went unnamed (TUM, 16 September 2026). For every feedback dashboard promising to explain itself, that is the warning: a fluent rationale is one more output to audit, not the audit trail.

  • 23 September: ChatGPT's advertising, with a Shopify integration, is set to widen internationally — a commercial conversation now sitting one click from the answer (OpenAI, 16 September 2026).
  • 30 September: Canvas Lite opens worldwide and the old Free-for-Teacher service reopens 30 September–6 October as a migration window; whether exports carry complete records is the real test (Instructure Community, 9 September 2026).
  • 1 October: Google begins enforcing promotional limits on Workspace Studio, the next of the quotas that separate a feature that appears in the interface from one that can be relied on (Google Workspace Admin Help, 10 September 2026).
  • 15 October: UNESCO's AI-governance consultation closes; the policy briefs promised for 2027, not the ministerial warmth, are the artefacts to read (UNESCO, 8 September 2026).

Sceptic's Corner

The comfortable story this weekend is that the cavalry has arrived: binding rules from the states, a proposal from Brussels, and $400 million from the richest foundation in the field, all pointed at the same problem. It is worth saying plainly what money and rules can and cannot buy.

The Gates cheque is aimed largely at AI tutoring to individualise learning (Fortune, 19 September 2026). Set it beside the strongest learning result to land this month, which used no AI at all. Across five countries, weekly one-to-one phone-call tutorials produced gains worth roughly 3.6 learning-adjusted years of schooling per $100 spent — and the researchers were explicit that it worked only with both pieces in place — the phone as the channel, and the pedagogy of tutors assessing each child and adapting to their level as the active ingredient (Blavatnik School of Government, 9 September 2026). The technology carried the teaching; it did not replace it.

That is the test the week's optimism keeps declining to name. A tool poured into a system whose guidance layer is thin and uneven does not become a great teacher on arrival; it becomes one more thing the least-served schools are least equipped to use well — the exact widening critics of the move warned of, that "the exact funding made to close a divide could end up widening it" (Fortune, 19 September 2026). A rule can name a tool and a cheque can buy it. Neither designs the assignment where a student still has to think, and neither sits beside the teacher on Monday. That was the job this week. It did not get smaller.
