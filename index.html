Thursday, 26 November 2026

Top story

Malaysia launched a homework AI that puts parents in charge of the chat.

Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim launched AI Temu AI in Putrajaya on 25 November. The package brings together DELIMa 3.0, the latest version of the national digital learning platform, the education ministry's AI literacy guide, and Cikgu ILMU, a tool from YTL AI Labs. Anwar also listed offline AI learning resources. There's a difference between announcing a chatbot for schools and launching it alongside guidance on what children and adults are meant to do with it. Malaysia is trying the second approach. Launch speech, 25 November.

YTL says Cikgu ILMU will be free for Malaysian parents, with material aligned to the national curriculum from Year 1 through Form 5 in Bahasa Melayu, English, Chinese and Tamil. Parents can photograph a homework question for a worked explanation or generate a quiz that a child completes in a separate interface. The child can do the quiz without a chat window, while the system still marks the answers and reports suggested areas for practice to the adult. That division matters because it gives the adult a chance to review the material and leaves the child with something to solve. The company describes how the product works. How well the explanations, questions and marking hold up across four languages is a separate question for an evaluation. YTL AI Labs, 25 November.

For international schools, the design is worth studying even where the local curriculum differs. Take a familiar homework problem and sketch who sees each step. The adult can inspect the explanation and decide whether to show a hint. The student can explain a first attempt. The teacher can ask whether the method matches what was taught. Each step is a chance to spot a plausible error before it becomes someone's new misconception. A four-language launch widens the audience. The quality of those handoffs will decide how much help families actually get.

Other stories

  • A high-school class gave lower self-ratings after an AI ethics course. Yujing Chen and Jessica Shuk Ching Leung analysed results from 45 tenth-graders in one class over ten weeks. Students examined issues including the ethics of self-driving vehicles and the consequences of automation for platform workers, mapped stakeholders and prepared policy proposals for simulated community presentations. On the researchers' questionnaire, all four dimensions of students' self-reported views fell, including how they understood the role of evidence and their sense of responsibility. Interviews with six students suggested that some had begun to give more conditional answers and consider competing interests. The authors interpret that as possible intellectual humility. One teacher, one class and no comparison group leave the cause open, and the evidence-related scale was less internally consistent than the other three. It's a reason to read what a student writes alongside the confidence rating. Ask them to show which assumption changed, then give them a different case and see whether they can recognise the same trade-off. A lower score on a survey can start a useful conversation in that setting. Chen and Leung, 24 November.

  • US teachers' reported classroom AI use barely moved in the latest EdWeek survey. The EdWeek Research Center surveyed 590 teachers from August to October and reports that 62% used AI in their classrooms at least a little this school year, from 61% in 2025 and 34% in 2023. It also reports that 64% had had at least one session of AI professional development by October. There's room for quite a lot of change inside those numbers. A teacher who tried a single demonstration and a teacher who regularly creates practice tasks both clear the threshold for some classroom use. The PD figure doesn't reveal whether teachers got time to trial a task, inspect the results or ask what happens if a student submits personal information. A school tracking its own progress can get more out of asking for one example from this term and a short account of what the teacher changed after using it. That gives a department something it can show a colleague who is still deciding when the tool would earn its place. Education Week Research Center reporting, 25 November.

  • A Wisconsin pilot will let teachers follow small-group maths discussions across languages. In a 23 November Wisconsin Public Radio interview, lead researcher Shamya Karumbaiah said her team plans to try its tool in six classrooms next year, with a focus on Grades 4–8. It records small-group discussion, transcribes speech and can translate it for the teacher, with models running on local servers. UW–Madison's 18 November grant announcement said the team received US$350,000 from Multilingual by Design and is extending earlier science-classroom work into maths, working with the Multilingual Learning Research Center and WIDA. The obvious use is making it easier for a teacher to hear a mathematical idea shared in a language they don't speak. The useful test is whether the tool preserves that idea when students switch languages mid-sentence or several classmates speak over one another. It matters that the teacher can go back to the group to check. Any school considering a similar recording tool would also need to know who can listen, how long the audio is kept and what the alternative looks like for a child who declines. Wisconsin Public Radio, 23 November. UW–Madison, 18 November.

  • A field study in northern England found that guidance was often scarcer than devices. SHINE published Ashiya Dewan's Learning in the Age of AI on 25 November. The report draws on summer fieldwork mainly in Middlesbrough, with additional perspectives from Sunderland and Rotherham, and includes staff interviews and pupil focus groups from Year 2 to Year 12. In participating schools, staff and pupils described widespread device access alongside uneven adult guidance. Pupils recognised that AI could be wrong and said they preferred help that showed how to approach a problem. These are accounts from a small, deliberately selected set of schools. They give a school a better question about homework than whether every student has a device. Can a student who has no one available at home see a worked example, ask for a clue and practise on similar material? For a class that relies on homework for its next lesson, the answer matters as much as the list of accounts the school pays for. SHINE, 25 November.

On the radar

  • Kawamata students are due to present proposals for their town in 2046. Kindai University said on 19 November that 24 first-year students at Kawamata High School in Fukushima and seven university students were expected at the 26 November presentations. The project supplied local statistics and a tool designed to ask about feasibility or suggest a different perspective. The school will have the proposals to inspect, alongside before-and-after questionnaires about attitudes towards AI. The thing I'd like to see is an annotated proposal that shows where a statistic or conversation made a group change a claim. That's a piece of work another school could learn from. Kindai University, 19 November.

  • Vietnam took its existing school AI framework to the regional table. On 25 November Deputy Minister Phạm Quang Hưng chaired the plenary of the Southeast Asian Ministers of Education Organization's high officials meeting in Kuala Lumpur. Vietnam presented its digital competency frameworks and school AI education framework and outlined preparations to host the organisation's council meeting in Hanoi in May 2027. The discussion also covered AI in university teaching and micro-credentials. For schools, the thing to look out for before May is whether the regional discussion produces teachable examples or shared ways to assess the skills countries are naming. Vietnam education ministry report, 25 November.

  • Google workers have added their names to the case for separate Search controls. Bloomberg reported on 19 November that more than 540 employees and contractors, according to the Alphabet Workers Union, backed a petition to let schools keep ordinary Google Search without its generative features. On 3 November, Google announced plans to switch off access to Search and several Gemini products by default for managed K–12 student accounts. In a 9 November letter, US Senator Ed Markey requested a written reply by 30 November on whether schools could keep Search and disable the generative features. The question to look for in any reply is whether there will be a separate setting inside Search. Bloomberg, 19 November. Google, 3 November. Markey letter, 9 November.

Sceptic's corner

Giving an adult the chat window ought to be safer than giving a child the same window. The adult can decide which help fits, notice a bad explanation and stop the conversation. Cikgu ILMU's design gives that adult a real opportunity to intervene. It also gives the machine a second job the moment a child takes a quiz. The score and advice to the parent still come from an automated judgement. The quiet failure is easy to imagine. A child uses a valid method that the system marks wrong, so the adult spends the next evening trying to fix a misconception that belongs to the software. YTL AI Labs, 25 November.

There's also a cost hidden inside the word parent. The north-of-England interviews suggest that adults' availability and confidence vary even where devices are common. Putting an adult into the design is a sensible safeguard if someone has time to do the job and the school provides a route for families who need help. A short worked example, a chance to practise at school, or a way to challenge a mark can matter more than adding another AI explanation. The test for this kind of homework tool is whether a child can show the same understanding the next day, with a new question and a human who can follow the reasoning. SHINE, 25 November.

The Week That Was

A week of small, conditional evidence. Wharton's Primer team watched simulated characters help students too early (arXiv, 12 November). Harvard's study of about 3.3 million students found that lockable phone pouches bought a small learning gain (Harvard CEPR, 17 November). Carnegie Mellon's tutor helped adults fix their own errors, with nearly twice the practice time bundled in (arXiv, 15 November). OpenAI's new models, released on 17 November, came with the admission that they were more likely to leave out obstacles that blocked their work (OpenAI). On Thursday the EU Council put curriculum and assessment on the same AI agenda (Council of the EU, 19 November).

The same Thursday, unreported in the weekday briefs, South Korea sat its university entrance exam under its tightest AI controls yet; it frames the centrepiece. Since Friday's brief: on Saturday 21 November, Fortune reported that three of America's four largest districts are pushing back on Google's Search terms. It leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

On Thursday South Korea ran what may be the most closely guarded exam in the world. For the College Scholastic Ability Test, 497,526 candidates sat at 1,312 sites with about 83,400 invigilators. Glasses were inspected, Wi-Fi within 100 metres was switched off, and the government sent three national emergency alerts during the day (Kyunghyang Shinmun, 19 November). The preliminary count of 98 covered the first four sessions. The ministry’s updated count after the fifth session was 111 electronic-device cases: 54 detections linked to the alerts, 52 other detections and five cases of device use (Ministry of Education, 20 November).

The week's most notorious case was not caught in an exam room at all. On 15 November, the top-ranked candidate in this year's tax accountant exam admitted to cheating with electronic devices and generative AI (News1 via Financial News, 17 November). Speculation about his near-perfect results came first; the confession followed (Al Jazeera, 17 November).

So the Sunday question: if guarding the room has limits, what should a school change instead?

Read the Korean numbers coldly. Most of what was caught was a device that went off or turned up. Detection counts the careless; whatever the careful managed is, by definition, missing from the count. England's chief regulator has reached the same place. In an 18 November letter, Sir Ian Bauckham said Ofqual would soon propose changes to coursework in A level history and the three English A levels, and told exam boards he expects exam-centre staff, invigilators included, to be trained to spot wearable misuse in time for the summer 2027 series. "But we cannot rely on invigilators alone," he wrote (Ofqual, 18 November).

Research has words for this. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu separate "discursive" changes, which tell students what is allowed, from "structural" ones, which change the task itself so it works whether or not students follow the instructions (Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 2025). Traffic-light rules for AI use are their example of the first kind. They borrow the look of a system with real enforcement and leave the enforcement behind. Korea chose the opposite extreme: maximum structure, applied to a single day.

One AP course already shows a middle path. In AP Computer Science Principles, students build a program of their own, and the supervised May exam asks written questions about that code (College Board). The project can be made anywhere; the explanation happens where nobody can prompt it.

Korea is edging the same way. Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin told the National Assembly the case was a chance to rethink the purpose and format of exams, and essay-type questions are under discussion, undecided (News1 via Financial News, 17 November).

For an international school, an AI-use scale is a discursive instrument, and a useful one: it tells students what each task allows. It cannot make that true. Its structural partner can be cheap. For any task set at the low-AI end of the scale, name the moment, in class and in front of a teacher, where the student explains, defends or extends what they handed in. The College Board does it once a year. A department can do it every unit.

Behind You! Behind You!

Three of America's four largest districts are pushing back on Google. New York City has switched off Gemini and AI Mode on student devices and is talking to Google; Los Angeles has asked for a way to turn the AI summaries off; Chicago says it will look at other search tools if it cannot suppress them (Fortune, 21 November). New Jersey's governor's office called the policy unfair to schools and students (NJ.com via Yahoo, 18 November). Miami-Dade, which has offered Gemini to about 100,000 high schoolers, did not answer questions on how it will adapt. Pushback so far, not departure.

Gemini skills can now be shared, and sharing starts switched on. In a gradual rollout from 12 November, on the web app only for now, Gemini users can share skills with View or Edit access and attach files from Drive, Gemini Notebook and Google Photos (Google, 16 November). Sharing is on by default and admins can turn it off. Skills follow the domain's Drive sharing settings, so external sharing applies to them too, and there is no admin control over attaching files. A well-built rubric skill is worth passing round; check the external setting first.

Gemini can now label sensitive Drive files, for schools on the right licence. On 17 November Google made Gemini-powered classification in Drive generally available. Admins describe a label in plain language, test it, then let Gemini apply it across Drive so labels can drive data-loss and retention rules; it will not overwrite labels people have set (Google, 17 November). In education it needs Google AI Pro for Education. For a school that must show where student records sit, losing the manual training step matters. A rule written in a sentence still needs testing on files whose contents you already know.

Sceptic's Corner

The centrepiece's remedy, change the task rather than guard the room, has a strong case against it. Korea is making it in public.

Redesign does not remove advantage; it moves it. When the National Education Commission's essay-type plans surfaced in August, one parent told Yonhap the family would simply add another academy, and a cram-school official expected demand for test technique to grow in the early years (Yonhap via Daum, 16 August). Grading fairness was the largest single concern in the commission's public comments: 754 of 3,246 (News1 via Financial News, 17 November). The ministry's plan for marking essays at scale is an AI that provisionally scores answers, trained on 1.1 million of them, in every regional education office by 2029 (Money Today, 5 August). The machine leaves the exam hall and returns as the marker.

Conversation-based checks have costs of their own. They reward the fluent and the confident, and a timed in-class write is harder on slow writers and anxious students.

Nor has the motive changed much. Stanford's Challenge Success surveys put the share of high schoolers reporting at least one cheating behaviour in the previous month at 60–70% for years, and the 2023 surveys, which added questions on tools like ChatGPT, found it about the same or slightly lower (Stanford GSE, 31 October 2023). The tool changes; the stakes drive it.

The line for the staffroom: redesigning the task is right, but it is neither free nor neutral. Before calling the new version fairer, check whom it favours.
