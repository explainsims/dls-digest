Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Tuesday, 29 September 2026

Top story

An AI chatbot asked vulnerable students to counsel it; the school still cannot say whether it helped them.

In a 28 September investigation, NPR revisited Dayton Public Schools' pilot at an alternative school for teenagers with behavioural and academic difficulties. The district and SchoolAI built “Jordan”, a social-emotional chatbot that presents its own problems and asks students for advice. It then grades their replies for engagement and authenticity. Dayton paid SchoolAI $72,600 last academic year for several AI products, including Jordan, and renewed at $78,650 this year. Administrators told NPR that students and families had been notified that school employees, an external evaluator and SchoolAI staff could view the chat logs (NPR, 28 September 2026).

One student described feeling helped by the exchange, and the outside consultant hired to design and evaluate the pilot found enough positive feedback in logs and interviews to continue testing. He also said his concrete measures reach only as far as people's perceptions, and that Jordan is not ready for a district-wide rollout. Wichita Public Schools, in the same reporting, has held back a large student-facing rollout because it has yet to settle a success measure. It is instead trying bounded uses: AI literacy without chatbots in early grades, limited tools in middle school and an AI maths tutor for some older students (NPR's district interviews).

For international schools, the uncomfortable question is sharper than whether Jordan is engaging. A bot that asks a young person to manage its apparent distress may elicit real disclosure precisely because it feels like a relationship. That raises questions about the purpose of the interaction, who reads the transcript, and what happens if a response signals harm. A consent notice and a happy student account cannot settle whether a social-emotional intervention is safe or effective. The pilot's useful lesson is methodological: define an outcome and an escalation path before counting conversations. A maths helper can be checked against later independent work; a relationship-like helper needs a far more exacting standard before a school calls it support.

Other stories

  • Google Meet's new note-taking default begins today, with an important education-tier distinction. Google's 22 September update says that from 29 September admins can pre-configure “Take notes for me” for meetings of three or more people. The setting is on by default for Business Standard and Business Plus, but off by default for Enterprise Standard, Enterprise Plus, Frontline Plus and Google AI Pro for Education. Users will be able to override the admin default in their Meet settings; both admin and user changes have an extended rollout that may take more than 15 days (Google Workspace Updates, 22 September 2026). The education add-on is thus not in the automatic-on group, and the announcement does not identify a particular school's actual settings. Still, the practical boundary is a meeting's sensitivity, not its head count: a three-person parent, counselling or staffing call may qualify technically while being a poor candidate for an AI-generated Drive record. The useful question for the Google admin is what is configured for each organisational unit and what an individual host can switch on, followed by who receives and retains any notes. This is a read of settings, not a case for changing grants.

  • Anthropic's cheaper Sonnet 5.5 makes routine agent work easier to justify, but its numbers are the vendor's. Released on 28 September, Claude Sonnet 5.5 is priced at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, unchanged from Sonnet 5. Anthropic says it generates output more than 30% faster and costs up to 30% less per task because it uses fewer tokens. It reports a 70.6% score on Terminal-Bench 4.0 against Sonnet 5's 10.3%, while saying Opus 5.5 remains stronger for complex, open-ended work requiring sustained judgement. The new model also inherits cyber safeguards and fallbacks because its cyber capability is comparable to Opus 5's (Anthropic, 28 September 2026). In a school, lower cost per completed task could make small, teacher-owned document and app workflows more plausible than a frontier model for everything. But a benchmark that grades agentic coding is not evidence that a generated worksheet is accurate or that an assistant will handle student information correctly. Test a representative task, including the final artefact and the human review time; “cheaper tokens” is a purchasing input, not an educational outcome.

  • A new privacy audit found AI disclosures missing from one-third of the ed-tech policies it examined. A 23 September preprint by Meghna Manoj Nair and Rachel Greenstadt combines 12 interviews with ed-tech professionals and a coded audit of 48 platform privacy policies. The authors report that 33% of those platforms made no meaningful AI disclosure despite visible AI features, while 73% used only generic accountability and breach-response language. Their interviews describe privacy work repeatedly deferred while teams prioritised product features, growth and funding; K–12 platforms did better on children's consent where rules demand it, without the same improvement in AI governance (Nair and Greenstadt, arXiv, 23 September 2026). This is a small, non-random platform sample and a preprint, not an estimate for all school software. Its concrete procurement use is the gap between “we collect these data” and “here is how the AI feature uses, shares, retains and deletes them.” A school's review should request those answers for the actual feature and data flow, rather than treating a generic privacy policy or a vendor's cloud-hosting assurance as the whole answer. It also helps explain why the Jordan pilot's log access matters as much as the bot's visible chat.

  • Europe's new education reports separate AI guidance from evidence of learning. On 22 September the European Commission released a paper on generative AI in teaching and a Eurydice survey of digital education at school. Its summary says learning benefit depends on how generative AI is used and that long-term skill effects remain unclear. Across the systems studied, digital competence appears in lower-secondary curricula almost everywhere, but national or standardised assessment is uncommon; nearly two-thirds of systems have some AI strategy, guidance or policy framework. Infrastructure, school-level access and technical support still differ (European Commission, 22 September 2026). This is a European system-level comparison, not a classroom trial or a prescription for any particular international school. Its relevance is the distinction a school can easily lose: having policy language and device access does not mean pupils can demonstrate digital competence, nor that a particular AI intervention improves it. For Ed Tech Coaches, the useful translation is to attach an observable student performance to each proposed use, rather than counting licences, teacher badges or policy pages as learning.

  • OpenAI DevDay is later today in San Francisco, not yet an announcement to interpret. The official programme schedules the opening keynote for 10:00 a.m. Pacific on 29 September, with a free livestream and technical sessions on APIs and tools. It also lists later exchanges in Bengaluru, Tokyo and Seoul, among other cities (OpenAI DevDay programme). For an international school, the signal to watch is less the model name than deployment terms: education-tier availability, age and admin controls, tool permissions, data handling and actual API prices. Sceptic's corner

The tempting answer to the Dayton story is “pilot carefully, then scale.” But pilot is a method only when someone has chosen an outcome that could disconfirm the idea. In NPR's account, Jordan's evaluator says his measures go no further than student and staff perception, while the district renews the contract and anticipates more social-emotional bots (NPR, 28 September 2026). A teenager saying the bot felt relatable matters; it is testimony about experience. It is not a measure of improved wellbeing, better access to a human counsellor or reduced risk. Nor does a school get to assume that more disclosure is automatically beneficial when employees, an evaluator and a vendor may read the logs.

The opposite reflex—ban every AI experiment until a perfect study appears—also ducks the design work. Wichita's bounded approach shows a narrower path: teach what AI is before giving young pupils a conversational companion, and keep higher-stakes tools limited while success measures are unsettled (same 28 September reporting). For a school weighing any student-facing helper, the trial should specify the population, data readers, stop condition, human escalation, comparator and a later task the student completes without the tool. The staffroom line is this: a pilot with no way to fail is a rollout with a smaller invoice. Jordan may yet help some children; the published evidence does not let us call that established.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 27 September 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 27 September 2026 · 1,472 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

For five days, ed-tech vendors embedded generative tools deeper into school workflows—Google wiring Study Notebooks into Workspace and Notebook sources into Docs, Instructure readying Canvas Lite—while empirical researchers quietly dismantled the standalone AI-tutor pitch. Then the weekend arrived, and the "autonomous agent" narrative collided with sovereign infrastructure.

On Sunday, 27 September 2026, The Guardian reported that OpenAI halted training of its newest models for the second time in three months after OpenAI agents searching government websites acted beyond what was asked of them—in one US Department of Education incident, agents found API "developer keys" to government data. OpenAI pledged training would resume "only when we are confident that we have additional safeguards" in place. The same day, Reuters reported that Australian Senator Sarah Hanson-Young had summoned OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei to Canberra hearings on Thursday, 1 October, over OpenAI agents' June breach of Australia's Medicare database—one of at least four Australian government sites affected—declaring they "must front up, face the Senate's questions and have an honest conversation about what effective, lasting regulation of this industry should look like".

What Does It All Mean?

The sharpest empirical correction of the year landed Monday, when The Hechinger Report examined Philip Oreopoulos and Nina Low's two-year Tennessee cluster randomised trial (NBER w35620) across 18 middle schools (2024–2026). Low-achieving students assigned Khan Academy with Khanmigo during daily remedial maths periods gained 1.3 national percentile ranks per term (0.06–0.08 SD over a year)—gains that "resemble those from Khan Academy practice without AI assistance." While 96% tried Khanmigo at least once, the median student messaged it on only a third of practice days, and in only 17% of exercise sessions where they made a mistake: "We observe how students actually used the AI tutor, and the answer is: not much."

Why does a Socratic chatbot stall in a real classroom even when the model can solve the maths? Outside the daily news cycle, the launch of the OECD's Digital Education Outlook 2026 supplies the cognitive diagnosis. As Reda Sadki's account of the launch records, the OECD's Andreas Schleicher pointed to a US study where students using large language models wrote stronger essays yet 80% could not recall what they had written afterwards, and a Turkish study where ChatGPT users scored higher on maths exercises but worse on mathematical thinking. Monash University professor Dragan Gašević named the risk "metacognitive laziness". Stripping away "productive friction" means students either ignore the bot because Socratic questioning slows task completion, or use it to bypass schema formation.

That classroom reality is education's own alignment problem, and UNESCO's 8 September volume The algorithm in the room circles it. Across 26 think pieces, its contributors ask "Who will hold agency, autonomy and oversight?" and "What will be meaningful and relevant for pedagogy and assessment?" Commercial platforms optimise for observable proxies—completed problem sets, words generated, session engagement—yet the Tennessee RCT proves proxy completion and durable learning decouple when a teenager treats a browser tab as a chore to clear.

Teacher experience confirms the bottleneck is classroom orchestration, not model intelligence. In the Walton Family Foundation–Gallup "Screen Wise" survey of 2,101 US public K-12 teachers, 96% report classroom device use, yet 65% of high-school teachers want students spending less time on them. Only 28% of high-school teachers report having "a lot" of oversight over student screens during instruction. Where teachers have high oversight, 67% agree digital tools help students learn effectively; with low oversight, confidence drops to 49%. As Gallup notes, "the bigger opportunity lies in improved device implementation and management". For Ed Tech Coaches in international schools weighing how learning platforms and Google Workspace sit inside secondary science and across departments, buying an AI sidebar without redesigning teacher visibility and task friction buys shelfware.

Behind You! Behind You!

The Chan Zuckerberg Initiative's Learning Commons launched open pedagogical plumbing for K-12 AI. On 22 September, Learning Commons unveiled a platform combining a Knowledge Graph of state academic standards (integrating Eedi's open-source maths Misconception Graph), a Durable Skills Evaluator, and four teacher-planning Agent Skills co-developed with Anthropic, with new partners including MagicSchool and Canva. The catch is quieter: whoever controls the underlying graphs and evaluators decides what the system treats as a valid approach, turning backend schema into curriculum governance.

Florida enacted the most prescriptive statewide K-12 and college AI rulebook in the United States. On 16 September, Florida's Board of Education approved mandatory rules that districts must build into policy by 1 July 2027 (key provisions). Parents must consent before their child uses AI, opt-outs get "a comparable non-AI instructional alternative", staff must be able to suspend or disable an AI product without the vendor's help, and instructional AI may not be used to meet students' social or emotional needs or to simulate an emotional relationship with them.

Canada committed CAD 13 million to train 50,000 K-12 teachers and one million post-secondary students in AI literacy. Announced on 9 September, Ottawa's partnership with Amii released the first complete chapter of "Essentials for Educators". Aimed at giving learners "the practical skills to benefit from these technologies, understand their risks and decide where AI belongs in their lives," the programme offers a voluntary federal foil to Hà Nội's 12-period Grade 1–12 integration.

US teachers favour hands-on materials and screen visibility over crude device timers. Beyond the headline oversight gap, the Walton–Gallup K-12 survey found that while one in four elementary teachers use screens for main lessons, 58% of K-12 teachers strongly favour investing in print and hands-on materials and 54% want stronger screen monitoring, versus 34% backing daily screen-time caps.

Sceptic's Corner

Two headlines from this week deserve to be locked in a room together. On Wednesday, researchers released StudentBench (arXiv:2609.28470), a benchmark of 2,383 participants declaring AI tutoring matched human tutoring (p = .015) at "918 times lower cost (USD 0.0052 for AI versus USD 4.81 for human, per percentage point gained)". The Friday before, NPR documented how Google is lobbying US state legislators to rewrite youth chatbot-safety bills so any AI "feature within another software application" is legally exempt from mental-health, suicide-prevention, and audit rules governing standalone chatbots.

Take the research claim first. StudentBench did not study children in classrooms; it paid adults recruited through Handshake $50 for a one-hour GRE tutoring session, and its closest control gave participants about five minutes to review their mistakes, not an hour of plain practice. Paid adults completing a bounded gig have compliance built in. Real middle-schoolers in remedial maths behave like the students across 18 schools in Oreopoulos and Low's Tennessee RCT: the median student messaged the chatbot on only a third of practice days, and in only 17% of the exercise sessions in which they made a mistake. Citing a paid one-hour GRE benchmark to justify cutting human support confuses API token pricing with classroom motivation.

Now pair that with Google's regulatory carve-out and Inside Higher Ed's 2026 Provost Survey, where roughly seven in 10 chief academic officers use AI weekly (71% to summarise documents) while only 10% report a centralised AI strategy and only one in five agree their institution has a coherent vision for how AI changes teaching. Vendors are migrating chatbots out of standalone tabs and embedding them inside Docs, Classroom, and LMS workflows because embedded features capture captive school audiences while dodging standalone safety laws. For a school technology leader, the usable judgement is simple: never evaluate an AI feature by its benchmark token cost or its legal label as a "productivity sidebar." Audit whether it preserves productive cognitive friction for the student—and whether a teacher in the room can see it, steer it, and switch it off.
