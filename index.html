Tuesday, 19 May 2026

Top story

Google I/O day starts with the school-relevant action already in Workspace.

Google I/O opens today, but the school-facing story is not only the keynote. The most practical signal is that Google has already been knitting Gemini and NotebookLM into the systems schools actually live in: Workspace, LMS integrations, Meet, Chrome, and admin controls.

The clearest new example is Google's 12 May expansion of Google Workspace Assignments LTI and Gemini LTI to Moodle, joining existing support for Canvas and Schoology. Gemini LTI brings the Gemini app and NotebookLM into third-party LMS environments, with access controlled through both Workspace admin settings and LMS admin setup. Google says students can use the integration for course-specific guidance, NotebookLM audio overviews, and AI training resources; Assignments LTI continues to handle distribution, grading, and originality reports. Availability is broad across Education Fundamentals, Standard, and Plus. (Google Workspace Updates).

For international schools, this is the bit worth underlining before the I/O fireworks: Google is making Gemini portable into non-Google LMS contexts. The practical question is less "Do we use Google Classroom?" and more "Can our learning platform expose Google AI safely, governably, and without fragmenting student workflows?" That makes Gemini LTI, NotebookLM permissions, Workspace Intelligence controls, and age-based access rules central to the AI governance conversation.

I/O's Google keynote is scheduled for 19 May, 10:00 PT. Official source: Google I/O schedule; Google's own preview says I/O will cover AI breakthroughs and updates "from Gemini to Android and more" (The Keyword).

Other Stories

  • NotebookLM is becoming a Workspace automation source, not just a study tool. Google added an Ask NotebookLM step to Google Workspace Studio flows. That means existing notebooks can serve as grounded knowledge sources inside automations, provided Gemini for Workspace steps are allowed. Rollout began 12 May for Rapid and Scheduled Release domains, with availability across Business, Enterprise, and Education editions, including Education Fundamentals, Standard, Plus, Google AI Pro for Education, and Teaching and Learning. The school interpretation: NotebookLM is moving from "student/teacher research assistant" toward "institutional workflow substrate." That is powerful, but it raises governance questions fast: whose notebook, which sources, which organizational unit, what compliance coverage, and who sees outputs? (Google Workspace Updates).

  • Workspace Intelligence gives Gemini broader context, with admin controls that matter. Google's Workspace Intelligence update grounds Gemini tasks across Gmail, Chat, Calendar, Drive, Docs, Sheets, and Slides. Admins can disable specific data sources at the domain, organizational-unit, or group level, and Google says responses respect user-level permissions and customer data is not used for model training or ads. For schools, this is a governance story more than a productivity story. The default is "on," so the real admin work is not whether Gemini can search across Workspace; it is deciding where broad context is appropriate, where it is not, and how that maps to student age bands, staff roles, shared drives, and sensitive records. (Google Workspace Updates).

  • Google Meet adds explicit-consent controls for AI notes, transcripts, and recordings. Google is rolling out an admin setting requiring participants to actively consent before automatic note-taking, recording, or transcription begins. The setting is off by default and can be enabled by domain, organizational unit, or group. Education Plus gets consent controls for transcripts and recordings; Google AI Pro for Education includes notes, recordings, and transcripts. This is immediately relevant to staff meetings, parent meetings, student support conversations, and any AI note-taking norms. The feature is not glamorous. It is exactly the kind of control that makes AI adoption survivable in schools. (Google Workspace Updates).

  • Canvas breach shifts from operational disruption to accountability. The Canvas/Instructure story has not produced a fresh technical turn today, but the aftermath is still material. AP reports Instructure struck a deal with the hackers to return data and received "digital confirmation" of deletion, while also acknowledging there is no complete certainty when dealing with cybercriminals. TechRadar reports Instructure CEO Steve Daly has been called to testify before the U.S. House Homeland Security Committee, with the hearing expected to cover breach details, data volume, containment, and customer notification. For schools, the lesson is unchanged but sharper: vendor trust needs evidence. "Data returned" and "shred logs" are not the same as assurance. (AP, TechRadar).

  • Android's Gemini Intelligence is a student-device story in disguise. Google's pre-I/O Android announcement pushed Gemini Intelligence toward proactive, cross-app assistance: app automation, Gemini in Chrome, smarter autofill, spoken-message rewriting, and generated widgets. Rollout starts with newer Samsung Galaxy and Pixel devices this summer, with broader device categories later. Even in Apple-forward schools, this is a student reality headline. Students will increasingly arrive with phones that can summarize, automate, fill forms, rewrite speech, and act across apps. AI policy that only thinks in terms of "websites students visit" will miss the device-layer assistant. (Google).

  • I/O developer keynote: watch whether Google pushes more school-relevant agent tooling through Workspace APIs, connector frameworks, Gemini LTI, or Chrome Enterprise/Education.

Sceptic's Corner

The lazy reading of Google's education AI story is "Gemini everywhere." That is true, but not sharp enough. The sharper reading is that Google is turning school AI from a tool-choice question into an admin-boundary question. Gemini LTI inside an LMS, NotebookLM as an automation source, Workspace Intelligence across Drive/Gmail/Chat/Calendar, and Meet consent settings are not four separate product updates. They are four places where a school either draws a boundary or inherits Google's default one.

That is why the I/O keynote is almost the wrong object to stare at. A slick demo can make Gemini look inevitable; the admin console decides whether inevitability becomes governed practice or ambient leakage. The practical sceptic's question is not "is Gemini good?" It is: which data source, which organizational unit, which age band, which meeting type, which notebook, which LMS surface, and which audit trail? If the answer is "we'll work that out after teachers start using it," the school has already accepted the vendor's governance model by default.

Google may well have the most school-native AI channel because Workspace already sits under the work. That is exactly why the scrutiny has to be harder, not softer. The closer a tool is to daily practice, the less it should be allowed to arrive as background weather.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 17 May 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 17 May 2026

Classify artefacts before classifying tools: student records, teacher-authored curriculum, operational workflows, and public communications are not the same risk surface.

The Week That Was

The week began with the Canvas breach still on the clock and ended with a cleaner, odder shape: Instructure paid ShinyHunters, Congress asked for a briefing, class actions split across Utah and New York, and Instructure's own incident page finally named the Free-for-Teacher route into Canvas. The Saturday Toddle webinar then made the adjacent point without meaning to: LMS vendors are no longer just storing assignments. They are putting AI into curriculum planning, reporting, communications, and governance work. (Instructure, Toddle)

The other thread was quieter but more durable. Google launched AI educator training for US teachers; OpenAI kept hardening the admin layer for ChatGPT Edu agents; the EU Council adopted teacher-centred AI conclusions. The market is now building both the classroom-facing fluency layer and the institutional-control layer at the same time. (Google, OpenAI, EU Council)

What Does It All Mean?

The useful question this week is not "which vendor is safer?" That is too flat. The better question is: when AI becomes part of school infrastructure, what kind of school artefact is it allowed to touch?

Canvas exposed the obvious class of artefact: names, emails, course enrolments, message contents. That is traditional student-data territory, and the vocabulary around it is reasonably mature: breach notification, FERPA, state AG reporting, incident response, third-party processor, remediation. The US Department of Education's late-May privacy webinars are almost comically well-timed here: one session on transparency, one on incident response and vetting educational technology. (Protecting Student Privacy)

Toddle points at the less mature class of artefact. Its PYP Refresh workshop framed Toddle AI as a curriculum-realignment assistant: revise units, unpack 2025 PYP updates, strengthen planning, use the Curriculum Design Assistant in breakout work. That is not student-grade data, but it is not nothing. Curriculum maps, teacher-authored units, assessment language, intervention notes, school-home communication templates, and local adaptation decisions all reveal how a school thinks. They are institutional knowledge, not just files.

This is where the weekly needs to leave the breach story and do the slower work. The OECD's PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy project defines the coming assessment domain around students' ability to act critically and ethically in AI-mediated environments. The EU Council's conclusions put teachers at the centre: AI should preserve teacher autonomy, learner well-being, data protection, accountability, and risk awareness. Those two policy moves are not about picking Gemini over Claude over ChatGPT. They are about whether schools can still name the human judgement in a system once the workflow has been partially handed to software. (OECD PISA, EU Council)

That is also why OpenAI's admin controls matter more than the demo videos. Workspace agents in ChatGPT Edu can connect tools, schedule runs, ask approval for sensitive steps, expose analytics, and give admins visibility through a compliance API. This is the boring layer that turns AI from teacher enthusiasm into governed service. Google's AI Educator Series is the other half: broad professional fluency, currently US-first, with Gemini and NotebookLM as training currency. One side teaches people what to do. The other side lets institutions see and constrain what was done. Neither is sufficient alone.

For international schools, the framing to carry is simple: classify artefacts before classifying tools. Student records are one class. Teacher-authored curriculum is another. Operational workflows are a third. Public-facing communications are a fourth. Once those classes exist, the vendor questions become sharper: which artefacts enter the model workflow; where are they processed; can they train a model; who can export them; what logs exist; what happens when a free-teacher surface touches the paid-school environment. That is a better rubric than "AI feature: yes/no."

Behind You! Behind You!

The retracted ChatGPT-learning paper is the research story that will keep mattering after the breach news cools. Ars Technica covered the retraction of an influential paper that had claimed strong learning benefits from ChatGPT. Ben Williamson's caveat is the one to remember: the headline finding may persist in policy and vendor decks even after the paper is gone. (Ars Technica)

The EU Council conclusions landed in the exact register international schools borrow from. Teacher autonomy, well-being, inclusion, data protection, accountability, risk awareness: not flashy, but that is policy language with legs. It gives school leaders a non-vendor vocabulary for saying "AI, yes, but not as automation theatre." (EU Council)

OpenAI's compliance story is becoming a governance asset. The agent launch is not just "make a bot." The useful bit is admin visibility into configurations, updates, runs, connected tools, approvals, and suspension. Schools will ask for exactly this layer once agents move from pilots to department routines. (OpenAI)

Instructure's Free-for-Teacher shutdown deserves its own post-mortem slot. The vendor says both the April 29 and May 7 activity used a Free-for-Teacher account, and that the product is temporarily shut down while a safer return path is designed. Free teacher entry points are innovation channels. They are also trust-boundary tests. (Instructure)

  • Late May / June — watch for IB AI Design Principles and any AILit final-framework movement. OECD's PISA 2029 MAIL page still points to preliminary framework work.
  • When available — Instructure's promised forensics summary. That is the next source worth reading closely.

Sceptic's Corner

"Human-centred AI" can become wallpaper if schools don't operationalise it. The EU wording is good. So is OECD's critical-literacy direction. But the phrase only earns its place if a school can point to concrete constraints: approval gates, data-classification rules, curriculum-artifact handling, parent-facing disclosure, teacher opt-out boundaries, and logs that a human can actually read. Otherwise "teacher at the centre" becomes the new "personalised learning" — true in the slide deck, foggy in the room.

Vendor fluency training is not the same as institutional judgement. Google's AI Educator Series may be useful; it will almost certainly become common reference currency. But training people to use Gemini and NotebookLM is not the same as teaching them when to refuse a tool, how to inspect data flows, or how to recognise a retracted evidence claim when it reappears in a sales deck.

The safest-sounding product may be the one that touches the most sensitive non-student data. A curriculum assistant looks calmer than a gradebook breach because no one says "social security number." But curriculum is a school's intellectual infrastructure. Once AI systems help rewrite it, schools need governance language for teacher-authored and institution-authored knowledge, not just student records. That is the blind spot this week exposed.
