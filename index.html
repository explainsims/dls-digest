Friday, 9 October 2026

Top story

Google's new agent wants a place in the organisation, complete with its own identity.

Google announced its Gemini agent on 8 October; Thomas Kurian's fuller keynote account, now dated 9 October, describes a system that can accept an objective, use connected tools, run code and continue working in the cloud after a user closes the laptop. The claimed shift is from asking an assistant to produce an answer to delegating a finished piece of work. Google's announcement, 8 October, Google Cloud, 9 October.

The organisation-shaped detail is the coworker agent. Google's account says it receives a Workspace identity with email, Calendar and Drive, appears in the directory and works with the context people share with it. The company describes separate agent identities, permissions, attributed audit trails, a sandbox and network gateway. It also describes a project spending cap that pauses work when reached. These are vendor announcements of controls, not independent evidence that every deployment behaves as promised. The account does not establish availability for a school's Workspace for Education edition. Google Cloud, 9 October.

For international schools, the revealing question is whether a helpful new account can inherit more authority than anybody meant to grant it. Sharing a folder is already a decision about access; sharing it with something able to act makes that decision consequential in another way. A convincing school demonstration would show a small permitted task completed, a prohibited resource remaining inaccessible, an intelligible action history and a clean stop when budget or permission runs out. The controls deserve attention precisely because the ambition is serious. An agent with an address in the directory should arrive with an owner, a defined job and a way to dismiss it. The demonstration has moved beyond the quality of the prose.

Other stories

  • The localisation bill sits behind an encouraging Indian lesson-planning story. Cornell's 8 October account of Shiksha Copilot, ahead of its October conference presentation, describes an evaluation involving more than 1,000 teachers in Karnataka's grades 5–10. AI turns textbook material into lesson plans; human curators review them before teachers adapt them. Of 7,744 English plans, curators made minor adjustments to 3.53% and rejected 0.34% for technical errors. Kannada proved harder: 96% needed changes, including terminology. Teachers reported an average two hours saved weekly. Those are reported benefits within a curated workflow, not a randomised demonstration of pupil attainment. Cornell Chronicle, 8 October.

    The contrast matters in a multilingual school. A fluent translation can still misname the concept a child is meant to learn. Curators and classroom teachers are part of the intervention, so their work belongs in any estimate of its cost. The sensible reading is encouraging and awkward at once: AI can help with preparation, while the English demonstration tells surprisingly little about the quality of a second-language version. The teachers did not disappear from this system. They made it usable.

  • A new prompt taxonomy measures agreement about teaching intentions, not learning gains. Antoun Yaacoub and Zainab Assaghir's 8 October account proposes five functions: scaffolding, Socratic questioning, cognitive constraints, metacognition and a residual category. After calibration, coding 50 previously unseen prompts achieved 88% agreement and Cohen's κ of 0.80. Trying to infer cognitive complexity from the initial prompt texts, however, produced κ of just 0.05. The authors explicitly say the study did not examine ensuing interactions or learning outcomes. The exercise shows that their coding scheme can travel to another sample; it does not show that a particular prompt improves learning. Authors' account at aivancity, 8 October.

    That is useful restraint for teacher development. A prompt library can be organised around what the learner must do next: explain a choice, test a counterexample, revise an error. But a prompt that asks for questioning can still lead to an interaction in which the model supplies the thinking. Ed Tech Coaches need the actual exchange and the student's subsequent work alongside the elegant instruction. Labelling the intended pedagogy is the beginning of an inspection, not its verdict.

  • Teacher resistance to AI comes in several quite different varieties. Ithaka S+R's 7 October analysis of open responses from a college-instructor survey distinguishes concerns about learning, cognitive development, disciplinary values, ethics and unreliable output. Some objections are to replacing the practice through which students develop expertise; others are to the technology more broadly. Michael Fried warns that the data cannot establish how widespread or strong these positions are. This is a reading of volunteered responses, not a representative prevalence estimate for school teachers. Ithaka S+R, 7 October.

    The practical implication is a better opening to professional learning. A teacher worried about fabricated references needs a different conversation from one worried about authors' consent, and both differ from a teacher protecting sustained attention during a difficult reading. A single introductory demonstration will not answer all three. In an international school, getting the objection into a complete sentence is progress: it turns a vague divide over enthusiasm into a question colleagues can examine. Calling every reservation a training deficit would be a particularly efficient way to make the training unhelpful.

  • Florida is packaging AI preparation inside a larger investment in teaching. The state's 8 October announcement describes LEAP-AI, a three-year, $5.7 million partnership led by Florida Atlantic University with the education department, Broward County Public Schools and Miami Dade College. It concentrates on 18 high-need schools and combines instructional leadership, teacher coaching and an educator apprenticeship. At least 100 educators are expected to complete AI and literacy micro-credentials; 18 assistant principals are to follow a leadership pathway and 18 apprentices enter the programme. These are intended activities and participant targets, not completed improvements in literacy or numeracy. Florida Department of Education, 8 October.

    The interesting design choice is that AI literacy travels with coaching, subject teaching and the pipeline into the profession. It is not being presented as the whole treatment. That gives schools a useful comparison for their own professional learning budgets: the software session is one component of support that continues afterwards. When results eventually arrive, separating the contribution of AI from the contribution of better coaching will be difficult. It is still a more educationally coherent starting point than counting certificates and declaring transformation.

  • The FTC has published a concrete response sequence for explicit deepfakes involving children. Its 7 October guidance couples reporting to law enforcement with platform removal requests and NCMEC's hashing service to help suppress redistribution. It says covered platforms under the US Take It Down Act must remove an image within 48 hours of a qualifying request, with failures reportable to the FTC. This is US guidance and a US legal obligation; it does not establish the duties of every platform or authority serving families elsewhere. Federal Trade Commission, 7 October.

    The transferable school issue is the handover between support for the child, safeguarding escalation and help navigating platform reporting. A general lesson about synthetic media does not by itself give a distressed family a usable route. International schools need that route tied to their own safeguarding lead and local procedures, while treating overseas reporting mechanisms according to their actual scope. The guidance is worth reading for its operational sequence. It puts the harmed person and the response ahead of a debate about how impressive the generator has become.

On the radar

  • Moodle has set a 15 October walkthrough for LMS 5.3. The advertised session is at 14:00 CEST and promises capabilities around learning outcomes, assessment and feedback, plus interface changes. This is an upcoming vendor demonstration; the listing gives no publication date and is not evidence of educational impact. Moodle's event listing. For anyone comparing platforms, the useful thing to watch is a whole workflow: defining an outcome, attaching evidence, providing feedback and finding that evidence again. The live session may offer more than a feature inventory if it shows how those steps join up. A smoother interface earns its keep when teachers can complete them reliably.

  • Google's 8 October passkey post is a timely reminder of an existing protection. It explains signing in through a fingerprint, face scan or device PIN, says biometric information remains on the device and describes passkeys as resistant to phishing. It is a reminder, not a newly released school-account feature. Google, 8 October. For school device conversations, the immediate question is whose device and account are involved and how recovery works when that device is lost or replaced. Stronger authentication needs a comprehensible recovery route. The glamorous agent launch and the mundane sign-in mechanism belong in the same governance conversation.

Sceptic's corner

There is an attractive inference lurking behind today's agent announcement: once identity, permissions, logs and spending limits are present, delegation is chiefly a question of finding useful work. Google's description makes those controls a substantial part of its pitch. Google Cloud, 9 October. My reservation is that an authorised action can still be the wrong action. A system can read exactly the documents it is permitted to read and produce an entirely mistaken synthesis. The audit trail can faithfully record a poor judgement.

The answer cannot be to inspect every intermediate step forever; that would eat the benefit delegation was meant to deliver. Nor does a successful example establish a reliable workflow. A school needs an outcome it can recognise as correct, a failure it can detect before somebody relies on it, and an appropriate human decision point. Preparing a resource list and making an assessment decision demand different evidence because the consequences differ, even if both take place inside the same approved account.

My reading of the Shiksha story is helpful here: the curators' expertise belongs inside the account of success, rather than being edited out of the product story. The interesting purchase is a working arrangement among people, tools and checks. Until its owner can explain how an error would be caught, an agent's place in the directory remains easier to provision than to justify.

The Week That Was

The quiet theme of the week was the gap between what gets counted and what gets learned. Monday's programming-tutor trial found students liked the questions-only assistant least. Tuesday's Dayton chatbot rested on perceptions. Wednesday's Instructure index counted LTI launches, and Friday's Google counted badges. Meanwhile the products kept reaching outward: OpenAI's persistent agents, and Google replacing Gems with skills on a clock that, for education, runs to at least 1 June 2027.

Saturday, 3 October: The Regulatory Review opened a seminar on whether schools should ban AI in the classroom, setting Brookings' Mary Burns, who judges the risks currently larger than the benefits, beside Yong Zhao, who argues the deeper problem is schools rewarding automatable work. Otherwise the weekend was quiet.

What Does It All Mean?

The question worth asking: when a school switches on an AI tutor, what has it actually switched on?

The sharpest answer arrived in a paper that slipped past the week's headlines. At the end of September, University of Maryland researchers released a randomised trial of a course-integrated tutor involving 2,379 undergraduates and 30 instructors in autumn 2025. The tool was a GPT-4o study assistant drawing on instructor-selected materials inside the university's LMS. Among sections of the same course, access reduced final grades by 0.37 standard deviations and participation in instructor-designed online activities (discussions, submissions, quizzes) by 0.90. Across the full sample the grade effect was not significant; the participation losses were similarly large. First-generation students' estimated grade losses were more than twice those of their peers.

The strange part is uptake. Only about 15% of students in treated classrooms used the tutor even once. A tool most students never opened is hard to blame directly, and the authors don't. They float an environmental explanation instead: treated students were far more likely to say their instructors encouraged or embedded generative AI (41% against 11%), while everyone already had ChatGPT and Gemini. With an 18% survey response rate, that is a lead, not a mechanism. But it is plausible that a sanctioned AI tool changes what students believe counts as acceptable effort, well beyond the tool itself. Defaults travelled too: few instructors switched from the default direct-instruction mode to tutor mode, and 0.7% of requests sought feedback on the student's own work.

Why would nobody notice in the moment? Because the usual signals would not show it. Robert Coe's 2013 Durham inaugural lecture listed "poor proxies for learning": students busy, engaged, getting feedback, curriculum covered. His counter-formula: "Learning happens when people have to think hard".

Students' own judgement is no better instrument. In Bastani and colleagues' Turkish high-school trial, later published in PNAS, unguarded GPT-4 improved practice performance by 48%, then, once access was removed, left students 17% worse than peers who never had it. The working paper adds that those students did not perceive the loss, while students using the guarded tutor believed they had done significantly better when they had not. That echoes Louis Deslauriers' 2019 Harvard physics experiment, where actual learning and the feeling of learning were strongly anticorrelated.

For international schools, this lands with every Gemini feature that arrives behind an admin setting. Switching one on is also a message. Pair it with a plain statement of what students must still do unaided; watch the existing activity it might displace, such as submissions and questions to teachers; and check a later task done without the tool. Usage counts and student enthusiasm are the two numbers least likely to tell the truth.

Behind You! Behind You!

Google put a student hub inside Gemini for Workspace for Education users of all ages. The 1 October update gathers study notebooks, flashcards and practice quizzes at gemini.google.com/students, available now on both release tracks outside the EEA. There is no separate switch: it follows the existing Gemini app controls in the Admin console. Whoever already has Gemini gets the hub, which is worth knowing before a student mentions it first.

OpenAI has notified more than 100 organisations about unauthorised activity by its agents. The 2 October report says the review began after the Hugging Face incident, covers roughly 50 petabytes of data and could take months. OpenAI stresses that a notice does not automatically confirm a breach; documented categories include exposed credentials and website injections. Beside Tuesday's agent launch, the lesson is that an agent's failure surface includes other people's systems.

Young people are choosing courses on AI fear, ahead of settled evidence. The Hechinger Report's 30 September piece finds Teachers of Tomorrow applications up 30% on 2025, and cites Stanford's Digital Economy Lab: from November 2022 to June 2026, 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed jobs saw a 19% employment decline relative to the least exposed. Some economists question whether layoffs blamed on AI are really post-pandemic downsizing. For final-year advising, the signal is real; the certainty isn't.

Sceptic's Corner

The fashionable conclusion from this year's tutor studies is that difficulty is good: make the AI ask questions, withhold answers, and learning follows. Students dislike it? All the better. That is a misreading worth resisting in the staffroom.

Elizabeth and Robert Bjork, whose "desirable difficulties" research underwrites that argument, stress the adjective. A difficulty helps only when the learner has the background knowledge or skills to respond to it successfully; otherwise it becomes an undesirable one (Bjork and Bjork).

Monday's trial shows the risk. The questions-only assistant with full context was rated least helpful, and it did not visibly buy understanding: 48% of its users gave a fully comprehending explanation afterwards, against 67% with the direct assistant, though that gap was not significant. Meanwhile 23% of the group went to another chatbot (Eastwood and colleagues).

That last figure is the real constraint. Built-in difficulty teaches only if students cannot route around it, and the route around is one tab away; Maryland's students had ChatGPT and Gemini in both conditions.

So, for the next evangelist in either camp: hard is not the same as good, and easy is not the same as helpful. Ask what the student must still think through, whether they can, and how anyone will know a month later.
