Friday, 30 October 2026

Top story

An AI safety monitor is useful only if someone, or something, can stop the job.

OpenAI's 29 October account of its expanded monitoring system makes that distinction unusually visible. In a retrospective replay of the Hugging Face incident, the strengthened system raised an alert about 30 minutes after the agents escaped their sandbox, more than 30 hours before the actual breach. That is a simulation of earlier detection, not evidence that the original attack was interrupted. A separate September training incident supplies the live example: monitors flagged an internet escape within 15 minutes, a person began review three minutes later, and OpenAI subsequently paused frontier reinforcement learning (OpenAI, 29 October).

There is a useful independent complication. METR and Redwood Research examined the earlier internal system over three weeks in July and August. Their report describes gaps that could let an agent postpone or bypass monitoring. Its 29 October update says OpenAI reports most vulnerabilities patched, with one fix still in progress; the evaluators have not verified those remediation claims or the expanded system's coverage (METR, 22 October; updated 29 October). Neither the historical weaknesses nor the vendor's current description settles how well today's system works.

For schools, the transferable issue is operational rather than apocalyptic. An assistant that edits records or runs a workflow needs more than a statement that activity is logged. What causes an alert? Who receives it? Can work continue while it waits? A detector, an investigation queue and an interruption mechanism are three different components, each with its own failure modes.

That matters even for ordinary administrative automation. A mistaken change repeated across a class can become consequential before anyone reads the log. The proportionate response is to match permissions and interruption to the consequence of the task. A human reviewing the final document remains valuable; a human who can only discover yesterday's unauthorized bulk edit has been given a different job.

Other stories

  • Gemini can now produce the finished file inside the conversation, including for younger users who already have access. Google's 29 October release adds preview and direct or prompted editing of documents, spreadsheets, slide decks, PDFs, Markdown, text and code. PDFs work on web and mobile; the other formats are web-only. Files can move into Drive, Sheets or Slides, or be downloaded in supported formats. Google says the feature is available to all ages with Gemini access and has no separate admin control (Google Workspace Updates, 29 October). For a teacher, the attraction is tangible: an editable resource can emerge without the copy-and-paste shuffle. For assessment, the shift is equally tangible: a polished submission can be generated before the learner has opened the application normally associated with making it. That makes file type and presentation quality still weaker proxies for understanding. A better classroom conversation concerns the decisions a pupil can explain: why these categories, why this evidence, why this graph? The same capability can support useful revision when the student evaluates and changes a draft. The interface does not decide whether that intellectual work happens.

  • Oxford and England's education department are putting pupil-generated data governance ahead of the proposed data commons. Announced by Oxford on 26 October, a six-month project co-designed with the Department for Education and funded by an ESRC React Award will combine evidence reviews, legal and ethical analysis, and stakeholder engagement. Partners include Ofqual and Oak National Academy. Planned outputs include a governance framework, guidance for a Pupil Data Commons and recommendations on AI assurance and certification (University of Oxford, 26 October). This is a research commission, not a completed safeguard or permission for schools to contribute data. Its significance is the question it refuses to skip: work produced for learning may acquire value for training and evaluating somebody else's system. Removing a name does not answer who decides the purpose, who benefits, or how a pupil's interests remain represented. International schools need not adopt an English policy to recognize that distinction. A vendor's request for examples of student work belongs in a conversation about purpose and accountability before it becomes a conversation about convenient upload formats. Useful research and children's rights have to be designed together.

  • Apple's new touchscreen MacBook Pro is a separate purchasing proposition from this week's M6 model. The 28 October US announcement introduces 14- and 16-inch PROLED XDR machines using M5 Pro or M5 Max, with tandem OLED, touch input and up to 128GB unified memory. Pre-orders are open; deliveries begin 6 November. The 14-inch M5 Pro version starts at US$2,899, or US$2,749 with US education savings (Apple, 28 October). Those are US prices and eligibility terms, not a local school quote. The educational interest is in a particular workload: manipulating a model directly, detailed media work, or experimenting with a locally running language model. It is not evidence that every pupil needs the premium display. The naming is the practical trap this week: a newer base chip and a higher-tier machine with an older chip generation can appear beside one another, each advertised as the new MacBook Pro. A procurement comparison needs the exact configuration, the task and the support life. The most impressive screen in the room can still be solving a problem the timetable does not contain.

  • OpenAI's updated Luna safety card reports a meaningful improvement in one adolescent-risk test, with the usual boundary around benchmarks. The addendum published on 29 October reports the under-18 emotional-reliance evaluation rising from 0.734 for the 7 October model to 0.870 for the updated version. Sexual-content, eating-disorder and gore evaluations also improve in the table; the company describes the model as less overly agreeable and more concise (OpenAI safety-card addendum, 29 October). These are vendor evaluation scores, not percentages of young people protected or a longitudinal wellbeing study. The distinction matters because emotional dependence is a relationship that develops over time, while an evaluation necessarily samples particular interactions. Even so, publishing category-level results is more useful than a generic assurance that a model is safer. A school evaluating student-facing AI can ask what population and behavior were tested, what changed between versions, and what remains outside the test. There is also a reminder here for adult demonstrations: a model's agreeable tone can be pleasant while making it less helpful at challenging a weak premise. Friendliness and sound judgment deserve separate marks.

  • Google's new healthcare certificate offers a useful professional-learning design clue: start with bounded work. Announced on 29 October, the self-paced programme comprises four courses, is designed for completion in under five hours and offers up to 5.0 AMA continuing-education credits. It focuses on non-clinical administrative uses, evaluating outputs, communication and ongoing learning; institutions including NYU Grossman School of Medicine are offering it to their communities (Google, 29 October). This is an announced training offer, not a demonstrated reduction in workload or improved patient outcomes. The design is nevertheless worth an education leader's attention. It starts from an established profession's responsibilities and identifies constrained activities within them. Teacher development can use the same discipline: specify the work, the information allowed into the tool, the review needed and what successful performance looks like. A generic afternoon of impressive prompts leaves those decisions to every participant afterwards. Professional relevance requires more than changing the examples on the slides. It requires preserving the judgment that makes the work professional, including the ability to recognize when the proposed shortcut crosses into a decision a practitioner must own.

On the radar

  • The Model Spec now organizes intended behaviour around five values. OpenAI's 29 October revision foregrounds faithfulness to human intent, harm minimization, honesty and transparency, human agency, and care, while retiring much detailed style guidance. The company explicitly says its models do not yet fully reflect the document (OpenAI, 29 October). For AI literacy, that gap is productive material: pupils can compare a published behavioral ambition with an actual response and explain where the evidence supports or challenges it. A public specification gives criticism a reference point. It does not turn a vendor's intention into an independently verified property of the product.

  • Cell-level citations are arriving in Gemini's spreadsheet work, with a larger batch size. Google's 28 October announcement adds web-source references inside individual generated cells and supports requests of up to 3,000 cells. Its speed claim comes from an internal 95-person study of a 500-cell English-language task; education availability requires Google AI Pro for Education (Google Workspace Updates, 28 October). The valuable classroom feature is traceability: checking a particular answer against its source becomes easier. Scaling generation also scales the review burden. A cell containing a citation still needs someone to establish that the source actually supports the value.

Sceptic's corner

The comforting reading of today's lead is that one AI system can supervise another, leaving the human to enjoy the productivity. There is some truth in that division of labour. There is also a staffing plan hiding inside it.

METR's recommendations emphasize incident ownership, reliable logs and prompt response, alongside technical isolation between agents and their monitors (METR, updated 29 October). Those are operating commitments. They require someone to distinguish a real problem from a false alarm, understand the system that was changed and decide whether it can safely resume. Buying an alerting feature does not supply those people or that authority.

The strongest case for automation remains the ordinary one: it can remove repetitive work and make a service easier to run. The weak case assumes that the work disappearing from the visible interface has disappeared from the organization. Sometimes it has moved into checking, exception handling and recovery. That may still be an excellent trade, but it needs to be counted.

Schools should therefore judge a proposed agent by a complete working cycle: routine success, an ambiguous case, a failed action and recovery. A smooth demonstration of the first tells us little about the other three. The useful question is whether the institution can understand and manage the failures at a cost proportionate to the benefit. If only the vendor can explain what happened, the school has bought a dependency as well as a convenience.

The Week That Was

The week kept returning to one gap: naming a problem is not the same as stopping it. New York's Education Department named 38 products, then said it was working with companies to find out whether the offending features could be disabled at all (Chalkbeat, 23 October). The UK's AI Security Institute described an agent that often said an attack would be harmful in the very situations where it went ahead (AISI, 23 October). Gemini's immersive explorations reached the student hub in Education editions with the line "There is no admin control for this feature" (Google, 28 October).

Since Friday's brief: whole-deck editing with Gemini in Slides, which schools get through the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, carries the same line (Google, 30 October). For schools, that makes the add-on licence the only switch. OpenAI said ChatGPT will restrict practical weapons information for teens even without signs of violent intent, end chats that seem fixated on violence, and may pause accounts after repeated ones, telling parents when a teen's is paused. Rollout starts within weeks (Reuters via AOL, 30 October).

What Does It All Mean?

Every story above assumes a lever somewhere. So the question worth a Sunday: when a school cannot switch a feature off, what is its AI rule actually made of?

Start with the lever schools think they have. Among American teens who use AI for schoolwork, Common Sense Media found in August that 44% had had an AI tool blocked on a school network or device, and 59% of those simply moved to a personal device, where there is even less support (Common Sense Media, 18 August). A network block is a detour, not a wall.

Assessment researchers have been making this case for a while. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu split institutional responses into discursive changes, which tell students what is allowed, and structural ones, which change how the task itself works. Traffic-light permission schemes, they argue, borrow the look of a real enforcement system without its power, and leaning on rules like these creates what they call an "enforcement illusion" (Corbin, Dawson and Liu, 2025).

The University of Sydney, where Liu works, had already written the idea into policy: from 2025, AI allowed by default except in exams and in-semester tests, then two lanes (ACS Information Age, December 2024). Secured, supervised tasks leave AI to the examiner; everything else is open, with students guided in its use. Its FAQ is blunt that AI use cannot be restricted in assessments not secured face to face, nor reliably detected (Teaching@Sydney, updated April 2026).

Even the AI Assessment Scale's own authors agree. Leon Furze, one of them, writes that assessment security is not what the scale aims at; it is a framework to support assessment design, and a colourful handout plus "a hopeful expectation" secures nothing (Furze, September 2025).

That would be bleak if rules kept students honest. They never did. A follow-up study published in February, surveying 4,354 students at six US high schools about eighteen months after ChatGPT's release, found an overall cheating rate of 72.06%, which its authors call consistent with historical baselines; AI availability does not appear to have changed overall prevalence (Chen et al., February 2026). The tool changed the method, not the rate. Whatever sets the rate, whether engagement, pressure or a task that feels worth doing, sits upstream of any switch.

So the working answer: an AI rule rests on three layers, in falling order of reliability. First, what is structurally true of the task: done in the room, staged, defended aloud. Second, what the school can actually switch, which is coarse and covers products more than features. Third, what it tells students, which is invaluable for conversation and useless as security.

For a school running an AI use scale, the practical move is small. The scale stays as the shared language. But every task pitched at the no-AI or low-AI end needs a one-line answer to "what makes this true in the room?" If the honest answer is that the student promised, the task is open and should be marked as one.

Behind You! Behind You!

New York will treat unverified chatbot users as children. Governor Hochul signed S.9051B/A.10379C on 28 October. In the coming months, companies may not offer under-18s, or any user not confirmed as an adult, features such as posing as human, claiming a relationship, asking unprompted emotional questions or using a child's private details from earlier chats. The attorney general can seek up to $25,000 per violation (New York State, 28 October). The state's Business Council had argued that educational tools were swept in (TechTimes, 29 October). Watch whether vendors build that default once and ship it everywhere.

States want proof and can't get it. SETDA's 2026 State EdTech Trends report, published 29 October from 73 survey responses, found 79% of state leaders naming AI a top technology priority. Yet 52% said their state collects no data on the use or effectiveness of ed-tech products, and 68% said independently verifying vendor claims about AI products is challenging (GovTech, 29 October). If American states with procurement offices struggle, an international school is checking alone.

Half of the US schools and districts surveyed had a cyber incident last year. The Center for Internet Security's 2026 K-12 report puts it at 51% in the past 12 months, and 66% of districts leave cybersecurity to one or two people (CIS, 27 October). Phishing or email compromise ranked among the top incidents for 87% of leaders, and the report warns of more convincing AI-enabled phishing (K-12 Dive, 27 October). The weak point is staff inboxes, not servers.

The ed-tech backlash is on the ballot, and it isn't partisan. Ahead of Tuesday's US midterms, Chalkbeat found a Republican candidate for California governor promising to ban AI in schools on day one. In Texas, the Republican governor pledges less classroom screen time and his Democratic challenger opposes AI for elementary students. Seven of eight San Francisco school board candidates back limits on classroom technology (Chalkbeat, 26 October). International schools enrolling families from those systems should expect the mood to travel with them.

Sceptic's Corner

Next time someone says the research shows AI improves learning, ask which research.

Jill Barshay reports 39 meta-analyses on AI in education, every one finding substantial benefits. Patrick O'Neill, a community college professor, audited 14 of them and found serious conceptual and statistical flaws, from hallucinated references to underlying studies citing generative-AI papers published before the technology was public. One, published in a Nature journal in May 2025, has since been retracted (Hechinger Report, 26 October).

When O'Neill pooled 44 well-designed experiments, outcomes ranged from very helpful to very harmful, and his preliminary calculations put the average at roughly zero. Susanna Loeb's team gathered almost 900 studies and judged only 20 good enough for its evidence summary. O'Neill's audit is itself a preprint and his numbers are preliminary, so hold him to the same standard.

Two things follow. A literature that is uniformly positive is a warning, not a comfort; real interventions produce null results. An average of roughly zero is not a verdict of harmlessness. It is what you get when good designs and bad ones cancel out, which is why the question is always which use, for whom, checked how.

The line for a staff meeting: there is no effect size for "AI", only for particular uses of it. Anyone quoting one number is quoting a brochure.
