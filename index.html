Top story

The agent layer is moving into the school office suite. Governance has to move from app approval to action approval.

Google's I/O week did not stop at model announcements. The sharper school signal is that Gemini is being framed as a cross-app actor: Workspace updates name voice workflows in Gmail, Docs, and Keep, AI Inbox, Google Pics, and Gemini Spark, a 24/7 personal AI agent that can integrate with Workspace apps and act on a user's behalf under direction. Google says Spark will be available soon in preview for Workspace business customers, while the Gemini app version can gather urgent Gmail updates, track Calendar events, and suggest next steps. (Google Workspace, Google Gemini app)

The education relevance is indirect but not distant. Schools do not need Gemini Spark in Education tomorrow for the pattern to matter. Google has already made Workspace Intelligence available to Education Plus, Google AI Pro for Education, and Teaching and Learning add-on customers. That layer grounds generative AI tasks in Gmail, Chat, Calendar, Drive, Docs, Sheets, and Slides, is on by default where available, and gives admins data-source controls by domain, OU, or group. Crucially, Google's admin note says that disabling a data source stops active search across that source, but users can still ask Gemini about a specific file they provide. (Google Workspace Updates)

For international schools, this is the next useful control-plane question. The old approval unit was "Can teachers use this app?" The new unit is "What actions may AI take, against which data, inside which account boundary, with what review?" A Gmail summary is one thing. A Calendar-aware daily brief is another. A NotebookLM-grounded Workspace Studio flow is another again. A future agent that drafts, schedules, routes, or updates records is not just a fancier chatbot; it is a delegated workflow.

For Ed Tech Coaches and school technology leaders, this argues for a school AI control matrix before the August sparkle tour. Put rows around actions, not products: summarize, draft, classify, translate, generate feedback, query a source notebook, update a spreadsheet, send a message, create a calendar event, call an external tool. Then mark allowed data classes, approved platforms, age bands, review requirements, logging expectations, and "hard no" zones. Otherwise schools will keep debating "AI tools" while the tools quietly become office plumbing.

Other stories

  • The Canvas incident now has official, boring details, which is exactly what schools should read. Instructure's incident page says unauthorized activity was detected on 29 April, the same actor gained additional access on 7 May through a second Canvas vulnerability, the second attack was disabled after about 10 minutes, Free-For-Teacher accounts were the route in both instances, and that product has been temporarily shut down. (Instructure) The U.S. Department of Education says the incident involved usernames, email addresses, course names, enrollment information, and messages, and recommends MFA everywhere, disabling unused or legacy accounts, reviewing logs, and rotating Canvas integrations, LTI tools, SSO connectors, and API keys. (Federal Student Aid) This belongs in LMS governance conversations because platform risk is no longer abstract procurement theatre.

  • OpenAI's Edu notes keep turning admin minutiae into the main story. The latest ChatGPT Enterprise/Edu release notes include new data-residency support in Japan for all apps with sync, token-based rates for new Enterprise workspaces while Edu remains on legacy message-based rates until migration, updated Box/Notion/Linear/Dropbox app actions, and prior controls for SCIM group discoverability in sharing flows. (OpenAI Help Center) The pattern matters even for Google-first schools: connectors, shared groups, app actions, and residency are where real risk lives. The chatbot text box is now the least interesting part of the system.

  • Apple is setting up WWDC as the next AI privacy checkpoint. Apple says WWDC begins 8 June and will include AI advancements, developer tools, Apple Intelligence labs, machine learning sessions, and student-developer work. (Apple Newsroom) Its May privacy-preserving machine-learning workshop is the more useful pre-read: foundation-model privacy, attacks and security, memorization, trust models, and privacy accounting. (Apple Machine Learning Research) For Google/Apple schools, the interesting comparison is not "which assistant is cleverer?" It is whether Apple can make privacy-preserving AI feel usable enough that schools can point to a different operating model than cloud-first everything.

  • Education Week's current AI cluster is about teacher training quality, student well-being, and customised school tools. The pattern is healthier than last year's detector panic: training that moves beyond basics, AI's effect on student well-being, and districts using AI coding to build cheaper local tools. (Education Week) Useful, but only if schools resist turning every local prototype into a shadow platform.

  • A new arXiv discourse study is a decent warning against policy by anecdote. The paper analyses 270,000 AI-related Reddit posts and comments across education communities from November 2022 to April 2026, finding that discourse moved from detection/evasion into a sustained enforcement regime, with K-12 teachers foregrounding cognitive dependency, academics focusing on detection and deliberation, and professional students worrying about career anxiety. (arXiv) It is Reddit, not a clean survey. Still, it captures the staffroom weather: the loudest conversations are often the most adversarial ones.

Sceptic's corner

The phrase to put under the lamp today is "under your direction."

It is the comforting line in almost every agent announcement. The AI acts under your direction. It asks before high-stakes actions. It works with your files, your inbox, your calendar, your workflow. Lovely. Also incomplete.

In schools, "your" is rarely simple. A teacher's Drive can contain student work, shared department material, old drafts of policy, parent communications, assessment accommodations, and exported spreadsheet oddities that nobody remembers owning. A calendar event can imply medical, behavioural, pastoral, or family context. A source notebook can be personal thinking one week and a de facto knowledge base the next. If an agent acts "under your direction" across that material, the school still has to define what the user was allowed to direct in the first place.

Consent is not the same as authority. A tired staff member clicking approve is not a governance model. The useful test is whether the school can say: this role may perform this action on this data class in this system, with this record of what happened, and this human checkpoint before consequence.

If the answer is no, the problem is not that the agent is too powerful. The problem is that the school has delegated before it has named the delegation.

School AI governance now lives at the handoff: source notebooks, LMS models, spreadsheets, and prototypes becoming workflows.

Week ending Sunday, 24 May 2026 · 1,141 words · ~5 min read

The Week That Was

Google I/O did not produce an "education announcement" in the tidy old sense. It produced something more useful to watch: the agent layer moving into ordinary infrastructure. Google put Gemini 3.5 Flash into AI Studio, Antigravity, the Gemini API, and Android Studio; Workspace Studio gained NotebookLM as a source-grounded step; and Google's education lane kept pushing Gemini, NotebookLM, Moodle LTI, and educator training as the normal school toolkit. (Google I/O developer highlights, Google I/O recap, Workspace Updates, Google for Education)

The LMS and governance thread did not go away. Canvas remains the cautionary story; Toddle's own privacy language now names multiple model providers behind its AI layer; OpenAI kept tightening admin visibility around workspace agents, sheets, and connected apps. The shape is clear enough: schools are no longer choosing "an AI tool." They are choosing which systems get to act. (TechCrunch, Toddle privacy policy, OpenAI release notes, OpenAI workspace agents)

What Does It All Mean?

The useful synthesis this week is that school AI has split into three layers, and they are arriving at the same time.

Layer one is personal productivity: teacher drafts a lesson, admin summarises a spreadsheet, student asks for feedback. This is the layer most staff conversations still imagine when they say "AI." It is also the easiest layer to govern badly, because individual use feels private even when school data is involved.

Layer two is workflow authority. Workspace Studio can now ask NotebookLM inside an automation. ChatGPT workspace agents can run on schedules, use connected apps, and appear in admin consoles. AI Studio and Antigravity are turning prototypes into deployable applications more quickly. Once a workflow can fetch, summarise, decide, notify, and repeat, the question is no longer "did a teacher use AI?" It is "who authorised this routine, what source knowledge does it rely on, and what stops it when it is wrong?"

Layer three is curricular settlement. The IB's draft AI Design Principles, the OECD's Digital Education Outlook 2026, PISA 2029's Media and AI Literacy domain, and the EC-OECD AILit classroom exemplars all point away from tool demonstrations and toward judgement: evaluate outputs, preserve teacher agency, protect data, make ethics visible, and design tasks where AI changes the learning activity rather than merely speeding up submission. (IB AI Design Principles, OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026, PISA, AILit exemplars)

For Google-heavy international schools, the practical problem is that those layers will not arrive in the correct order. The Google layer is already there. Modern LMS platforms are bringing AI into curriculum planning, reporting, communications, behaviour documentation, and analytics. AI Studio prototypes will tempt capable teachers because they solve real classroom problems quickly. Meanwhile the policy layer will still be trying to write language that sounds stable enough for parents, teachers, and IT.

So the governance unit is not the app. It is the handoff. Student work handed to an LMS model. A teacher-authored unit handed to a curriculum assistant. A source notebook handed to a Workspace flow. A spreadsheet handed to ChatGPT. A prototype handed to Google Cloud. Each handoff needs an owner, a permitted data class, a logging expectation, an expiry rule, and a human judgement point that is more than decorative.

That is a better frame than "AI tools for teachers." It lets Ed Tech Coaches say yes to practical usefulness without letting every product define its own risk category.

Behind You! Behind You!

The IB published a useful school-level example on 22 May. DSB International School's piece is not grand theory; that is the point. It grounds AI in DP criteria, ATL skills, visible ethics, school-managed accounts, and teacher feedback. The line to borrow is structural: start with pedagogy, anchor in IB frameworks, teach judgement rather than dependency. (IB)

AILit is moving from framework to classroom exemplars. The May update says the final framework is due "in the coming weeks" and previews two multilingual exemplars: primary categorisation and secondary verification. That matters because fluency work needs a concrete activity layer, not just a standards map. (AILit Framework)

Toddle's privacy page is now more specific than its marketing page. The policy lists Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Whisper, and Gemini 2.5 Pro TTS for different Toddle AI functions, with an updateable third-party list. Good: more visibility. Still to ask: which modules are enabled, what the school contract overrides, what "pseudonymized" means in practice, and what logs/export controls admins get. (Toddle privacy policy, Toddle AI)

Google's policy-lab language is worth stealing, not worshipping. Their global AI Policy & Guidance Labs emphasise shared vocabulary, teacher lead, case studies, and 12-month implementation roadmaps. Vendor source, yes. Still a useful shape for turning "we need an AI policy" into working sessions. (Google for Education)

Sceptic's Corner

The phrase to be careful with after this week is "human in the loop." It sounds reassuring and often means almost nothing. A teacher glancing at an AI-generated report comment after three hours of fatigue is technically in the loop. An admin approving a Workspace automation they do not understand is in the loop. A student being told to "check Gemini's answer" without having the subject knowledge to check it is in the loop too. The loop can be a fig leaf.

The real test is stopping power. Can the human see the source, the prompt, the data class, the model route, the output history, and the consequence of accepting it? Can they refuse without breaking the workflow? Can they explain the judgement to a parent, student, colleague, or compliance officer? If not, the loop is theatre.

This is why the boring governance questions are now the interesting ones. Agentic AI makes school work faster by moving decisions into systems. Schools do not have to panic about that. But they do have to name which decisions are allowed to move.
