Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Tuesday, 24 November 2026

Top story

South Korea is bringing entrance-exam security into high-school finals.

Acting Education Minister Choi Eun-ok took a new plan for school assessments to Cabinet on 24 November, after the controversy over rules for last week's national university entrance exam. The ministry says smart glasses had already figured in cheating incidents in middle- and high-school assessments this year. High schools are first in line to apply the new measures during second-semester finals. Middle schools are to use the plan as a reference when tightening their own supervision. South Korean Ministry of Education, 24 November.

The plan calls for phones and other electronics to be collected, bags to be kept away from exam rooms and school Wi-Fi to be switched off. It envisages metal-detector checks for all students, with advance notice to students and parents and prior consent. Some seniors had already started their finals on Monday, so the ministry gave schools steps they could use immediately, including using detectors when staff suspect a violation or a student re-enters the room. The proposed academic penalty for possessing or using a prohibited smart device moves from zero in the affected subject to zero in every subject examined that day. The ministry also plans to recommend that schools strengthen their disciplinary rules, with expulsion as a possible sanction at high-school level. Education ministry summary on Korea's policy portal, 24 November.

That change gives school leaders two distinct decisions. Running a room fairly requires a way to handle a discrepancy at the door without delaying everyone else's paper. Imposing a penalty across several subjects requires a reliable record of what happened, a chance for the student to respond and someone with authority to decide on the evidence. At an international school, those decisions may sit with different people. The greater the consequences of the first report, the more it matters that the invigilator knows precisely what to record before the next session starts.

Other stories

  • Teachers delegated lesson drafts and kept the learning objectives. Muhammad Saleh Farooq, Muhammad Ahmad Khan and Muhammad Jon Raza interviewed 13 teachers, four administrators and one student in Punjab, Pakistan, and coded teachers' responses across 12 tasks. Nine teachers accepted a draft or substantial AI help for lesson planning. None accepted AI-drafted learning objectives, and those willing to use AI for marking wanted it to have the rubric or marking criteria. Three government-school teachers declined to use AI to mark quizzes even when they had an answer key. They thought students expected to see the teacher's effort. The preprint draws on reported practice and interview scenarios in a small, purposive sample, with no observation of how teachers checked actual outputs. Its practical insight is that the smallest unit for a school AI guideline may be a step inside the task. A teacher can ask for four practice examples, check each against the textbook and still own the decision about what students are learning and why. Farooq, Khan and Raza, arXiv preprint, 20 November.

  • UNESCO puts time for professional learning on the timetable. UNESCO and the International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030 launched their new Global Report on Teachers in Addis Ababa on 23 November. UNESCO says 45% of countries with complete data require continuing professional development across every level from pre-primary through secondary education. That figure describes requirements in policy. The report also describes unequal access, especially in rural, underserved and crisis-affected settings, and argues for learning tailored to career stage and context, including support for AI. Its recommendations cover mentoring, peer collaboration and working conditions that give teachers time to learn. Ed Tech Coaches can put that into practice by designing the follow-up as carefully as the workshop. Give teachers time to bring one assignment from their own subject, try the method, and come back with where it helped or failed. A school can count attendance on the first day and learn far more about classroom fit from the work teachers bring back. UNESCO launch statement, 23 November.

  • Google plans to bring Drive files and Gemini chats into AI Mode in Search. Its 23 November announcement starts the opt-in Wallet rollout first, with Drive and the Gemini app following in the coming weeks. Google's help pages describe the connected-content experience as a personal-account feature for adults and list Drive access in English in the US. The help text also says summaries and excerpts from connected files can be used to train generative models, and disconnecting an app leaves existing Search Services History for a user to manage separately. For a school, the relevant copy might be the one a teacher downloaded to edit at home and saved to a personal Drive. A data-handling rule that only names the school account leaves that copy unaddressed. The announcement is a reminder to describe what can travel with a file, and the terms that apply to whichever account has the copy. Google Search announcement, 23 November · Google Search Help.

On the radar

  • Singapore and China have agreed to test education AI together. Their education ministries signed a memorandum on 23 November. The National University of Singapore's AI Centre for Educational Technologies will work with the Shanghai Institute of AI for Education at East China Normal University. The agreement covers testing technology, exchanging AI-enabled teaching practices and developing ways to assess vocational and human skills. The first protocols and results are worth watching. A classroom test that can travel between two systems would have to say what counted as a successful lesson and how the researchers judged it in each curriculum. NUS AI Centre for Educational Technologies, 23 November.

  • Qatar has launched a curriculum-linked school AI project. The education and communications ministries and Qatar University launched two projects with Scale AI as technical partner on 17 November. The Smart Assistant is intended to tailor school support to a pupil's level and help teachers plan and monitor progress. The separate university project supports academic advising. The school project is worth following for how teachers can check what the assistant tells a pupil. A useful evaluation would show the source the tool used, how it handles material outside the expected unit and what happens when a teacher corrects an answer. Qatar Ministry of Education and Higher Education, 17 November launch · Qatar News Agency, 17 November.

Sceptic's corner

Giving a teacher an AI draft can save time. The attractive version of that claim assumes the review will be quick because the teacher already knows the subject. The Punjab interviews suggest what can disappear from that calculation. Teachers had to check outputs against the textbook, consider the class and sometimes show students what care had gone into the work. The study gives those costs names. It leaves the size of the time saving an open question. Farooq, Khan and Raza, 20 November.

Take an exit ticket that asks pupils to explain why two fractions are equivalent. Generating eight plausible questions is quick. The teacher still has to inspect whether a student can answer by spotting a pattern on the page, using a shortcut the class already knows, or giving the very explanation the lesson was meant to develop. All three can produce the same answer. The review gets easier if the tool shows the objective and expected reasoning beside each question, and a colleague can help examine an uncertain item. UNESCO's case for time to learn with peers has a practical application here. UNESCO, 23 November.

The fair comparison is two versions of the same task, each ready to hand to pupils. Count the minutes through checking and correction, then see which version elicits the reasoning the teacher meant to assess. That is the saving a department could defend when someone asks what improved.

The Week That Was

A week of small, conditional evidence. Wharton's Primer team watched simulated characters help students too early (arXiv, 12 November). Harvard's study of about 3.3 million students found that lockable phone pouches bought a small learning gain (Harvard CEPR, 17 November). Carnegie Mellon's tutor helped adults fix their own errors, with nearly twice the practice time bundled in (arXiv, 15 November). OpenAI's new models, released on 17 November, came with the admission that they were more likely to leave out obstacles that blocked their work (OpenAI). On Thursday the EU Council put curriculum and assessment on the same AI agenda (Council of the EU, 19 November).

The same Thursday, unreported in the weekday briefs, South Korea sat its university entrance exam under its tightest AI controls yet; it frames the centrepiece. Since Friday's brief: on Saturday 21 November, Fortune reported that three of America's four largest districts are pushing back on Google's Search terms. It leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

On Thursday South Korea ran what may be the most closely guarded exam in the world. For the College Scholastic Ability Test, 497,526 candidates sat at 1,312 sites with about 83,400 invigilators. Glasses were inspected, Wi-Fi within 100 metres was switched off, and the government sent three national emergency alerts during the day (Kyunghyang Shinmun, 19 November). The preliminary count of 98 covered the first four sessions. The ministry’s updated count after the fifth session was 111 electronic-device cases: 54 detections linked to the alerts, 52 other detections and five cases of device use (Ministry of Education, 20 November).

The week's most notorious case was not caught in an exam room at all. On 15 November, the top-ranked candidate in this year's tax accountant exam admitted to cheating with electronic devices and generative AI (News1 via Financial News, 17 November). Speculation about his near-perfect results came first; the confession followed (Al Jazeera, 17 November).

So the Sunday question: if guarding the room has limits, what should a school change instead?

Read the Korean numbers coldly. Most of what was caught was a device that went off or turned up. Detection counts the careless; whatever the careful managed is, by definition, missing from the count. England's chief regulator has reached the same place. In an 18 November letter, Sir Ian Bauckham said Ofqual would soon propose changes to coursework in A level history and the three English A levels, and told exam boards he expects exam-centre staff, invigilators included, to be trained to spot wearable misuse in time for the summer 2027 series. "But we cannot rely on invigilators alone," he wrote (Ofqual, 18 November).

Research has words for this. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu separate "discursive" changes, which tell students what is allowed, from "structural" ones, which change the task itself so it works whether or not students follow the instructions (Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 2025). Traffic-light rules for AI use are their example of the first kind. They borrow the look of a system with real enforcement and leave the enforcement behind. Korea chose the opposite extreme: maximum structure, applied to a single day.

One AP course already shows a middle path. In AP Computer Science Principles, students build a program of their own, and the supervised May exam asks written questions about that code (College Board). The project can be made anywhere; the explanation happens where nobody can prompt it.

Korea is edging the same way. Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin told the National Assembly the case was a chance to rethink the purpose and format of exams, and essay-type questions are under discussion, undecided (News1 via Financial News, 17 November).

For an international school, an AI-use scale is a discursive instrument, and a useful one: it tells students what each task allows. It cannot make that true. Its structural partner can be cheap. For any task set at the low-AI end of the scale, name the moment, in class and in front of a teacher, where the student explains, defends or extends what they handed in. The College Board does it once a year. A department can do it every unit.

Behind You! Behind You!

Three of America's four largest districts are pushing back on Google. New York City has switched off Gemini and AI Mode on student devices and is talking to Google; Los Angeles has asked for a way to turn the AI summaries off; Chicago says it will look at other search tools if it cannot suppress them (Fortune, 21 November). New Jersey's governor's office called the policy unfair to schools and students (NJ.com via Yahoo, 18 November). Miami-Dade, which has offered Gemini to about 100,000 high schoolers, did not answer questions on how it will adapt. Pushback so far, not departure.

Gemini skills can now be shared, and sharing starts switched on. In a gradual rollout from 12 November, on the web app only for now, Gemini users can share skills with View or Edit access and attach files from Drive, Gemini Notebook and Google Photos (Google, 16 November). Sharing is on by default and admins can turn it off. Skills follow the domain's Drive sharing settings, so external sharing applies to them too, and there is no admin control over attaching files. A well-built rubric skill is worth passing round; check the external setting first.

Gemini can now label sensitive Drive files, for schools on the right licence. On 17 November Google made Gemini-powered classification in Drive generally available. Admins describe a label in plain language, test it, then let Gemini apply it across Drive so labels can drive data-loss and retention rules; it will not overwrite labels people have set (Google, 17 November). In education it needs Google AI Pro for Education. For a school that must show where student records sit, losing the manual training step matters. A rule written in a sentence still needs testing on files whose contents you already know.

Sceptic's Corner

The centrepiece's remedy, change the task rather than guard the room, has a strong case against it. Korea is making it in public.

Redesign does not remove advantage; it moves it. When the National Education Commission's essay-type plans surfaced in August, one parent told Yonhap the family would simply add another academy, and a cram-school official expected demand for test technique to grow in the early years (Yonhap via Daum, 16 August). Grading fairness was the largest single concern in the commission's public comments: 754 of 3,246 (News1 via Financial News, 17 November). The ministry's plan for marking essays at scale is an AI that provisionally scores answers, trained on 1.1 million of them, in every regional education office by 2029 (Money Today, 5 August). The machine leaves the exam hall and returns as the marker.

Conversation-based checks have costs of their own. They reward the fluent and the confident, and a timed in-class write is harder on slow writers and anxious students.

Nor has the motive changed much. Stanford's Challenge Success surveys put the share of high schoolers reporting at least one cheating behaviour in the previous month at 60–70% for years, and the 2023 surveys, which added questions on tools like ChatGPT, found it about the same or slightly lower (Stanford GSE, 31 October 2023). The tool changes; the stakes drive it.

The line for the staffroom: redesigning the task is right, but it is neither free nor neutral. Before calling the new version fairer, check whom it favours.
