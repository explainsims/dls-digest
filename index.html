Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Monday, 16 November 2026

Top story

Wharton has opened up the design of an AI simulation.

In a 12 November preprint, Wharton Generative AI Labs describes Primer, an open-source prototype that interviews a teacher about the learning goal, prerequisite knowledge and likely misconceptions, then drafts a simulation plan for teacher review. The student experience has three parts. A mentor prepares the learner, a practice agent plays the counterpart, and a feedback agent helps the learner reflect. The paper describes roleplay and teachback, where the learner explains an idea to a simulated character. The design gives the teacher a place to specify what the learner has to work out and when the system should help.

The researchers tested the authoring workflow with K–12 teachers between February and June 2026. Teachers made a simulation and played through it as students would. The team observed transcription mistakes, drift from the learning goal and agents stepping in with help too early. That is usability and design evidence. How much students learn from the simulation, or can do independently afterwards, remains an open question. The team also says it has not reviewed the prototype for compliance with U.S. student privacy laws or district policies. It describes recorded sessions reviewed by AI agents, with privacy and costs depending on whether the models run locally or remotely. Those are decisions a school would need to own even with access to the source code.

For Ed Tech Coaches and science teachers in international schools, a narrow rehearsal from a familiar investigation is a useful first design test. The teacher could specify that the simulated classmate insists on comparing insulation tests that started at different temperatures. The learner has to spot why the comparison fails, propose a fair test, and defend that choice under a follow-up question. Have a colleague play the evasive student and see whether the system holds back the answer. The quality of the character's acting comes a distant second to whether the student still has to do the physics.

Other stories

  • Italy's privacy regulator puts some of the work back on the ministry. In its 12 November newsletter, the Garante disclosed a favourable opinion, issued on 20 October, on the education ministry's draft decree providing initial guidance on school AI use. A technical annex describes organisational and security measures for pseudonymising accounts and using the systems in schools. The regulator also asked the ministry to help schools choose from the market, suggesting supplier standards, model services and a dedicated support service that could help schools deal with providers. Those suggestions concern what the ministry could put in place. The opinion is on the draft decree. The most transferable part is who does the homework. A school can explain the purpose of a tool and identify the data it needs. It is a different undertaking to verify the vendor's handling of the same data across account provisioning, support and deletion. A common supplier standard would give school teams something concrete to check, and a support route would give them somewhere to go when the contract and the settings tell different stories.
  • Clever brings student-level browser activity into the buying conversation. In its 13 November roadmap, Clever distinguishes existing application login tracking from a November launch of per-student active browser tab duration and time-of-day analytics. The company puts Safari on iOS on its back-to-school 2027 timetable. The same post describes existing field-level controls over the student information system attributes shared with each app and discusses extending identity, access and visibility controls to AI tools as work in progress. The data-minimisation feature and the activity metric belong in the same procurement conversation. A school that wants to know whether it is paying for unused software might be able to answer from aggregate use. A screen showing every student's browser minutes is a different collection decision and can skew a comparison if one group spends more time on a device or browser outside coverage. Ask the vendor to explain which devices and routes are counted and who can see the individual record. That will tell a school more about the dashboard's value than another layer of usage charts.
  • A Thai Nguyen robotics hub gives AI literacy something to measure. The provincial government's 14 November account confirms the opening of the AI Research and Innovation Hub at Thai Nguyen University of Information and Communication Technology and its Physical AI Convention. The province says the centre will focus on Edge AI, which runs on or near the device, and Physical AI, which acts in the world, with universities, institutes and companies supporting student projects and real-world testing. The organiser's programme scheduled a guided small-team build for students and teachers on 15 November, starting from sensors and moving through perception, decision and action. For a school robotics programme, there is a curriculum question here. A cart that works on the taped course is only the first run. Change the lighting or the surface, ask teams to predict which readings will shift, and make them explain how the cart should behave when the input is uncertain. The technology becomes something a student can test and explain, and teachers can assess how the group makes sense of a failure.
  • Pause and replay deserve attention in accessible science video. Mamdouh Helali and Wafaa Ragab describe eight weeks of Edpuzzle-based work with 30 learners with hearing impairments, aged 14–16, at two schools in Egypt in a 14 November article in press. The programme used interactive video and embedded questions. The authors report better visual literacy scores after the programme. They tested the same group before and afterwards, with no comparison group and no assessment of long-term retention. The design cannot separate effects of the platform from those of adapted materials, repeated opportunities to practise or the teaching that came with the videos. A useful question for a science department is whether the student can stop at the moment a reading changes, see the scale clearly and explain what has happened before proceeding. It is worth comparing an accessible clip with and without embedded prompts using the same evidence question. The paper supplies a reason to inspect that part of the lesson. A school can do that with a clip it already uses.
  • A tutoring review asks what the comparison group actually did. Larisa Tararina and colleagues' 13 November systematic review synthesized 89 evaluations of systems that adapt instruction using performance data. Twenty-one used large language models. Seventy-three studies compared the intervention with conventional instruction, business as usual, print materials or no additional intervention. Thirteen used an alternative structured or technological intervention, and three did not give the authors enough to classify the comparator cleanly. Only 12 included a delayed measure. The review counted the direction and statistical certainty of findings without pooling effect sizes. It also excluded comparisons of two adaptive versions of the same system from the main synthesis because those comparisons address design features. That distinction matters to a department choosing between a tutor and practice it can already provide. The comparison that helps with that choice gives both groups a serious learning task, then tests what they can do later without either set of supports. An improvement over doing nothing can be helpful evidence. It answers the first and easiest question.

On the radar

  • 20 November is the deadline for a U.S. formative assessment infrastructure call. The K–12 AI Infrastructure Program's 7 October request for proposals closes at 11:59 p.m. Anywhere on Earth on 20 November. It offers up to US$1 million across up to two awards, with a US$600,000 cap on an individual award, for openly licensed infrastructure from U.S.-based applicants serving U.S. students. One example is connecting evidence from an AI tutoring session to an upcoming lesson so a teacher can respond to the misconceptions that surfaced. The milestone to watch is whether the resulting tools make that evidence easy for a teacher to interpret and act on.
  • An equity check can change the prediction without changing the classroom. An 11 November preprint looked at what happens when a learning model is forced to remove an average difference between groups. In a held-out replay of the Eedi maths data, for pupils with recorded financial-support eligibility, one version predicted a gap close to zero. Pupils eligible for support scored about 8.5 percentage points lower on the study’s rolling measure of correct answers. The Eedi analysis used recorded learning trajectories and could only test prediction. Measuring the effect of extra help would require intervention data. Watch for replications that can connect a change to the dashboard with a change to the help learners receive.

Sceptic's corner

Adaptive practice is often sold on the idea that a system will keep each learner at the right level of difficulty. The promise sounds sensible. It also lets the system decide how long a student should work on a problem before receiving a hint. The Primer team saw agents help too early in testing. In a lesson, that can happen in the friendliest possible exchange. The student asks whether they should compare temperatures, the character says yes and adds which readings to use, and the rest of the conversation flows. The student may leave with a neat explanation of an analysis the character effectively supplied.

A good design review makes the point where help becomes too much embarrassingly specific. Give the simulator a confident but wrong answer, then a hesitant but sound one. See which learner gets pressed for evidence and whether the character gives away the deciding observation. Keep a record of how much help each run needed. Then change the example and ask for an explanation away from the simulator, after enough time for the wording of the conversation to have faded. The two tasks tell a department whether the learner can use the idea in a new setting and what the system contributed along the way. I would ask to see both performances before paying for the claim that the system can find the right challenge.

Week ending Sunday, 15 November 2026 · 1,387 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

A week of rulebooks. South Korea confirmed four cases of cheating with AI glasses in school assessments (Ministry of Education, 9 November). Stanford's SCALE review grew from 20 causal papers to 52, and its verdict stayed conditional (SCALE, 10 November). England opened a consultation on safety certification for edtech and AI (DfE, 10 November). Brazil approved a framework that rules out AI grading of interpretive work and sanctions based only on a detector (Secom, 10 November). Google shipped skills, a "Wait until" step and standalone canvas links.

Since Friday's brief: NPR asked why Google won't let schools switch off AI in Search, and put a former Google education lead's antitrust theory on the record (NPR, 13 November). It frames the centrepiece. On Saturday, The 74 carried Chalkbeat's account of Wednesday's Chicago rally; it leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

Google's position on Search is now plain. AI Overview and AI Mode are "deeply integrated" into it, so a school cannot keep Search and switch them off (New York Times via The Star, 10 November). Senator Ed Markey's 9 November letter calls that "an ultimatum to effectively force schools to enable generative AI" (same report). So the Sunday question: if a school cannot remove the AI box from the results page, what is left for it to govern?

First, why the box won't come off. Tracy Pizzo, who spent more than a decade at Google, much of it on education, told NPR the answer may lie in court rather than in classrooms. In September, Judge Amit Mehta dismissed publishers' antitrust suits; Penske Media's tying claim failed because Search and AI Overviews were not plausibly separate products; reports quote the ruling's phrase "an integrated search experience" (MIXED, 4 October). A schools-only off switch, Pizzo argued, would be evidence that the two can be separated. The antitrust experts NPR asked were not entirely convinced, and Google did not answer (NPR, 13 November). Treat it as a theory. It does explain why "off by default" stops at the Search bar.

Second, what the box does to reading. Pew tracked the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025. When an AI summary appeared, they clicked a traditional result on 8% of visits, against 15% without one, and clicked a link inside the summary on 1% (Pew Research Center, 22 July 2025). Common Sense Media found that "AI Mode completed 100% of the homework assignments we gave it", and that neither feature can be switched off by parents or schools (Common Sense Media, July 2026).

Third, what schools have been trying to teach. The Stanford History Education Group found that professional fact-checkers beat historians and undergraduates at judging websites because they read laterally: they leave the page and check the source elsewhere. In a randomised trial across six high schools, six 50-minute lessons made students twice as likely to spot questionable sites, though they still earned only half the available points (Stanford GSE, 19 April 2022). Google.org funded the study.

Put those together. The habit information literacy builds is leaving the page. The interface that now greets every query is built so that most people don't.

For an international school on Google, that moves the question from the admin console to the task. Whatever a school decides about Gemini for students, every student who searches will meet an Overview. A school's AI-use scale tells students what they may use; it cannot treat Search as an AI-free zone. A research task can ask for the move the Overview skips: name two sources outside it, say what each adds, and mark where the summary was wrong or thin. A ninth-grader who can do that has a skill the box cannot take away.

Behind You! Behind You!

Chicago took the argument to Google's door. About 30 people, mostly parents and educators, rallied outside Google's West Loop office on Wednesday, alongside a protest in Cambridge, Massachusetts (Chalkbeat via The 74, 14 November). Chicago Public Schools, which has blocked student access to generative AI tools, said it is "actively exploring all options to independently suppress" the Overview, and failing that will look at other search tools. Watch whether a district that size actually leaves.

Anthropic signed the teachers' unions' privacy standard. The AFT and its New York affiliate, the UFT, announced on 10 November a legally binding agreement covering Claude for Teachers and Anthropic's existing district agreements (K-12 Dive, 10 November). Data may be used only for authorised educational purposes, and schools control how long it is kept and when it is deleted. Microsoft's equivalent took effect on 1 November. A school outside the unions' reach can still ask any vendor to match those terms in writing.

Ho Chi Minh City began planning its AI periods. The city's Department of Education and Training held a workshop on 12 November at Le Hong Phong High School for the Gifted on bringing the national AI education framework into secondary schools this year: 12 periods per class per school year, as topics or woven into subjects (SGGP, 12 November). The bottlenecks named included teacher capacity, funding, student data safety and formalistic assessment. Vietnam's largest city is working the same problems as the international schools around it, on a national timetable.

Gemini in Gmail reached every adult Workspace account, on by default. From 10 November, Google is extending thread summaries and non-personalised Help me write to "all Workspace users 18 years and older" (Google, 10 November). The features are on by default; users can disable them and admins can switch them off. The post does not name Education editions. For a school, that means every staff inbox and any student account set as adult. The decision is better made before the habit forms.

  • Overdue: Vault API support for Gemini exports, due within 14 days of the 22 October rollout; still no update note (Google, 22 October).
  • Slow burn, missed last Sunday: Under a plan reported after Australia's education ministers met, primary students would be blocked from generative AI in classrooms and secondary students restricted from using it in assessments; the ministers agreed a Digital Detox Taskforce to advise government by mid-2027 (CathNews, 5 November). No start date yet. The assessment half is the one to follow.

Sceptic's Corner

The protest slogan wants Search without the AI. The strongest case against it is that the old results page was never the gateway campaigners remember.

In clickstream data that mostly predates AI Overviews, SparkToro and Datos found that "58.5% of American Google searches resulted in zero clicks", and that for every 1,000 US searches just 360 clicks reached a site Google doesn't own (SparkToro, 2 July 2024). Answer boxes, panels and the habit of stopping at the top all came before the chatbot. The Overview made an old pattern fluent.

That doesn't make the Overview harmless. A box that writes the homework on request is a fair thing to want gone for younger students, and Common Sense's evidence gives campaigners a real case. But switching it off would not restore a habit of checking sources that most users never had. Worse, the fight over the switch lets everyone treat the problem as solved once the switch is found.

The Stanford trial is the better guide. Six lessons doubled students' ability to spot a dubious site and still left them on half marks. That is the work, with or without the box.

The line for the staffroom: the AI box isn't the first time Google answered for students; it's the first time it answered in paragraphs. Teach them to leave the page.
