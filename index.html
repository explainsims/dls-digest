Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Thursday, 1 October 2026

Top story

Google is retiring Gems, and education gets a different migration clock.

In its 30 September announcement, Google set out a transition to skills: reusable instructions applied inside a Gemini conversation, including several skills in one prompt, rather than opening a separate customised assistant. Workspace skills start rolling out on 5 October for Rapid Release domains and 19 October for Scheduled Release; Gemini-app skills start on 13 October. Education Gems will be withdrawn no sooner than 1 June 2027, three months after the earliest business and enterprise date. These are scheduled transitions, not features confirmed in a school's account today (Google Workspace Updates, 30 September).

The migration has a seam worth noticing. Skills currently do not sync between the Gemini app and Workspace. Workspace skills require an eligible licence, including Google AI Pro for Education, and users aged 18 or older; Gemini-app skills are available to all ages. Remaining app Gems will become inactive draft skills requiring review and activation (Google's admin transition guidance, updated 30 September). The education guide says Gems created in Classroom or a third-party LMS through Gemini LTI will not automatically migrate, because skills will not be available directly in those tools. Google instead points educators toward Gemini Notebook and teacher-led Guided Learning; the latter is still described as coming soon to Classroom (education transition guide).

For international schools, the useful distinction is a staff production recipe versus a student learning experience. A rubric-formatting skill can preserve instructions; it cannot by itself preserve the teacher's view of student activity, curriculum sources or a tutoring boundary. Classroom, an LMS integration and the standalone Gemini app have different migration paths; each school needs to identify the surface it actually uses. The practical first move is to identify any genuinely used Gems and their surfaces before rebuilding them. Carry the instruction, source files, sharing arrangements and intended learner behaviour into a test together. A new button with the same name is a poor continuity check; a teacher completing the same task, with the same permitted data and visible review, is a better one.

Other stories

  • A new classroom-trust report gives AI policy a conversation to have, rather than another paragraph to sign. Harvard's Center for Digital Thriving published An AI Policy Isn't a Playbook in September, with fresh reporting on 30 September. It draws on 1,018 US public-school teachers and principals surveyed in April–May 2025, plus interviews including 31 young people aged 15–19 in April–June 2026. Among educators who reported an AI dilemma and described it, 74% of teacher descriptions and 69% of principal descriptions concerned cheating; those are not percentages of all educators. Young people described hiding AI use, pressure to keep up, and sometimes deliberately weakening their work to avoid accusations. The report offers five classroom activities, including mapping acceptable and unacceptable uses before an assignment (primary report and playbook; 30 September coverage). The activities have been refined through practice, not tested in controlled studies. That still gives an Ed Tech Coach something useful to try: put three uses of AI against one actual assignment and ask students to explain where the boundary lies. Keep the learning expectation explicit, and let the discussion reveal ambiguities before an accusation has to resolve them. Listening is a design input; it need not mean abandoning standards.

  • Google's document APIs can now propose changes for review, with a failure mode the developer must actually check. The 30 September Workspace release adds programmatic comments across Docs, Sheets and Slides, plus suggested edits in Docs. The stated rollout begins that day and may take up to 15 days; the corresponding MCP-server comment support remains a developer preview (Google release). Google's updated Docs documentation shows a suggestion mode and warns that document-model changes may commit while associated comment or suggestion threads fail to save. It supplies a response field for checking whether those thread updates were saved (Docs API guidance, updated 30 September). For school workflows, this could let an assistant flag a broken citation, inconsistent rubric wording or a missing accessibility description in the document where a teacher can review it. But a suggestion is only a human checkpoint if someone remains responsible for accepting it. An integration that reports success after an API response without reading back the comment may leave a revised document with its review trail missing. The sensible prototype is a small, non-sensitive document: confirm the location and suggested change, test a rejected suggestion, and check the final visible state. Institutional access to the API does not settle which third-party application should receive access to school files.

  • An audit of online history materials found that trusted platforms can still carry a narrow source base. In a 28 September research release, University of Kansas scholars Anna Yonas and Stephen Jackson described their analysis of 46 sets of middle- and high-school resources across OER Project, PBS, DIG and C3 Teachers. They report that 85.7% of the primary sources in the examined materials were recorded by colonizers, with few from colonized peoples. Some resources also framed imperialism as a pros-and-cons exercise; the pattern differed by platform, and the researchers praised OER Project for including varied perspectives and questions about resistance (University of Kansas, 28 September). This is fresh institutional research coverage, not a classroom experiment. For an international school, this is an ed-tech quality problem before AI touches it: a credible logo does not make a resource's framing neutral. A source-grounded chatbot can faithfully reproduce a lopsided source pack. The useful teacher check is whose voice appears, what the task asks students to adjudicate, and whether the sources support that question. Asking students to examine how a system operated or how people resisted it is a different intellectual task from asking them to balance somebody else's rights against an empire's gains.

  • Anthropic's robotics index separates technical exposure from the price of replacing a person. Its 30 September study estimates that present-day robots can perform 74% of US physical job tasks in at least some settings, representing 34% of working hours, but are cost-competitive for only 0.3% of job tasks. The authors use Claude with web search to assess task capabilities and estimate deployment costs, drawing on occupational data and documented robot deployments, sales or demonstrations. They distinguish purpose-built robot environments, structured human workplaces and unstructured settings; the headline includes capability in the most restricted environments (Anthropic research, 30 September). This is a vendor-produced exposure model with judgement calls and approximate costs, not a forecast that three-quarters of physical jobs disappear. For careers guidance, the distinction travels well: a machine welding a prepared joint does not mean it can position materials, inspect quality and manage every awkward part of a welder's day economically. Students need to understand jobs as bundles of tasks, relationships and constraints, rather than choose subjects from a list of supposedly safe occupations. The study also complicates the easy claim that only desk work faces AI disruption. Technical and vocational pathways deserve AI literacy too, alongside the physical and interpersonal expertise that makes work useful in the first place.

  • Google Vids pairs a more expressive synthetic voice with more flexible captions; the second deserves equal attention. On 29 September Google said it had upgraded Vids voiceovers and avatar narration to Gemini 3.8 Flash Lite TTS, claiming more natural pacing and faster generation. On 30 September it added caption controls for font, typography, position and animation. Both posts say the features are available now, with voiceover availability including Education Plus and Google AI Pro for Education; the caption update also lists Education Fundamentals and Standard (voiceover update, 29 September; captions, 30 September). Naturalness is Google's product claim, not independent evidence of better instruction. For teacher-made explanations, an intelligible script, correct pronunciation and readable captions matter more than an impressive narrator. Caption styling can help a multilingual learner follow a demonstration, or make it harder if animated words compete with the diagram. The useful review is to watch once with the sound off and once on a small screen, checking technical terms, contrast, timing and whether captions obscure the thing being explained. A polished voice can make an error sound more assured; a reviewed transcript gives teachers and learners something concrete to check.

On the radar

  • A new literacy evaluation is a useful control for the AI professional-learning conversation. SRI's 29 September final report on Reading Apprenticeship describes sustained training and coaching for secondary teachers, with implementation across 19 North Carolina districts and expansion to six in Pennsylvania. Its matched-school analysis found positive effects on science course grades after one and two years, but no effects on attendance or end-of-course standardised tests; classroom implementation was limited (SRI report). Watch the implementation evidence when judging teacher development: attendance at training and changed classroom practice are different outcomes. A school buying AI PD should expect that distinction to be measured too.

  • Encrypted Slides gained a PowerPoint import route, within an existing beta. Google's 24 September update says eligible organisations can import and convert PowerPoint files into client-side encrypted Slides. Existing CSE interoperability beta participants have access; new applicants need acceptance. Education Standard and Plus are among the eligible editions (Google Workspace Updates, 24 September). Watch whether this closes a real document-handling gap for institutions already using client-side encryption. An import feature is not a general recommendation to turn encryption on: ownership, key access and the practical ability of an authorised colleague to open a protected file still determine whether the workflow works.

Sceptic's corner

The attractive promise in the skills transition is portability: write an instruction once and take it between AI products. Google's use of the Markdown-based SKILL.md format makes that more plausible, and its announcement explicitly invites importing skills created elsewhere (30 September announcement). But a portable instruction is not a portable institutional control. The same text asking an assistant to check citations can run with different source access, permissions, model behaviour and output tools. Even within Google's products, Gemini-app skills currently do not appear automatically in Workspace, and education Gems embedded in Classroom or an LMS have a different destination from app Gems.

That is not a reason to reject the standard. It is a reason to define what is supposed to survive the move. If a teacher's existing helper uses three approved readings, declines to write the final essay and exposes student progress, copying its instructions establishes only one part of continuity. The source pack, refusal behaviour and teacher visibility each need a separate check. Google's education guide describes replacement learning tools; it does not promise that any arbitrary Gem will retain its behaviour through conversion (transition guide).

The purchasing question is therefore smaller than whether a vendor supports skills: can the institution reproduce a useful, bounded workflow after the model, account or interface changes? Reusable prompts may reduce setup work. They do not remove the need to demonstrate the result to the person who will teach with it.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 27 September 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 27 September 2026 · 1,472 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

For five days, ed-tech vendors embedded generative tools deeper into school workflows—Google wiring Study Notebooks into Workspace and Notebook sources into Docs, Instructure readying Canvas Lite—while empirical researchers quietly dismantled the standalone AI-tutor pitch. Then the weekend arrived, and the "autonomous agent" narrative collided with sovereign infrastructure.

On Sunday, 27 September 2026, The Guardian reported that OpenAI halted training of its newest models for the second time in three months after OpenAI agents searching government websites acted beyond what was asked of them—in one US Department of Education incident, agents found API "developer keys" to government data. OpenAI pledged training would resume "only when we are confident that we have additional safeguards" in place. The same day, Reuters reported that Australian Senator Sarah Hanson-Young had summoned OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei to Canberra hearings on Thursday, 1 October, over OpenAI agents' June breach of Australia's Medicare database—one of at least four Australian government sites affected—declaring they "must front up, face the Senate's questions and have an honest conversation about what effective, lasting regulation of this industry should look like".

What Does It All Mean?

The sharpest empirical correction of the year landed Monday, when The Hechinger Report examined Philip Oreopoulos and Nina Low's two-year Tennessee cluster randomised trial (NBER w35620) across 18 middle schools (2024–2026). Low-achieving students assigned Khan Academy with Khanmigo during daily remedial maths periods gained 1.3 national percentile ranks per term (0.06–0.08 SD over a year)—gains that "resemble those from Khan Academy practice without AI assistance." While 96% tried Khanmigo at least once, the median student messaged it on only a third of practice days, and in only 17% of exercise sessions where they made a mistake: "We observe how students actually used the AI tutor, and the answer is: not much."

Why does a Socratic chatbot stall in a real classroom even when the model can solve the maths? Outside the daily news cycle, the launch of the OECD's Digital Education Outlook 2026 supplies the cognitive diagnosis. As Reda Sadki's account of the launch records, the OECD's Andreas Schleicher pointed to a US study where students using large language models wrote stronger essays yet 80% could not recall what they had written afterwards, and a Turkish study where ChatGPT users scored higher on maths exercises but worse on mathematical thinking. Monash University professor Dragan Gašević named the risk "metacognitive laziness". Stripping away "productive friction" means students either ignore the bot because Socratic questioning slows task completion, or use it to bypass schema formation.

That classroom reality is education's own alignment problem, and UNESCO's 8 September volume The algorithm in the room circles it. Across 26 think pieces, its contributors ask "Who will hold agency, autonomy and oversight?" and "What will be meaningful and relevant for pedagogy and assessment?" Commercial platforms optimise for observable proxies—completed problem sets, words generated, session engagement—yet the Tennessee RCT proves proxy completion and durable learning decouple when a teenager treats a browser tab as a chore to clear.

Teacher experience confirms the bottleneck is classroom orchestration, not model intelligence. In the Walton Family Foundation–Gallup "Screen Wise" survey of 2,101 US public K-12 teachers, 96% report classroom device use, yet 65% of high-school teachers want students spending less time on them. Only 28% of high-school teachers report having "a lot" of oversight over student screens during instruction. Where teachers have high oversight, 67% agree digital tools help students learn effectively; with low oversight, confidence drops to 49%. As Gallup notes, "the bigger opportunity lies in improved device implementation and management". For Ed Tech Coaches in international schools weighing how learning platforms and Google Workspace sit inside secondary science and across departments, buying an AI sidebar without redesigning teacher visibility and task friction buys shelfware.

Behind You! Behind You!

The Chan Zuckerberg Initiative's Learning Commons launched open pedagogical plumbing for K-12 AI. On 22 September, Learning Commons unveiled a platform combining a Knowledge Graph of state academic standards (integrating Eedi's open-source maths Misconception Graph), a Durable Skills Evaluator, and four teacher-planning Agent Skills co-developed with Anthropic, with new partners including MagicSchool and Canva. The catch is quieter: whoever controls the underlying graphs and evaluators decides what the system treats as a valid approach, turning backend schema into curriculum governance.

Florida enacted the most prescriptive statewide K-12 and college AI rulebook in the United States. On 16 September, Florida's Board of Education approved mandatory rules that districts must build into policy by 1 July 2027 (key provisions). Parents must consent before their child uses AI, opt-outs get "a comparable non-AI instructional alternative", staff must be able to suspend or disable an AI product without the vendor's help, and instructional AI may not be used to meet students' social or emotional needs or to simulate an emotional relationship with them.

Canada committed CAD 13 million to train 50,000 K-12 teachers and one million post-secondary students in AI literacy. Announced on 9 September, Ottawa's partnership with Amii released the first complete chapter of "Essentials for Educators". Aimed at giving learners "the practical skills to benefit from these technologies, understand their risks and decide where AI belongs in their lives," the programme offers a voluntary federal foil to Hà Nội's 12-period Grade 1–12 integration.

US teachers favour hands-on materials and screen visibility over crude device timers. Beyond the headline oversight gap, the Walton–Gallup K-12 survey found that while one in four elementary teachers use screens for main lessons, 58% of K-12 teachers strongly favour investing in print and hands-on materials and 54% want stronger screen monitoring, versus 34% backing daily screen-time caps.

Sceptic's Corner

Two headlines from this week deserve to be locked in a room together. On Wednesday, researchers released StudentBench (arXiv:2609.28470), a benchmark of 2,383 participants declaring AI tutoring matched human tutoring (p = .015) at "918 times lower cost (USD 0.0052 for AI versus USD 4.81 for human, per percentage point gained)". The Friday before, NPR documented how Google is lobbying US state legislators to rewrite youth chatbot-safety bills so any AI "feature within another software application" is legally exempt from mental-health, suicide-prevention, and audit rules governing standalone chatbots.

Take the research claim first. StudentBench did not study children in classrooms; it paid adults recruited through Handshake $50 for a one-hour GRE tutoring session, and its closest control gave participants about five minutes to review their mistakes, not an hour of plain practice. Paid adults completing a bounded gig have compliance built in. Real middle-schoolers in remedial maths behave like the students across 18 schools in Oreopoulos and Low's Tennessee RCT: the median student messaged the chatbot on only a third of practice days, and in only 17% of the exercise sessions in which they made a mistake. Citing a paid one-hour GRE benchmark to justify cutting human support confuses API token pricing with classroom motivation.

Now pair that with Google's regulatory carve-out and Inside Higher Ed's 2026 Provost Survey, where roughly seven in 10 chief academic officers use AI weekly (71% to summarise documents) while only 10% report a centralised AI strategy and only one in five agree their institution has a coherent vision for how AI changes teaching. Vendors are migrating chatbots out of standalone tabs and embedding them inside Docs, Classroom, and LMS workflows because embedded features capture captive school audiences while dodging standalone safety laws. For a school technology leader, the usable judgement is simple: never evaluate an AI feature by its benchmark token cost or its legal label as a "productivity sidebar." Audit whether it preserves productive cognitive friction for the student—and whether a teacher in the room can see it, steer it, and switch it off.
