Thursday, 29 October 2026

Top story

Gemini's new visual lessons arrive without a separate school switch.

Google's 28 October release introduces immersive explorations: scenes with interactive nodes and guided explanations, covering subjects including the Silk Road, jet engines and lightning. The library is available now on web and mobile; access from inside a Gemini conversation and audio narration are still forthcoming. Google lists Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus for the student hub, and all Workspace customers and personal accounts for the direct Gemini library. Crucially, it says there is no feature-specific admin control or end-user setting; administrators are referred to the broader Gemini app control (Google Workspace Updates, 28 October).

That is a useful teaching development and a slightly awkward governance one. A visual explanation can make scale, structure and relationships easier to discuss than another paragraph of chatbot prose. A physics teacher could ask pupils to predict the relevant mechanism before opening a layer, then explain which detail changed their model. A humanities teacher could interrogate what a trade-route scene foregrounds and what it leaves out. The intellectual work comes from those questions; tapping all the nodes is an interface achievement.

The control boundary matters because school approval of an application now includes changes to the learning experiences inside it. There need not be a new app to install or a new procurement conversation before something materially different reaches a class. Ed Tech Coaches therefore need a reasonably current account of what an approved environment can do, alongside the familiar account of who may use it.

Language is another classroom detail hiding in the release: six languages are supported, including English, Indonesian and simplified Chinese; other languages currently receive English. Vietnamese is absent from that supported list. In international schools, a visually accessible resource can still impose a reading-language barrier. The interesting question is whether pupils can explain a relationship afterwards, including without the scene in front of them. Attractive navigation has never been an assessment criterion worth much.

Other stories

  • Frontline's breach has moved from containment to individual notification. The school administration supplier began notifying affected people on or about 26 October about an August incident involving Frontline Central and School Health Management. Its notice says a third-party software vulnerability allowed unauthorized access beginning 14 August, with the last activity on 15 August. Depending on the individual, affected records included Social Security numbers, birth dates, driving-licence and passport numbers, other government IDs, Medicaid IDs and student IDs. Frontline says the incident is remediated and offers eligible US adults two years of credit monitoring, with cyber monitoring for minors (Frontline Education, 26 October). The new event is the notification, rather than a new attack. The notice does not establish the total number affected or identify the vulnerable product. For schools elsewhere, the transferable issue is what happens after the technical repair: a person's identity data remains exposed even when a server is secure again. Procurement discussions tend to dwell on prevention and uptime; this is the case for giving notification ownership, usable contact records and post-incident support equal space. A vendor can finish its remediation while a family's consequences are only beginning. Those are different clocks, and a reassuring status update usually shows only one.

  • Minnesota supplies a more useful unit of AI policy: the learning process. In a dated 22 October university Q&A, learning scientist Panayiota Kendeou explains a new expert-consensus report's distinction between initial thinking and subsequent practice: learners should first do the thinking themselves, then use AI for feedback, examples and checking understanding. Younger children require particular care because foundational reading, writing and self-management still need practice (University of Minnesota, 22 October). The underlying October report maps 65 processes, reaching consensus on 42, and explicitly warns that the empirical literature lacks adequate rigor (expert-consensus report, October). This is expert judgment organized transparently, rather than a trial demonstrating one intervention's effect. Its practical contribution is a better question than whether AI is allowed for an assignment: which part of the thinking is this particular use replacing? Generating another practice example after an independent attempt and generating the first explanation of an unfamiliar problem have different instructional purposes, even inside the same approved tool. A whole-school policy can acknowledge that difference without creating an encyclopedia of applications. The report earns attention for its granularity; treating its judgments as settled causal evidence would undo that advantage.

  • Anthropic's drug-development results are an evidence lesson before they are a careers slogan. Its 27 October account describes partnerships with six organizations and work across molecule design, manufacturing documentation and clinical-trial planning. At BigHat Biosciences, scientists and an Anthropic researcher used Claude while the laboratory made and tested about 1,010 antibody-fragment variants over roughly ten weeks. Partway through, concern about the existing stability and clumping measures prompted reanalysis and a revised protocol, which scientists refined and ran. Anthropic emphasizes that the wider work is mostly preclinical (Anthropic, 27 October). That combination is more educationally useful than a headline about AI discovering cures: a large search space, a physical test, a measurement problem and a human decision about whether the test was meaningful. Biology and chemistry classes can follow the boundary between a plausible design, a laboratory result and an effective treatment in people. Those are three different claims requiring different evidence. There is also a sound careers angle: scientific work includes coordinating experiments and understanding data systems, as well as disciplinary knowledge. This is the vendor's account of collaborative projects, with an obvious interest in demonstrating progress. It is credible material for examining a research workflow; it is not independent proof that its model reliably delivers medicines.

  • Google's latest AI-literacy quest puts the unglamorous work on screen. Polar Peaks, launched on 26 October with the Stanford Accelerator for Learning, is the third AI Quest for ages 11–14. Pupils investigate a character's memory loss by scanning and mapping neurons, including data-cleaning and model-testing tasks. Google reports more than 400,000 quest completions across the series and localization into ten languages; those are completions, not a demonstrated count of learners or learning gains. Teacher modules for the first two quests are available, while the third quest's training module is due next month (Google, 26 October). The worthwhile distinction is between learning to prompt a chatbot and learning why an AI system can go wrong. Cleaning inputs and testing a model make the second kind of literacy concrete. For middle-school science or digital-literacy teaching, the neuroscience setting may provide a reason to care about the data rather than treating responsible use as a detached list of rules. The debrief is where the lesson earns its place: can a pupil identify a bad input, explain a mistaken output and say what a human should check? A polished quest provides the occasion for that conversation. Completion totals do not answer those questions on its behalf.

On the radar

  • An AI crawler's name is becoming a disguise. Cequence's 28 October threat report tracks one actor impersonating about 32 bots associated with providers including OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity, probing roughly 90 paths for configuration files, keys and other secrets. Nearly all requests were blocked or failed in its observed environment (Cequence, 28 October). This is vendor telemetry, not a school breach census. The portable lesson for school web services is nevertheless plain: a request claiming to be a familiar assistant has not thereby proved its identity. The interesting governance boundary is verified identity and permitted behavior, rather than whether an agent's brand sounds respectable.

  • ServiceNow's AI announcement spans today and next year. The 28 October OpenAI partnership expansion says its MCP plugin is available now, with enhanced capabilities expected by year-end. Voice is expected in the first quarter of 2027, security early access in January and general availability in the second quarter; the Codex workflow integration is expected in 2027 (OpenAI, 28 October). The design pattern is relevant beyond large enterprises: conversation becomes a place to update operational records, with existing business rules and permissions applied underneath. For schools evaluating workflow assistants, a permission model and a release calendar belong beside the demonstration. Announced integrations have several different tenses.

Sceptic's corner

The tempting inference from today's Google release is that a visual, interactive AI experience must be more educational than a chatbot answer. That is a hypothesis about learning dressed as a description of an interface.

Google's announcement establishes what users can open, how the scenes unfold and where the controls sit. It supplies no classroom comparison or retention result (Google Workspace Updates, 28 October). That omission does not make the feature useless. It means the announcement answers a product question, while the teacher still has a learning question to answer.

Consider two uses of the same lightning scene. In one, pupils browse until the picture feels familiar. In the other, they predict what would change under a different condition, inspect the explanation, and then draw and defend their own account with the screen closed. The second use creates something observable to assess. Its advantage is an instructional argument, not a result demonstrated by this release.

The counterweight to overclaiming should not become an impossible demand that every diagram arrive with its own randomized trial. Teachers reasonably use resources whose value depends on how they are taught. The usable judgment is narrower: give a new resource a defined job, preserve an independent student response, and compare what that response shows against an existing way of teaching the idea. A scene that provokes better explanations has earned attention. A scene that merely provokes longer browsing has earned a usage statistic.

The Week That Was

Google shipped at its usual clip, most usefully insight summaries that give teachers "greater visibility into how students interact with assigned notebooks" (Google, 19 October). The research ran the other way: at the University of Maryland, offering undergraduates a course-integrated AI tutor lowered their grades (IZA, October 2026). That gap is this week's question.

Since Friday's brief: Texas sued OpenAI and Sam Altman in Collin County on Friday 23 October. It wants an injunction requiring OpenAI to block minors from its product and to get any new model approved by a court-appointed evaluator (Texas Tribune, 23 October). Among the harms Paxton's press release lists: "users have lost critical thinking skills". The Tribune notes that Elon Musk, an OpenAI rival, is the largest single donor to Paxton's Senate campaign. Google also made Gemini Notebook a connected app inside the Gemini app, on by default outside the European Economic Area wherever both are enabled (Google, 23 October).

Saturday 24 October: Fortune ran a commentary arguing that every child will get an AI tutor but only families who can pay will keep human teachers (Fortune, 24 October). Its author founded school and IT-education networks, which is worth knowing. Sunday was quiet.

What Does It All Mean?

Last week's answer was that manner matters more than dose. Maryland raises the next question: who sets the manner? Is an AI tutor something a school switches on, or something a teacher designs?

Read past the headline, a fall of up to 0.37 standard deviations in final grades, and the Maryland paper half-answers it (IZA). The tutor had a mode that guides students toward an answer instead of supplying one. The default was direct instruction, and only two instructors ended the term with tutor mode on. About 15% of students offered the tool used it even once. Of their requests, 73.8% sought information, explanations or solutions; 0.7% sought feedback on their own work. The authors allow that the tool's arrival may have shifted perceived permission to use AI generally, so the result may measure a signal as much as a product.

The trials where AI tutoring worked look different. At Harvard, Greg Kestin and colleagues built their physics tutor's prompts around active engagement, cognitive load and growth mindset, and walked students through each problem in sequence. Students learned more, in less time, than in an active-learning class (Scientific Reports, June 2025). A six-week after-school programme in Nigeria produced gains of about 0.3 standard deviations; the World Bank team's verdict is that the tool works as a tutor when implemented "with teacher support" (World Bank, January 2025). Stanford's Tutor CoPilot put the AI behind the human: students whose tutors had it were 4 percentage points more likely to master topics, and 9 points with lower-rated tutors (Wang and colleagues, arXiv).

The cleanest high-school test isolates the variable. Hamsa Bastani's team gave nearly 1,000 maths students in Turkey either a plain ChatGPT-style interface or a tutor that "includes teacher input and is designed to guide students with hints rather than directly giving answers" (Wharton, August 2024). On the unaided exam, the plain group scored 17% below students who never had AI. The tutor group avoided that loss (PNAS, July 2025).

So the working answer: AI tutoring helps when someone has designed what it does, when, and what follows. Made merely available, it becomes an answer machine, and the defaults decide the rest.

That matters for schools running Gemini, because Google's defaults are generous. The Gemini student hub, with study notebooks, flashcards and quizzes, reaches Education users of all ages, governed only by the admin console's generative AI settings (Google, 1 October). That is Maryland's condition at scale. A teacher-assigned Gemini Notebook is nearer Harvard's. The number worth tracking this year is not accounts provisioned but tasks designed, each followed by a short unaided check.

Behind You! Behind You!

Only one US state requires AI tools to be checked for their effect on learning. CRPE's annual State of the American Student report counts 36 states with published or endorsed AI guidance, most of it advisory (CRPE). The only one requiring AI tools to be vetted for academic outcomes is Maryland (The 74, 21 October), home of the university behind this week's sobering tutor trial. International schools have no state doing even that; for them, the only vetting layer is the school itself.

Anthropic handed the Agent Skills standard to a neutral foundation. On 22 October it donated the format to the Agentic AI Foundation, the Linux Foundation body it co-founded with Block and OpenAI. Anthropic says more than 70 agent platforms, including ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot, have integrated it. Google's own transition page says Gemini skills use "the open, Markdown-based SKILL.md format" (Google). So a teacher's skill built in Gemini should travel between tools. "Should" is doing work there; test one before promising anyone.

Google Vault can now export Gemini conversations. Under the 22 October update, admins on Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus can filter exports by user or organisational unit, up to 5,000 users, and by date range. It covers Gemini in Gmail, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Slides and Meet when conversation history is on; retention rules and litigation holds come later. Gemini chats inside Docs and Gmail are now retrievable records. Who may request them, and why, deserves a written answer before the first request.

Meet's speech translation now covers 63 languages. The 22 October update allows any combination of them, with automatic language detection. In schools it needs the Google AI Pro for Education add-on; Scheduled Release domains start on 2 November, and base plans cap sessions at 90 minutes. Google doesn't list the 63, so check the languages a school's families actually speak before anyone promises a parent a translated conference.

The Riff Returns But Slower

  • 1 November: Microsoft's AFT-negotiated standards start applying to schools it contracts with: no training AI on student or educator data, bar a narrow exception for data that could help protect students, plus third-party audits, according to the AFT. As of September, Google had not said whether it would follow (AP via KTAR, 14 September). For a Google school, that silence is the item.
  • 2 November: Meet speech translation begins reaching Scheduled Release domains, with visibility taking up to 15 days (Google, 22 October).
  • Early November: Vault API support for Gemini exports, due within 14 days of the interface rollout (Google, 22 October).
  • 17 November: Gems move to Settings in the Gemini app; Gemini-app skills finish rolling out by mid-November (Google).
  • Slow burn: the state suits against OpenAI. Texas's court-appointed evaluator is the remedy to watch; if any state wins something like it, pre-release review stops being voluntary (Texas Tribune, 23 October).

Sceptic's Corner

Next time someone says the answer is simply a well-designed tutor, offer three facts.

First, design reliably prevented harm; it did not reliably produce learning. Bastani's guard-railed tutor lifted practice scores by 127%, yet its students "scored about the same on the exam as the control group", who never had AI (Wharton).

Second, much of the positive evidence comes from builders marking their own homework. The Harvard team built the tutor it tested, and the paper notes that its authors overlap with those behind the active-learning method it beat (Scientific Reports). A September preprint on GCSE science found a solid effect (g = 0.33) for the Medly platform against self-directed revision; Medly funded the evaluation, and attrition was 30.7% (arXiv, September 2026).

Third, the horizons are short: two weeks at Harvard, four in England, six in Nigeria. Nobody yet knows what a year of well-designed AI does to a fifteen-year-old's ability to work without it.

So the defensible claim is narrower than the pitch. Designed AI use can avoid the damage open access does. Whether it beats a good teacher given the same minutes has been shown, so far, mainly in one Harvard physics course. Guardrails are a seatbelt, not an engine.
