Top story

The Gemini story has moved from launch theatre to implementation discipline. Quietly, thank God.

The weekend did not bring a new education bombshell. The useful signal is that school-facing Gemini adoption is now being written about less as "look what AI can do" and more as "what training, support, and expectations make it usable." A 22 May EdTech Magazine piece on K-12 Gemini adoption is vendor-adjacent, but the case examples are still useful: districts are using Gemini for planning, scheduling comparisons, meeting summaries, staff communication, Drive/Docs/Sheets workflows, and student-data/budget analysis, while emphasizing purpose, training, champions, and consistent expectations. (EdTech Magazine)

That matters for international schools because Google AI is no longer a separate "AI tool" conversation. It is settling into the Workspace layer teachers and leaders already use. The practical school question is therefore not whether Gemini exists. It is whether a school can name the approved use cases, the data boundaries, the staff training path, and the admin controls before the tool becomes informal infrastructure by habit.

Google's own education push points the same way. Its AI Educator Series, announced with ISTE+ASCD, is framed as free AI literacy training for U.S. K-12 and higher-ed educators, with modules released monthly. Its wider education update also ties Gemini, NotebookLM, Moodle LTI, NotebookLM limits, and student test-prep into one package. (Google for Education)

For Ed Tech Coaches and school technology leaders, the lesson is usefully dull: start with routines, not marvels. Email drafting, meeting summaries, source-bound research, curriculum planning, and data-table interpretation are the places staff will actually encounter Gemini. That is where norms need to live. If the first staff PD asks "what can Gemini do?", it will drift into party tricks. If it asks "which school workflows may Gemini touch, under what conditions, and how do we know?", it has a chance of becoming governance.

Other stories

  • NotebookLM inside Workspace Studio is still the highest-risk useful Google update on the table. Google's 12 May update makes existing notebooks usable as an AI knowledge source in Workspace Studio flows through an "Ask NotebookLM" step. It is available by default if Gemini for Google Workspace steps are allowed, including Education Fundamentals, Standard, Plus, Google AI Pro for Education, and Teaching and Learning add-ons. (Google Workspace Updates) This is not new since Friday, but it remains the operational hinge: a school notebook can become a workflow source. That turns source quality, ownership, and review dates into live governance, not filing etiquette.

  • Google's AI control center is the admin-side story to pair with every Gemini enthusiasm session. Google describes the control center as a centralized Admin console view for generative AI and agent access to Workspace data, including visibility into AI usage across Gmail, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Meet, Calendar, Chat, and the Gemini app. It also surfaces security controls, trust rules, classification labels, and data-protection rules. (Google Workspace Updates) Schools should ask what can be seen, what can be disabled, and what still falls outside the dashboard's polite little fence.

  • OpenAI's Edu admin surface is converging on the same governance shape. The latest ChatGPT Enterprise/Edu release notes now include admin-console areas for Analytics and Agents, with visibility into active users, message activity, GPTs, projects, skills, tool interactions, connector interactions, and workspace agents. The same notes flag ChatGPT for Excel and Google Sheets for Enterprise, Edu, and K-12 workspaces, with workspace controls including RBAC, residency where available, EKM, and Compliance API coverage. (OpenAI Help Center) Even for Google-first schools, this matters as a market signal: vendors know schools will ask for control planes. Procurement should make them prove those controls work in the school's actual data shape.

  • AILit is moving toward classroom exemplars, not just framework language. The AI Literacy Framework team says the final version is due in the coming weeks and will be accompanied by two ready-to-use multilingual exemplar activities: one primary activity around categorization and one secondary activity around verifying LLM outputs. (AILit Framework) This is useful because AI literacy work needs examples teachers can adapt inside subjects. Frameworks are necessary. So are activities with desks, timings, misunderstandings, and students in the room.

Sceptic's corner

The claim to challenge today is that "training" solves AI adoption.

Training helps. Bad training is still worse than no training because it gives everyone the confidence to be casually wrong in the same direction. A two-hour Gemini workshop can teach useful habits: source checking, prompt discipline, privacy boundaries, and where not to use the tool. It can also accidentally teach staff that every problem is now a Gemini-shaped problem, which is how schools end up with automated meeting notes, AI-polished family communications, and spreadsheet summaries before anyone has decided what data is allowed to move.

The real test is whether training changes defaults. After a session, do staff know which student data can go into Gemini? Do leaders know which workflows need review? Do departments know when NotebookLM is personal research and when it becomes a school knowledge base? Can IT see enough to help, or only enough to be blamed later?

AI PD should not be a confidence bath. It should create shared limits, shared vocabulary, and a small set of approved routines. Everything else is just product onboarding wearing a lanyard.

School AI governance now lives at the handoff: source notebooks, LMS models, spreadsheets, and prototypes becoming workflows.

Week ending Sunday, 24 May 2026 · 1,141 words · ~5 min read

The Week That Was

Google I/O did not produce an "education announcement" in the tidy old sense. It produced something more useful to watch: the agent layer moving into ordinary infrastructure. Google put Gemini 3.5 Flash into AI Studio, Antigravity, the Gemini API, and Android Studio; Workspace Studio gained NotebookLM as a source-grounded step; and Google's education lane kept pushing Gemini, NotebookLM, Moodle LTI, and educator training as the normal school toolkit. (Google I/O developer highlights, Google I/O recap, Workspace Updates, Google for Education)

The LMS and governance thread did not go away. Canvas remains the cautionary story; Toddle's own privacy language now names multiple model providers behind its AI layer; OpenAI kept tightening admin visibility around workspace agents, sheets, and connected apps. The shape is clear enough: schools are no longer choosing "an AI tool." They are choosing which systems get to act. (TechCrunch, Toddle privacy policy, OpenAI release notes, OpenAI workspace agents)

What Does It All Mean?

The useful synthesis this week is that school AI has split into three layers, and they are arriving at the same time.

Layer one is personal productivity: teacher drafts a lesson, admin summarises a spreadsheet, student asks for feedback. This is the layer most staff conversations still imagine when they say "AI." It is also the easiest layer to govern badly, because individual use feels private even when school data is involved.

Layer two is workflow authority. Workspace Studio can now ask NotebookLM inside an automation. ChatGPT workspace agents can run on schedules, use connected apps, and appear in admin consoles. AI Studio and Antigravity are turning prototypes into deployable applications more quickly. Once a workflow can fetch, summarise, decide, notify, and repeat, the question is no longer "did a teacher use AI?" It is "who authorised this routine, what source knowledge does it rely on, and what stops it when it is wrong?"

Layer three is curricular settlement. The IB's draft AI Design Principles, the OECD's Digital Education Outlook 2026, PISA 2029's Media and AI Literacy domain, and the EC-OECD AILit classroom exemplars all point away from tool demonstrations and toward judgement: evaluate outputs, preserve teacher agency, protect data, make ethics visible, and design tasks where AI changes the learning activity rather than merely speeding up submission. (IB AI Design Principles, OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026, PISA, AILit exemplars)

For Google-heavy international schools, the practical problem is that those layers will not arrive in the correct order. The Google layer is already there. Modern LMS platforms are bringing AI into curriculum planning, reporting, communications, behaviour documentation, and analytics. AI Studio prototypes will tempt capable teachers because they solve real classroom problems quickly. Meanwhile the policy layer will still be trying to write language that sounds stable enough for parents, teachers, and IT.

So the governance unit is not the app. It is the handoff. Student work handed to an LMS model. A teacher-authored unit handed to a curriculum assistant. A source notebook handed to a Workspace flow. A spreadsheet handed to ChatGPT. A prototype handed to Google Cloud. Each handoff needs an owner, a permitted data class, a logging expectation, an expiry rule, and a human judgement point that is more than decorative.

That is a better frame than "AI tools for teachers." It lets Ed Tech Coaches say yes to practical usefulness without letting every product define its own risk category.

Behind You! Behind You!

The IB published a useful school-level example on 22 May. DSB International School's piece is not grand theory; that is the point. It grounds AI in DP criteria, ATL skills, visible ethics, school-managed accounts, and teacher feedback. The line to borrow is structural: start with pedagogy, anchor in IB frameworks, teach judgement rather than dependency. (IB)

AILit is moving from framework to classroom exemplars. The May update says the final framework is due "in the coming weeks" and previews two multilingual exemplars: primary categorisation and secondary verification. That matters because fluency work needs a concrete activity layer, not just a standards map. (AILit Framework)

Toddle's privacy page is now more specific than its marketing page. The policy lists Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Whisper, and Gemini 2.5 Pro TTS for different Toddle AI functions, with an updateable third-party list. Good: more visibility. Still to ask: which modules are enabled, what the school contract overrides, what "pseudonymized" means in practice, and what logs/export controls admins get. (Toddle privacy policy, Toddle AI)

Google's policy-lab language is worth stealing, not worshipping. Their global AI Policy & Guidance Labs emphasise shared vocabulary, teacher lead, case studies, and 12-month implementation roadmaps. Vendor source, yes. Still a useful shape for turning "we need an AI policy" into working sessions. (Google for Education)

Sceptic's Corner

The phrase to be careful with after this week is "human in the loop." It sounds reassuring and often means almost nothing. A teacher glancing at an AI-generated report comment after three hours of fatigue is technically in the loop. An admin approving a Workspace automation they do not understand is in the loop. A student being told to "check Gemini's answer" without having the subject knowledge to check it is in the loop too. The loop can be a fig leaf.

The real test is stopping power. Can the human see the source, the prompt, the data class, the model route, the output history, and the consequence of accepting it? Can they refuse without breaking the workflow? Can they explain the judgement to a parent, student, colleague, or compliance officer? If not, the loop is theatre.

This is why the boring governance questions are now the interesting ones. Agentic AI makes school work faster by moving decisions into systems. Schools do not have to panic about that. But they do have to name which decisions are allowed to move.
