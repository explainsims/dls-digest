Friday, 6 November 2026

Top story

A Google Classroom assignment can now include a tutor and a chat record.

The 4 November Workspace release details the rollout of teacher-assigned Guided Learning. An educator attaches the activity to an assignment and can choose class materials to give Gemini context. The student opens a focused tab with Gemini's side navigation hidden and new chats disabled within that tab. Google says the rollout began on 3 November for Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus, in all Gemini-supported languages, and can take longer than 15 days to appear. Both the teacher and students need Gemini enabled through their group or organisational unit (Google Workspace Updates, 4 November).

The teacher's view changes the character of the assignment. Classroom can show AI-generated summaries for the class and for individual students. Teachers can see transcripts from their assigned activities when Gemini activity is on, while conversations students start separately sit outside the teacher's view. The help page says deleting the Classroom post removes student access to the activity but leaves past conversations in place. It also says generated insights can make mistakes and are intended to assist instruction. Google's guidance reserves them for instructional support and cautions against using them for assessment or placement (Google Classroom Help for teachers).

For Ed Tech Coaches in international schools, consider a student using the tool to work through an explanation of why two circuits behave differently. The teacher could supply the class diagram and then see which misconception to revisit tomorrow. The same chat may also include an improvised disclosure or a personal detail the student would never put on a worksheet. The assignment therefore needs two clear purposes. Students should understand the thinking the tutor is there to support, and they should know who can read the conversation. The platform makes it easier to attach the tutoring activity. It takes a teacher's judgment to define its limits and to check whether the learner can explain the circuit after the chat closes.

Other stories

  • Australia gives primary AI and secondary assessment different policy boundaries. In a communiqué dated 5 November following their 2 November meeting, Australian education ministers agreed to restrict student use of generative AI in primary schools and in secondary student assessments. They agreed to introduce, trial or consider limits on screen use and left implementation to states, territories and education sectors. A new taskforce will advise ministers on practical measures, with representation from the independent and Catholic sectors among others. Digital devices and AI stay on the ministers' agenda until at least the end of 2027 (Education Ministers Meeting communiqué, 5 November, p. 1). The distinction matters well beyond Australia. A supervised secondary lesson about the reliability of generated claims and a secondary assessment of a student's own argument can have different rules even in the same subject. For international schools that teach across divisions, a single permissions table can hide that distinction unless teachers and students can see where the boundary changes. Implementation will show whether the restrictions are stated in terms teachers can apply to the task in front of them.

  • State AI guidance talks to adults first. A University of Washington preprint posted on 4 November compared 36 K–12 AI guidance documents from 35 U.S. states and Puerto Rico. The authors coded privacy and academic integrity as addressed in 97% of the documents each, while 83% gave guidance on evaluating vendors. Only 47% mentioned funding or budget and 47% committed to a review cycle. Only 22% addressed students directly and 33% addressed IT staff directly. The authors found specific examples, including vendor rubrics in Montana, Michigan and North Carolina. The study examined what the documents say, leaving actual implementation outside its scope (Memon and colleagues, 4 November, Table 1 and sections 4.1 and 5.2). The transferable question for a school is how a principle reaches each person who has to use it. A teacher needs to know where to take a new product for review. The team doing that review needs to know what evidence counts. A student needs to know how to ask for help when a tool crosses a boundary. An elegant policy can leave all three people guessing.

  • The Philippines puts classroom safety into teacher AI training. On 5 November Microsoft announced a Philippine launch with the Department of Education, reporting that around 100 senior high school teachers attended a day of training in Makati. With training partner Audentes Technologies, the programme aims to train and credential 2,000 public school educators in AI literacy. Microsoft lists two virtual sessions this month and eight live learning sessions from January through May 2027. The launch also brought in scenario-based materials developed with Cyberlite on oversharing, AI-assisted decisions and emotional attachment. Microsoft says the materials drew on co-design workshops with 131 students aged 12–18 across nine schools in India and Singapore (Microsoft Source Asia, 5 November). The credential count will describe the programme's reach. The classroom test is whether a young person can recognise when a conversation has crossed into private or emotionally consequential territory and identify a trusted adult to turn to. A fictional scenario lets a class practise that judgment without asking anyone to disclose what they have said to a chatbot.

  • A student journal can show where the thinking happened. University of Sydney researchers Ali Shakiba and Thomas Chaffey coded 962 self-reported prompts from 105 students in an AI-permissive first-year software engineering course. In their 3 November preprint, 84.2% of recorded prompts showed some contribution beyond delegation under the researchers' coding scheme, while explicit constraints on the AI's output were rare. Students chose which interactions to report, and the researchers coded what was visible in the prompt text. Their measure describes the contribution visible there and supplies no direct measure of learning (Shakiba and Chaffey, 3 November, methods, results and limitations). In a secondary design class, the useful adaptation is a small check on one decision. A student can record what they had already tried, what help they sought, and which result they accepted or rejected and why. A teacher can then ask them to defend that choice against a new constraint. That would put the emphasis on judgment and keep the journal short enough to read alongside the work.

  • Burnaby's recovery and its exposure investigation are on different clocks. In a 3 November update on its September ransomware incident, the British Columbia district confirmed that attackers had copied and stolen files containing personal information. It said it had received reports of a claimed dark-web release, believed some district files might be included, and had brought in an external expert to establish the extent. The district lists registration documents used to establish residency and records of specialised learning support among the information that may have been affected. It says phones were restored on 28 September and access to MyEducation, including the family portal, on 1 October (Burnaby Schools, updated 3 November). For international schools, the detail about residency is a reminder that an ordinary enrolment folder can contain identity documents as well as contact details. An incident review can therefore ask what duplicate exports exist and who can open them, alongside the question of when the platform came back. A family needs a credible answer about its own records even after it can sign in again.

On the radar

  • UNESCO puts reporting and referrals at the centre of its school-safety dialogue. Its 5 November Member State meeting, co-sponsored by Ireland and Mexico, was scheduled to consider how to turn commitments on school violence and bullying into reporting and referral systems, teacher support and accountability (UNESCO event agenda, 5 November). The agenda included a technical brief on technology-facilitated violence first published on 26 October and updated on 27 October. It treats governance, safeguarding and curriculum as linked and identifies a role for platforms and regulators (UNESCO technical brief page). The follow-through to watch is how reporting gets translated into a route a young person can actually find and trust.

  • Indonesia plans to give competition projects a life after the final. The Ministry of Primary and Secondary Education's 3 November notice scheduled the ImajiNation visual-coding final for 5 November in Jakarta, with four junior secondary teams due to develop digital work live. The ministry reports 6,800 student and teacher participants from 38 provinces across the competition and plans to showcase winning teacher innovations in the Ruang Murid section of Rumah Pendidikan (Indonesia's education ministry, 3 November). What travels is the design of that second step. A teacher elsewhere can learn from a winning resource if its context and instructions travel with it.

Sceptic's corner

The sales pitch for an AI summary is that it can turn a roomful of conversations into a clear picture of where the class is stuck. The Classroom launch makes that appealing. A teacher planning tomorrow's lesson could see repeated questions before another student puts up a hand. Google's own help describes the themes as a sample from the interactions and says summaries have usage limits. Its student guidance also says students will see a message telling them which conversations their teacher can view. Those facts matter when interpreting who appears in a summary and what they felt comfortable writing.

Consider a class report that flags confusion about fractions. One student typed a detailed question, one discussed the problem with a partner, and one copied a worked example before opening the activity. The summary can surface a question the teacher might otherwise miss. The class still needs a common piece of work if the teacher wants to know how widespread the difficulty is. An exit ticket asking everyone to explain why two differently written fractions represent the same quantity gives the teacher something to compare across the class, including students who had little to say in chat.

There is real value in finding a good question faster. Give the dashboard that job. Evidence that a student understands the idea still has to come from something the student can do and explain.

The Week That Was

The week kept returning to one gap: naming a problem is not the same as stopping it. New York's Education Department named 38 products, then said it was working with companies to find out whether the offending features could be disabled at all (Chalkbeat, 23 October). The UK's AI Security Institute described an agent that often said an attack would be harmful in the very situations where it went ahead (AISI, 23 October). Gemini's immersive explorations reached the student hub in Education editions with the line "There is no admin control for this feature" (Google, 28 October).

Since Friday's brief: whole-deck editing with Gemini in Slides, which schools get through the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, carries the same line (Google, 30 October). For schools, that makes the add-on licence the only switch. OpenAI said ChatGPT will restrict practical weapons information for teens even without signs of violent intent, end chats that seem fixated on violence, and may pause accounts after repeated ones, telling parents when a teen's is paused. Rollout starts within weeks (Reuters via AOL, 30 October).

What Does It All Mean?

Every story above assumes a lever somewhere. So the question worth a Sunday: when a school cannot switch a feature off, what is its AI rule actually made of?

Start with the lever schools think they have. Among American teens who use AI for schoolwork, Common Sense Media found in August that 44% had had an AI tool blocked on a school network or device, and 59% of those simply moved to a personal device, where there is even less support (Common Sense Media, 18 August). A network block is a detour, not a wall.

Assessment researchers have been making this case for a while. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu split institutional responses into discursive changes, which tell students what is allowed, and structural ones, which change how the task itself works. Traffic-light permission schemes, they argue, borrow the look of a real enforcement system without its power, and leaning on rules like these creates what they call an "enforcement illusion" (Corbin, Dawson and Liu, 2025).

The University of Sydney, where Liu works, had already written the idea into policy: from 2025, AI allowed by default except in exams and in-semester tests, then two lanes (ACS Information Age, December 2024). Secured, supervised tasks leave AI to the examiner; everything else is open, with students guided in its use. Its FAQ is blunt that AI use cannot be restricted in assessments not secured face to face, nor reliably detected (Teaching@Sydney, updated April 2026).

Even the AI Assessment Scale's own authors agree. Leon Furze, one of them, writes that assessment security is not what the scale aims at; it is a framework to support assessment design, and a colourful handout plus "a hopeful expectation" secures nothing (Furze, September 2025).

That would be bleak if rules kept students honest. They never did. A follow-up study published in February, surveying 4,354 students at six US high schools about eighteen months after ChatGPT's release, found an overall cheating rate of 72.06%, which its authors call consistent with historical baselines; AI availability does not appear to have changed overall prevalence (Chen et al., February 2026). The tool changed the method, not the rate. Whatever sets the rate, whether engagement, pressure or a task that feels worth doing, sits upstream of any switch.

So the working answer: an AI rule rests on three layers, in falling order of reliability. First, what is structurally true of the task: done in the room, staged, defended aloud. Second, what the school can actually switch, which is coarse and covers products more than features. Third, what it tells students, which is invaluable for conversation and useless as security.

For a school running an AI use scale, the practical move is small. The scale stays as the shared language. But every task pitched at the no-AI or low-AI end needs a one-line answer to "what makes this true in the room?" If the honest answer is that the student promised, the task is open and should be marked as one.

Behind You! Behind You!

New York will treat unverified chatbot users as children. Governor Hochul signed S.9051B/A.10379C on 28 October. In the coming months, companies may not offer under-18s, or any user not confirmed as an adult, features such as posing as human, claiming a relationship, asking unprompted emotional questions or using a child's private details from earlier chats. The attorney general can seek up to $25,000 per violation (New York State, 28 October). The state's Business Council had argued that educational tools were swept in (TechTimes, 29 October). Watch whether vendors build that default once and ship it everywhere.

States want proof and can't get it. SETDA's 2026 State EdTech Trends report, published 29 October from 73 survey responses, found 79% of state leaders naming AI a top technology priority. Yet 52% said their state collects no data on the use or effectiveness of ed-tech products, and 68% said independently verifying vendor claims about AI products is challenging (GovTech, 29 October). If American states with procurement offices struggle, an international school is checking alone.

Half of the US schools and districts surveyed had a cyber incident last year. The Center for Internet Security's 2026 K-12 report puts it at 51% in the past 12 months, and 66% of districts leave cybersecurity to one or two people (CIS, 27 October). Phishing or email compromise ranked among the top incidents for 87% of leaders, and the report warns of more convincing AI-enabled phishing (K-12 Dive, 27 October). The weak point is staff inboxes, not servers.

The ed-tech backlash is on the ballot, and it isn't partisan. Ahead of Tuesday's US midterms, Chalkbeat found a Republican candidate for California governor promising to ban AI in schools on day one. In Texas, the Republican governor pledges less classroom screen time and his Democratic challenger opposes AI for elementary students. Seven of eight San Francisco school board candidates back limits on classroom technology (Chalkbeat, 26 October). International schools enrolling families from those systems should expect the mood to travel with them.

Sceptic's Corner

Next time someone says the research shows AI improves learning, ask which research.

Jill Barshay reports 39 meta-analyses on AI in education, every one finding substantial benefits. Patrick O'Neill, a community college professor, audited 14 of them and found serious conceptual and statistical flaws, from hallucinated references to underlying studies citing generative-AI papers published before the technology was public. One, published in a Nature journal in May 2025, has since been retracted (Hechinger Report, 26 October).

When O'Neill pooled 44 well-designed experiments, outcomes ranged from very helpful to very harmful, and his preliminary calculations put the average at roughly zero. Susanna Loeb's team gathered almost 900 studies and judged only 20 good enough for its evidence summary. O'Neill's audit is itself a preprint and his numbers are preliminary, so hold him to the same standard.

Two things follow. A literature that is uniformly positive is a warning, not a comfort; real interventions produce null results. An average of roughly zero is not a verdict of harmlessness. It is what you get when good designs and bad ones cancel out, which is why the question is always which use, for whom, checked how.

The line for a staff meeting: there is no effect size for "AI", only for particular uses of it. Anyone quoting one number is quoting a brochure.
