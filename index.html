Monday, 26 October 2026

Top story

New York's school AI restrictions have reached the awkward business of deciding which features count.

In reporting published on 23 October, Chalkbeat obtained the Education Department's list of 38 flagged products and contacted the companies behind them. The department says the restrictions concern student-facing generative AI features, rather than necessarily banning whole products. Several suppliers dispute why their tools were listed. Others have disabled features and regained permission to operate. That is the new development here: an account of implementation, more than a month after the initial announcement (Chalkbeat, 23 October).

The examples resist a clean approved-or-banned label. Amira, used in more than 200 city schools, was stopped outright because officials said it could not function without AI. Magma Math says it disabled AI-generated hints on 4 September and received permission to continue on 5 October. Wordly disputes its inclusion, saying its translation tool does not use generative AI. These are reported company positions and departmental explanations, not an independent technical audit. They illustrate why an inventory of product names cannot quite do the job of an inventory of what each product does.

For international schools, the transferable question is what a teacher can actually enable inside an already familiar tool. A school might reasonably approve a worksheet platform while holding back its new conversational tutor. That distinction needs to survive the sign-in, the class setup and the next supplier update. Otherwise staff inherit the impossible job of interpreting a policy from whatever buttons happen to appear that morning.

The practical measure of governance is whether the rule can be explained and reproduced: this function, for this age group, with this data, under these controls. A blanket yes conceals variation; a blanket no can remove useful functions along with disputed ones. Neither position saves the institution from understanding the software. The quieter lesson from New York is that drawing a boundary is only the beginning of making it hold.

Other stories

  • Google Sheets is gaining a useful statistics feature without asking anyone to prompt a chatbot. Its 22 October announcement introduces native box-and-whisker charts from raw numeric data, including selectable inclusive or exclusive quartile calculations and controls for outliers, inner points and mean markers. Excel imports retain these charts, and Sheets exports them as editable Excel charts. Rapid Release rollout started on 22 October; Scheduled Release starts on 6 November, with up to 15 days for visibility. Google lists all Workspace customers and personal accounts, with no separate administrator control (Google Workspace Updates). For teaching, that lowers the mechanical cost of comparing distributions while leaving the interesting decisions intact. Which observations belong together? Does a difference in medians hide a much wider spread? Why did changing the quartile convention alter the picture? Those questions are available to students who can inspect the data and the chart settings. The same caution applies to school dashboards: an outlier marker identifies a position in a distribution, without explaining the person behind it. A modest charting improvement can support better statistical discussion than a fluent automated interpretation, provided someone still asks what the plot permits them to conclude.

  • Apple has opened a school purchasing route for app subscriptions sold by the seat. From 22 October, organisations using Apple School Manager or Apple Business can buy seats for auto-renewable subscriptions through one in-app purchase and assign them through existing device-management tools. Seats can be added or removed as needs change. Developers control availability, although multiseat purchasing is enabled by default unless they have previously changed the setting. This is distinct from consumer group purchases: sandbox testing for those starts in November, with the App Store launch planned for winter (Apple Developer, 22 October). For an Apple school, the meaningful change is the possibility of institutional ownership where a useful subscription previously sat on a teacher's individual account. It does not establish that a particular app participates, offers suitable terms, or handles student information appropriately. Nor does convenient seat management answer what happens to work when a subscription ends. The sensible comparison is the whole lifecycle: purchase, assignment, renewal, a staff departure and eventual exit. A tidier checkout is welcome. It should make those questions easier to administer rather than make them disappear behind the purchase button.

  • A newly posted workplace study counts the labour between an AI draft and something safe to send. Najeeb Gambo Abdulhamid and colleagues submitted their Kenya-and-Nigeria study on 22 October. It draws on 21 interviews with 27 workers in 21 small and medium-sized businesses, conducted from July to October 2023. The publication is new; the tools and experiences described are from that earlier period. Participants reported checking claims, repairing unusable references, comparing tools and correcting language or local-context failures before circulating outputs. The authors treat the two countries as situated cases, without claiming a representative picture of either region (preprint). This is qualitative workplace evidence, not a school trial or a measured productivity effect. Its useful contribution for teacher professional learning is a fuller unit of work. Timing the generation of a resource stops the clock too early if the teacher then spends the saved minutes checking examples, rebuilding sources and correcting assumptions about the learners. Conversely, that checking may make the finished resource better than the previous one. A fair evaluation needs both possibilities visible. Asking a teacher to bring the first draft, the repaired version and an account of the changes would reveal more than another satisfaction score about how easy prompting felt.

  • Google Vids is getting ordinary editing controls that make explanatory video more workable. Two 23 October announcements add independent audio extraction and clip playback-speed adjustment. Detached audio can be trimmed or moved across scenes without disturbing the video; speed ranges from 0.25× to 2×. Audio extraction began a gradual rollout on 19 October, speed adjustment on 16 October, both taking up to 15 days across release tracks. Google lists all Workspace customers, Workspace Individual subscribers and personal accounts (audio extraction; playback speed). A teacher explaining a procedure can keep narration running while the visual moves to the relevant detail, or slow a short sequence rather than repeat a whole recording. Students making explanations gain an editorial choice worth discussing: what does the audience need time to notice? These are editing capabilities, with no learning gains demonstrated in the announcements. In a science explanation, altered speed also needs to be made explicit whenever timing is evidence. A clip made easier to watch should not quietly become misleading data. That distinction itself makes a worthwhile media-literacy conversation, alongside crediting footage and preserving the meaning of the original event.

  • Blackboard's latest institutional conversation puts the burden of complexity on the agenda. Its 23 October account of a UK higher-education executive roundtable describes overlapping systems, unnecessary licences, course-template checking and interest in portfolios and assessment across a longer learning journey. Participants also discussed making data useful to the people expected to act on it. This is a supplier's summary of a discussion, with no reported intervention results or demonstrated savings (Blackboard Community). The school-relevant tension is between consistency and fit. A common course structure can help families find the assignment; forcing every learning experience into an identical sequence can create its own workarounds. Equally, a specialist tool may solve one department's problem while adding another login and another support obligation for everyone else. Neither the largest platform nor the smallest collection of subscriptions wins automatically. The useful evidence would follow a few real tasks across the system: where a teacher repeats an entry, where a student loses the thread, where someone must repair the handover. Simplification earns its place when those journeys improve. Counting fewer products is an administrative result, not yet an educational one.

On the radar

  • Frederick County's student-AI restrictions now have an implementing regulation. The district says Regulation 124-01 was adopted on 22 October, following its 7 October board policy. Its public explanation restricts student AI use while allowing specific curricular needs, explicit AI-literacy instruction and uses required by an Individualized Education Program or Section 504 Plan (district update). The adoption notice establishes the regulation's existence, rather than showing how it works in classrooms. The implementation worth watching is whether staff can explain those exceptions consistently in ordinary lessons, and whether families understand what remains available. A restriction's practical meaning emerges through those decisions.

Sceptic's corner

The reassuring claim running through today's announcements is that a human remains in charge. It is a necessary starting condition and a remarkably incomplete description of a working arrangement.

My criterion for workable oversight is more demanding than putting a person at the end of a workflow: the reviewer needs the authority and information to interpret, override or reject an output before it affects someone. The difficult words are information and before. A teacher receiving a polished recommendation with no usable trail may have formal permission to reject it while lacking any realistic means of deciding whether to do so. A review that arrives after the message has gone out cannot protect its recipient.

My test would be mundane. Can the responsible adult explain one consequential claim, show the evidence that supports it and describe what would cause them to stop the process? Can they do that within the time the workflow actually allows? If the answer requires an afternoon nobody has, the review condition exists on paper while the system runs on trust.

That does not mean every use needs a committee. A rough brainstorm and a recommendation about a child's support deserve different scrutiny. It means the person doing the checking needs a task they can complete, evidence they can inspect and a real chance to change the outcome. Putting a human in the loop is easy. Giving that human a workable job is the part worth specifying.

The Week That Was

Google shipped at its usual clip, most usefully insight summaries that give teachers "greater visibility into how students interact with assigned notebooks" (Google, 19 October). The research ran the other way: at the University of Maryland, offering undergraduates a course-integrated AI tutor lowered their grades (IZA, October 2026). That gap is this week's question.

Since Friday's brief: Texas sued OpenAI and Sam Altman in Collin County on Friday 23 October. It wants an injunction requiring OpenAI to block minors from its product and to get any new model approved by a court-appointed evaluator (Texas Tribune, 23 October). Among the harms Paxton's press release lists: "users have lost critical thinking skills". The Tribune notes that Elon Musk, an OpenAI rival, is the largest single donor to Paxton's Senate campaign. Google also made Gemini Notebook a connected app inside the Gemini app, on by default outside the European Economic Area wherever both are enabled (Google, 23 October).

Saturday 24 October: Fortune ran a commentary arguing that every child will get an AI tutor but only families who can pay will keep human teachers (Fortune, 24 October). Its author founded school and IT-education networks, which is worth knowing. Sunday was quiet.

What Does It All Mean?

Last week's answer was that manner matters more than dose. Maryland raises the next question: who sets the manner? Is an AI tutor something a school switches on, or something a teacher designs?

Read past the headline, a fall of up to 0.37 standard deviations in final grades, and the Maryland paper half-answers it (IZA). The tutor had a mode that guides students toward an answer instead of supplying one. The default was direct instruction, and only two instructors ended the term with tutor mode on. About 15% of students offered the tool used it even once. Of their requests, 73.8% sought information, explanations or solutions; 0.7% sought feedback on their own work. The authors allow that the tool's arrival may have shifted perceived permission to use AI generally, so the result may measure a signal as much as a product.

The trials where AI tutoring worked look different. At Harvard, Greg Kestin and colleagues built their physics tutor's prompts around active engagement, cognitive load and growth mindset, and walked students through each problem in sequence. Students learned more, in less time, than in an active-learning class (Scientific Reports, June 2025). A six-week after-school programme in Nigeria produced gains of about 0.3 standard deviations; the World Bank team's verdict is that the tool works as a tutor when implemented "with teacher support" (World Bank, January 2025). Stanford's Tutor CoPilot put the AI behind the human: students whose tutors had it were 4 percentage points more likely to master topics, and 9 points with lower-rated tutors (Wang and colleagues, arXiv).

The cleanest high-school test isolates the variable. Hamsa Bastani's team gave nearly 1,000 maths students in Turkey either a plain ChatGPT-style interface or a tutor that "includes teacher input and is designed to guide students with hints rather than directly giving answers" (Wharton, August 2024). On the unaided exam, the plain group scored 17% below students who never had AI. The tutor group avoided that loss (PNAS, July 2025).

So the working answer: AI tutoring helps when someone has designed what it does, when, and what follows. Made merely available, it becomes an answer machine, and the defaults decide the rest.

That matters for schools running Gemini, because Google's defaults are generous. The Gemini student hub, with study notebooks, flashcards and quizzes, reaches Education users of all ages, governed only by the admin console's generative AI settings (Google, 1 October). That is Maryland's condition at scale. A teacher-assigned Gemini Notebook is nearer Harvard's. The number worth tracking this year is not accounts provisioned but tasks designed, each followed by a short unaided check.

Behind You! Behind You!

Only one US state requires AI tools to be checked for their effect on learning. CRPE's annual State of the American Student report counts 36 states with published or endorsed AI guidance, most of it advisory (CRPE). The only one requiring AI tools to be vetted for academic outcomes is Maryland (The 74, 21 October), home of the university behind this week's sobering tutor trial. International schools have no state doing even that; for them, the only vetting layer is the school itself.

Anthropic handed the Agent Skills standard to a neutral foundation. On 22 October it donated the format to the Agentic AI Foundation, the Linux Foundation body it co-founded with Block and OpenAI. Anthropic says more than 70 agent platforms, including ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot, have integrated it. Google's own transition page says Gemini skills use "the open, Markdown-based SKILL.md format" (Google). So a teacher's skill built in Gemini should travel between tools. "Should" is doing work there; test one before promising anyone.

Google Vault can now export Gemini conversations. Under the 22 October update, admins on Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus can filter exports by user or organisational unit, up to 5,000 users, and by date range. It covers Gemini in Gmail, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Slides and Meet when conversation history is on; retention rules and litigation holds come later. Gemini chats inside Docs and Gmail are now retrievable records. Who may request them, and why, deserves a written answer before the first request.

Meet's speech translation now covers 63 languages. The 22 October update allows any combination of them, with automatic language detection. In schools it needs the Google AI Pro for Education add-on; Scheduled Release domains start on 2 November, and base plans cap sessions at 90 minutes. Google doesn't list the 63, so check the languages a school's families actually speak before anyone promises a parent a translated conference.

The Riff Returns But Slower

  • 1 November: Microsoft's AFT-negotiated standards start applying to schools it contracts with: no training AI on student or educator data, bar a narrow exception for data that could help protect students, plus third-party audits, according to the AFT. As of September, Google had not said whether it would follow (AP via KTAR, 14 September). For a Google school, that silence is the item.
  • 2 November: Meet speech translation begins reaching Scheduled Release domains, with visibility taking up to 15 days (Google, 22 October).
  • Early November: Vault API support for Gemini exports, due within 14 days of the interface rollout (Google, 22 October).
  • 17 November: Gems move to Settings in the Gemini app; Gemini-app skills finish rolling out by mid-November (Google).
  • Slow burn: the state suits against OpenAI. Texas's court-appointed evaluator is the remedy to watch; if any state wins something like it, pre-release review stops being voluntary (Texas Tribune, 23 October).

Sceptic's Corner

Next time someone says the answer is simply a well-designed tutor, offer three facts.

First, design reliably prevented harm; it did not reliably produce learning. Bastani's guard-railed tutor lifted practice scores by 127%, yet its students "scored about the same on the exam as the control group", who never had AI (Wharton).

Second, much of the positive evidence comes from builders marking their own homework. The Harvard team built the tutor it tested, and the paper notes that its authors overlap with those behind the active-learning method it beat (Scientific Reports). A September preprint on GCSE science found a solid effect (g = 0.33) for the Medly platform against self-directed revision; Medly funded the evaluation, and attrition was 30.7% (arXiv, September 2026).

Third, the horizons are short: two weeks at Harvard, four in England, six in Nigeria. Nobody yet knows what a year of well-designed AI does to a fifteen-year-old's ability to work without it.

So the defensible claim is narrower than the pitch. Designed AI use can avoid the damage open access does. Whether it beats a good teacher given the same minutes has been shown, so far, mainly in one Harvard physics course. Guardrails are a seatbelt, not an engine.
