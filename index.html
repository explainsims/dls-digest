Wednesday, 11 November 2026

Top story

The evidence grew; the verdict stayed conditional.

Stanford's SCALE Initiative published its updated AI-in-education review on 10 November, expanding its causal evidence base from 20 papers to 52. The 32 additions comprise 28 examining student-facing tools and four examining educator-facing tools. Five new student-facing studies took place in U.S. K–12 settings, where the earlier review had none. These are newly reviewed findings, mainly from papers published between October 2025 and June 2026, rather than 32 experiments conducted this week.

The distinction that survives the larger sample is between using AI to support learning and using it to produce the work. The former looks more promising, with mixed results; the latter can weaken later independent performance. Pleasant learning experiences also frequently fail to translate into stronger learning outcomes. The researchers still cannot establish whether gains persist over longer periods. Their repository relies heavily on preprints, and causal findings in one setting do not automatically travel to another.

For international secondary schools, that makes the most useful evaluation question surprisingly small: what can the student do when the assistance comes away? Imagine two classes using the same assistant to prepare explanations of an unfamiliar graph. Both submit cleaner explanations. A week later, give each student a different graph and ask them to defend an interpretation without the assistant. The second task tests the claim that matters to a teacher; the polished first product tests something else.

That need not become a miniature university trial every time someone tries a tool. A department can name one intended learning gain, retain a modest comparison with its usual practice and arrange a delayed check before expanding. The report supplies a reason to make those choices explicit. Fifty-two papers are a stronger starting point than twenty. They still do not make the product category the intervention: the task, guidance and conditions of use remain part of what is being tested.

Other stories

  • OpenAI's new education report puts a number on the guidance gap. In its 10 November account of EMEA partnerships, OpenAI cites ThinkYoung findings that 79% of surveyed students used AI for homework or assignments, while 47% said they had been shown how. ThinkYoung's research page describes surveys of 5,000 students aged 13–19 and 600 teachers, plus student and parent focus groups. These are reported experiences, and the percentages should not be treated as an estimate for students in another school or as evidence of learning gains. The practical question is what a student thinks instruction permits after attending the introduction. Show a fictional homework task, an acceptable request for help and a request that hands over the thinking. Ask the pupil to explain the difference and where to seek clarification. A school can have a polished presentation about responsible AI and still leave that choice obscure. Training attendance tells us who was in the room; a worked example tells us whether the boundary travelled home.

  • Claude is becoming a place where the shared working file stays. Anthropic's 10 November launch introduces Whiteboards and Sheets in beta on paid plans and makes the former Dashboards generally available as Claude Data. Sheets can create tabs, formulas and dropdowns, with export to Excel or CSV. Data adds certified queries and live sharing; viewers can see dashboard results without having the connectors behind them. On Team plans all three are enabled by default. On Enterprise, Whiteboards and Sheets start off, while Data switches on by default on 19 November unless administrators change the setting. For a school evaluating the platform, the interesting test is a handover. Build a harmless departmental tracker, let a second teacher edit it and export it, then check what remains legible: formulas, changes, ownership and the definition behind any displayed number. Generating a spreadsheet is a useful convenience. Leaving the next colleague a file they can understand is the working standard, especially when the person who prompted it is absent.

  • A maths programme teaches AI by making the model visible. A 10 November Concord Consortium profile of researcher Jie Chao describes a six-hour supplement for Algebra I or Integrated Math 1. Students use algebra and statistics to create, test and refine mathematical models of language. Its spring 2025 pilot involved more than 80 Florida Virtual School students in Grades 6–11. A separate 2025–26 randomised pilot with 80 Algebra I students reported greater gains in AI self-efficacy for the treatment group. That is confidence in understanding and creating AI, rather than a demonstrated increase in mathematics achievement. The organisation is describing its own programme, so the distinction belongs beside the result. For secondary teachers, the curricular idea is stronger than another session on prompt tips: let pupils see what a model does with an example, alter the example and explain the change. A student who can identify which features shifted a prediction has something more durable than familiarity with a particular chatbot's menu. The profile also points to six free modules connecting AI with subjects including English and history.

  • Google's reusable skills arrive as copies, with a maintenance consequence. Its 9 November Workspace release starts the rollout of custom instructions across Studio, Gmail, Docs, Slides, Drive and Chat. The education eligibility listed is the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, for users aged 18 and over; it is not a blanket student rollout. Rapid Release starts on 9 November, Scheduled Release on 30 November, each taking up to 15 days. Sharing from Studio gives recipients independent copies, so later edits to the original do not update copies they saved. Referenced Drive files need their own permissions. English prompts and instructions are supported; Sheets and Meet are outside the initial surfaces. For an international school, a departmental resource-formatting skill could therefore be useful and quietly diverge into six versions. Put the owner, revision date and source policy in the instructions themselves, then give colleagues a way to recognise a retired copy. Sharing a rule is easy. Knowing which version produced a resource three months later is the more interesting administrative problem.

  • Workspace automation can wait, which makes old assumptions worth checking. Google's 9 November Studio update adds a pause inside a flow, including branches and loops, for a specified date or duration up to 14 days. The Activity view records the intended resume time and time zone. Rollout was scheduled over one to three days for both release tracks, including Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus. There is no separate admin control for the step. This is useful plumbing for a reminder sequence that previously needed separate schedules. It also creates an obvious school test: what happens if the request is resolved while the automation sleeps? In a synthetic example, mark a form complete after the pause starts and see whether the resumed sequence checks current status before sending its reminder. A delay preserves the flow's place in time. It does not, by itself, establish that the reason for the next action still exists. The most helpful reminder is sometimes the one that gets cancelled.

On the radar

  • A Google integration deadline arrives today. An official developer notice dated 12 October says Workspace MCP servers start rejecting requests on 11 November from OAuth clients that have not been marked for an AI-powered agent. Clients left unmarked by 12 November will require replacement to use MCP. This concerns agent integrations, including the listed Gmail, Calendar, Chat, Docs and Drive servers; ordinary REST-only applications are unaffected. The current deadline, rather than the original announcement, earns the slot. A supplier using MCP should be able to say which client changed and show a successful authorised call after enforcement, rather than merely confirm that someone saw the notice.

  • California's summit supplies a useful accountability question. The education department's 9 November release describes an AI summit spanning literacy, mathematics, workforce preparation, safety and privacy, with educators, students and district leaders involved. It calls for evidence of improved student outcomes and keeping educators central to instructional decisions. Those are commitments in an event statement, rather than results from an intervention. Watch for the implementation detail that would make them inspectable: who decides an experiment is unsuccessful, what evidence they use and whether the district can stop without losing the underlying service. An ambitious summit becomes useful to another school when those choices acquire names, dates and examples.

Sceptic's corner

Satisfaction is an especially slippery scoreboard because both sides can make it mean what they need. If students like an AI tutor, advocates can call that engagement. If they dislike it, advocates can call that productive struggle. The argument then becomes impossible to lose and rather difficult to evaluate.

There is a useful uncomfortable detail in OpenAI's new EMEA report, in its Estonia discussion. It reports a student Net Promoter Score of −27.5 and satisfaction of 3.07 out of five for the ITI tutor, with 182 respondents. Feedback included excessive questioning, slow exchanges and requests for clearer explanations. OpenAI explicitly separates those experiences from proof of whether learning improved. Publishing an awkward result is useful; treating the awkwardness itself as proof that the pedagogy worked would undo the benefit.

A student who wants the answer may resent a good question. A student who already understands the first step may also resent a bad sequence of six unnecessary ones. The teacher needs enough evidence to tell those situations apart. Inspect a few exchanges against the task's learning goal, ask students where the conversation stalled and check what they can subsequently explain independently. Keep the usability finding and the learning finding side by side.

The question is not whether a tutor can sustain dialogue. It is whether this dialogue earns the time it takes from this learner. Endless questions can be a teaching method. They can also be a loading screen with manners.

Filed Sunday, 8 November 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 8 November 2026 · 1,434 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

The week belonged to Google, and to the question of who decides. On Tuesday it announced that student Gemini and Search would be off by default, said schools rejecting its AI would lose Search, then reversed within the day; a spokesman confirmed they are "separate controls" (GovTech, 4 November). Search still arrives with its AI features (Bloomberg via Claims Journal, 5 November), which Google says will offer step-by-step guided learning (CNBC, 3 November). On Wednesday Google set out how teachers can attach Guided Learning to Classroom assignments and read each student's chat, rolling out since Tuesday (Google, 4 November).

Since Friday's brief: a quiet weekend. Nothing material surfaced on Saturday or Sunday. The biggest story the dailies missed broke on Thursday, US time; it leads Behind You!

What Does It All Mean?

Two of the week's stories sit on opposite sides of one screen. Guided Learning now gives teachers "individual student chat transcripts" (Google, 4 November). A lawsuit alleges that a 13-year-old discussed suicidal thoughts with Google's AI on a school Chromebook for months before her school alerted her parents, and that Google does not provide chat data on student accounts (Bloomberg via Claims Journal, 5 November). So the Sunday question: should a school read its students' AI conversations, and if it does, what is it reading?

Start with what a transcript is evidence of. Nicholas Soderstrom and Robert Bjork's review of the motor- and verbal-learning research separates performance, what a learner can do during practice, from learning, the change that lasts. The first is often an unreliable index of the second, and learners often mistake one for the other (Soderstrom and Bjork, 2015).

AI supplies a vivid demonstration. In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 Turkish high-school maths students, those given an unguarded GPT-4 tutor did 48% better than the control group in practice, then 17% worse on the exam once it was taken away. A hint-giving version lifted practice by 127% and left exam scores about level (Wharton, August 2024; since published in PNAS, 2025). A smooth transcript records performance with help on hand. It shows where a student got stuck. It cannot show what they will know next month.

Then consider what being read does. Help-seeking is social, and part of AI's appeal is that no one is watching. When researchers surveyed 6,960 students at four Australian universities in 2024, 49% were using AI for feedback, and one wrote that "[AI] allows me to ask stupid questions that I'm too ashamed to ask my teacher" (The Conversation, 27 August 2025). Put the teacher inside the chat and some of that safety leaves.

School monitoring shows where reading drifts. In the Center for Democracy and Technology's research, approximately half of students said they were not comfortable expressing their true thoughts and feelings online when they knew they were monitored. Among teachers at schools using monitoring software, 78% reported students flagged for disciplinary action, against 54% reporting referrals to a counsellor or other adult (CDT, August 2022). Software bought for care slides towards discipline.

The lawsuit shows the opposite failure: a private channel with no adult anywhere near it. So the answer is not to look away. It is to look narrowly, and to say so out loud.

For a school, that means three rules before any course switches on Guided Learning insights. Students hear, at the start of the task and in plain words, who can read the chat. Transcripts are read to find misconceptions, never marked or used for discipline. Safeguarding runs through the trained route the school already has, not a class teacher's dashboard. An AI use scale tells students what they may use. It does not yet tell them who is reading.

Behind You! Behind You!

A family is suing Google over a school Chromebook. Parents of a 13-year-old North Carolina seventh-grader have sued in California state court, alleging she spent months discussing suicidal thoughts with Gemini and Search's AI Mode, and that the warning came from her school, not Google (Bloomberg via Claims Journal, 5 November). Google told the New York Times it is reviewing the claims (SheKnows via Yahoo, 6 November). These are untested allegations. For any Google school, the question is which of its own alerts would fire on a chat.

Deepfakes are outrunning the rulebook. One in three of 852 US educators surveyed by the EdWeek Research Center this summer don't know whether their school has a policy on AI-generated fake images and videos; nine in 10 haven't been trained to detect or respond to deepfakes in the past two years (EdWeek via GovTech, 5 November). The same week Google paused a Vids feature that built an avatar from an uploaded image, a day after announcing it. It had no admin control, listed Education Plus, and said nothing about consent from the person pictured (Google, 2 November, updated 3 November).

Gemini now reads Forms responses. Gemini in Google Forms will analyse responses, find patterns across questions and suggest next steps, writing and running Python code so the counts come from the actual data (Google, 5 November). It comes with Education Plus and the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, reaching Scheduled Release domains from 19 November. Course evaluations and wellbeing check-ins are now one click from a model; the setting the post names is smart features and personalisation.

  • Missed, 2 November: Google's deadline to answer Senators Blumenthal and Hawley. As of Tuesday, Blumenthal's staff said they had received no response, and Google did not link that day's announcement to the letter (CT Insider via Yahoo, 4 November).
  • Midterms, read against the backlash: Democrats won in Texas, Georgia, Iowa and Ohio (Chalkbeat, 4 November). Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa campaigned against AI use by elementary students (Chalkbeat, 26 October). On preliminary results, a majority of Chicago's first fully elected school board signed a pledge to ban AI-based tools for students and staff (Chalkbeat, 5 November). The backlash has now won seats.
  • Overdue: Vault API support for Gemini exports, due within 14 days of the 22 October interface rollout; no update note by Sunday (Google, 22 October).
  • 17 November: Gems move to Settings in the Gemini app (Google).
  • Early 2027: Google's K-12 defaults take effect (SheKnows via Yahoo, 6 November), with specifics promised "in the coming months" (News & Observer via GovTech, 6 November).
  • Slow burn: No update surfaced this week on New York City's 38 products (Chalkbeat, 23 October). Search, which schools must take with its AI, is now the bigger test of feature-level control.

Sceptic's Corner

Google launched Guided Learning in Classroom alongside a stack of evidence: a blog promising "1.5x higher learning gains in just a single study session" (Google, 3 November), and a teacher guide calling the tool "proven to improve learning outcomes" (Google, Classroom guide). Follow the numbers home.

The 1.5x compares gains of 8.1 and 5.3 percentage points after half a day of studying statistics at the University of Tokyo. Google's November AI in Education report gives the same trial as a +0.14 standard deviation improvement (Google, November 2026). One framing sounds like half as much again; the other is a modest nudge from one sitting.

"Proven" rests on a pre-registered trial with 1,763 students in 12 schools in Port Loko, Sierra Leone, which DeepMind put at "roughly 1.2 to 1.7 years of typical learning progress" (Google DeepMind, 9 June). The November report rounds that to between one and two years; the guide quotes 1.7, a conversion calibrated to progress in low- and middle-income countries. What was tested was a package of teacher training, paired shared devices, structured lesson plans and technical support, in a design not built to separate the tool from the rest (EdTech Innovation Hub, June 2026). Students who started stronger gained most.

The same week, the Hechinger Report described an October study by Maryland's Jing Liu, who randomly assigned an always-available AI helper to 15 of 30 professors' courses. Students in those courses earned grades 0.11 points lower on a four-point scale, and only 15% used it (Hechinger Report, 2 November). Google's own report concedes that optional "tutor-on-the-side" AI in its ASU Study Hall and Coursera pilots failed to yield meaningful gains, largely through low adoption (Google, November 2026).

The honest reading: teacher-directed AI inside a designed lesson can help. A tab students may open is a different product, and the brochure sells the second on the strength of the first.

The line for the staffroom: "proven" means it worked somewhere, for someone, as part of something. Ask which part you are buying.
