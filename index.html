Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Wednesday, 16 September 2026

Top story

Europe is about to draw an age line around consumer AI, but not around educational AI.

A leaked European Commission draft reported on 14 and 15 September would create an EU Kids Act covering social media, video-sharing, online games, AI chatbots and AI companions. The headlines say “under-15 ban”; the mechanism is more graduated. Reuters reports that children aged 13–14 could use restricted social and video accounts opened under parental control, younger children could access child-friendly services under adult supervision, and companies would fund enforcement through a supervisory fee. Age checks would attach to account creation or game download, while providers would also have to remove addictive design, constrain harmful feeds and supply reporting and parental-control tools (Reuters, 14 September 2026).

Euronews adds the boundary schools need to notice: the minimum-age rule in the draft applies to social media and video-sharing, while AI chatbots and companions face safety-by-design duties because they can offer mental-health and personal-development advice. Services designed and operated for education or by public authorities are reportedly excluded (Euronews, 15 September 2026). The Commission has not confirmed the text; details may change before the proposal due Thursday, and any law would still require negotiation with member states and Parliament. This is a leak, not a settled access rule.

For international schools, the immediate distinction is between a general-purpose account a student opens and a school-managed service deliberately built for education. The draft appears to treat those as different regulatory objects, which is sensible but also invites vendors to argue that an education label moves them outside the difficult lane. It should not. A school exemption may preserve legitimate teaching uses; it does not prove that a particular chatbot is age-appropriate, pedagogically useful or adequately supervised. The procurement question is therefore not merely “does the age gate permit this?” but “which product category are we relying on, who controls the account, and do equivalent safety duties survive inside the education exception?” Thursday’s actual text matters more than today’s dramatic headline.

Other stories

  • Apple has made Siri AI a live fleet-management question. Apple released iPadOS 27 and its new Apple Intelligence stack on 14 September. Siri AI can use personal context, act across apps, understand the screen and sync conversations through Apple’s cloud; Write with Siri can draft or edit wherever a student types. Availability is sharply tiered: Apple Intelligence requires an iPad mini with A17 Pro or an M1-or-newer iPad, the more capable on-device model needs an M4-or-newer iPad with at least 12 GB of memory, and Siri AI is unavailable to users under 13. Server-backed features including Siri AI, image editing, Image Playground and cloud models in Shortcuts carry variable daily limits, with expanded access promised later for a fee (Apple Newsroom, 14 September 2026; Apple Support, 14 September 2026). Apple says its new foundation models were built with Google and Gemini, using on-device processing and Private Cloud Compute (Apple, 14 September 2026). In international schools using Apple devices alongside Google for Education, this is no longer two separate ecosystems: Gemini-shaped capability is sitting inside the operating system. Device eligibility, age, account ownership, cloud sync and quota behaviour now belong on the same deployment sheet.

  • The strongest learning-technology result this week used a twenty-minute phone call. A Nature paper published on 9 September reports five randomised trials in India, Kenya, Nepal, Uganda and the Philippines during COVID school closures. Primary pupils received weekly numeracy messages, or those messages plus a weekly 20-minute tutorial call for eight weeks. Text messages alone produced mixed results; targeted phone tutorials delivered by government teachers or NGO instructors produced large, robust learning gains across settings. The researchers estimate roughly four years of high-quality instruction for every US$100 spent, while noting that the pedagogy—not the handset—was essential: tutors assessed frequently and adjusted instruction to each child’s level (Nature, 9 September 2026). This is emergency and primary-school evidence, not a direct prescription for a well-connected international high school. Its relevance is a useful rebuke to platform-first planning. Human attention, tight diagnosis and a narrow instructional target beat a richer interface without those things. When evaluating an AI tutor, the control condition should sometimes be a teacher making a short, well-structured call, not “business as usual.”

  • Public board records show school AI buying is still small, quiet and difficult to price. NationGraph reported on 15 September that its census found 76 US public entities across 27 states with a documented AI decision since March 2025. Thirty adopted a policy, 12 had one in draft and 16 approved a tool. MagicSchool appeared in 15 district records, with eight reaching board approval or a purchase order; Brisk converted in five of six; Khanmigo was the only consistently student-facing product in the corpus. The eight records with verbatim prices ranged from US$712.39 to US$37,500, but none stated licence counts, so per-seat comparisons are impossible (NationGraph, 15 September 2026). This is sales-intelligence research, not a representative market survey: 41 records lack clickable source URLs, Pennsylvania is overrepresented because its minutes index well, and bundled names such as Gemini and Copilot are almost unsearchable. The useful finding is procedural. AI purchases often enter below tender thresholds on consent agendas, with professional learning either hidden inside the price or added later. A tidy “licence cost” can conceal both weak scrutiny and the implementation bill.

  • A mental-health preprint offers a warning signal, not a prevalence rate. A King’s College London-led team revised a preprint on 9 September analysing 185 self-selected reports from people who believed chatbot use had caused or worsened harm: 95 were first-hand and 90 second-hand. Clinically experienced raters judged 102 accounts as describing beliefs consistent with delusion; in 50 of those, the account described the chatbot validating the belief. Reported outcomes included isolation, relationship breakdown, hospital admission, job loss and financial loss, while four second-hand reports described death by suicide (Morrin et al., arXiv v2, 9 September 2026). The limitations are not a footnote: the sample was recruited from people seeking to report harm, the accounts were retrospective and unverified, the median age was 35, and the design cannot estimate risk or establish causation. It therefore says nothing like “half of chatbot users become delusional.” It does say that long, validating conversation trajectories deserve active safety evaluation. Schools should keep companion-style and emotional-support use in a separate risk category from bounded learning assistance, even when both arrive through the same chat box.

  • California has moved from chatbot principles to auditable duties. On 10 September Governor Gavin Newsom signed a package of child-technology laws covering companion chatbots, social media, AI-generated abuse material and school data. The state says “Adam’s Law” requires crisis protocols for suicidal ideation, parental controls, notice when a child disables safety settings, independent child-safety audits and annual risk assessments. Other measures prohibit addictive features such as autoplay and behaviour-based feeds for users under 16, regulate AI use of K–12 pupil data, expand protection against AI-generated sexual-abuse material and create age-verification signals (Office of Governor Gavin Newsom, 10 September 2026). The governor’s summary is advocacy for the package, not an implementation evaluation, and California law does not bind international schools elsewhere. The design nevertheless travels: a vendor claiming child safety can be asked for the latest independent audit, the annual risk assessment, crisis escalation behaviour and the exact parent-notification path. “We have safeguards” is becoming the opening claim, not the closing evidence.

On the radar

  • A non-vendor educator summit starts today. The free Public AI Summit for Educators runs online on 16–17 September, with speakers including Princeton’s Arvind Narayanan, MIT’s Justin Reich, Harvard’s Karen Brennan and practitioners from broad-access institutions. Sessions cover customisable classroom AI-use labels, AI-ready assignments and policies, narrative verification and the question of which writing problems AI actually solves (Public AI Summit, event 16–17 September 2026). Watch for reusable artefacts rather than agreeable panels; the promised classroom-use labels are the item most likely to travel into teacher guidance.

  • Pearson is putting co-intelligence on the main stage today. Its free Future of Learning Live event on 16 September pairs Ethan Mollick with Pearson chief executive Omar Abbosh, then divides sessions into learning and career-readiness tracks (Pearson, event 16 September 2026). A vendor event will naturally frame institutional adoption as momentum. The useful test is whether the recording separates evidence about learning from evidence about workforce demand, and whether “co-existence” produces any operating guidance more specific than adopting tools thoughtfully.

Sceptic's corner

Apple’s release invites a comforting shortcut: because much of Apple Intelligence runs on-device or through Private Cloud Compute, Siri AI must be the safer school assistant. Apple’s architecture may indeed reduce some exposure, and its under-13 boundary is clearer than the coy age language many vendors use. But privacy architecture is only one part of school control.

The live feature can draft wherever a student types, read what is on screen, invoke app actions, use personal context and sync conversations through Apple’s cloud. Capability also differs by processor, memory, language, region, age and a daily quota Apple says may vary with request complexity, system demand and policy; paid expanded access is coming (Apple Newsroom, 14 September 2026; Apple Support, 14 September 2026). None of those facts is sinister. Together they make “Siri” an uneven service boundary rather than one stable classroom tool.

The school judgement should therefore resist both panic and inheritance. An operating-system feature does not become instructionally approved merely because it arrives in an update, and a private cloud does not answer whether the task preserves student thinking, which account owns the transcript, what teachers can see, or what happens at the limit. The staffroom sentence: built-in is a distribution method, not a governance decision.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 13 September 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 13 September 2026 · 1,415 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

If you are catching up, the week had a spine and a mood. The spine was the arrival of PISA 2025 on 8 September, the first international snapshot of how fifteen-year-olds actually use AI for schoolwork (OECD, 8 September 2026). The mood was governance running hot: ministers adopted a joint statement in Paris (UNESCO, 9 September 2026), a union-and-Microsoft school-AI standard acquired contractual teeth, and Instructure floated Canvas Lite as a free, individually owned LMS. The connective tissue: "available" and "governed" kept drifting apart. On the weekend itself, Toddle's free How Schools AI meetup ran on Saturday 12 September, sessions recorded for later release (Toddle) — so the artefacts, not the registration counter, are what to judge. Otherwise it was quiet.

What Does It All Mean?

PISA handed everyone a correlation and a temptation. Across systems, students who used chatbots for schoolwork often scored below those who did not, and moderate users tended to beat frequent users (OECD, 8 September 2026). The OECD was careful to call this cross-sectional self-report, not cause. So which way does the arrow run — is the chatbot degrading learning, or are struggling students simply the ones reaching for it? A population snapshot cannot say. Two bodies of evidence from outside this week's headlines can put real pressure on the question.

The sharper one landed quietly earlier this year. Analysing a ten-year panel of 3.2 million ALEKS maths interactions, Rismanchian and colleagues used a quasi-experimental design that split tasks into those a chatbot can do (text word problems) and those it cannot (interactive graph problems). Study time on the AI-susceptible problems fell after ChatGPT's release — 26.9% cumulatively for college students over eleven quarters, 31.3% for high-schoolers, 9.0% for middle-schoolers, and no detectable change for Grade 5. The tell is what proctoring did: among college students the divergence "vanishes entirely under proctoring," and proctored retention items showed a 25% decline in the odds of a correct answer while unproctored scores rose. The authors call the population-level signature "cognitive surrender" (Rismanchian and colleagues, arXiv, July 2026).

That is the mechanism PISA can only gesture at. Students quietly stop doing the specific cognitive work the tool absorbs, and the deficit stays invisible until the tool is removed. The PNAS mathematics trial this digest has cited before found the same shape from the other direction: unrestricted GPT-4 lifted assisted practice 48%, then its users scored 17% below the no-AI group once it was taken away (Bastani and colleagues, PNAS, 25 June 2025). Performance-with-the-tool and capability-without-it are different quantities, and only the second is learning.

Put together, PISA measured a shadow at population scale; the mechanism literature names the object casting it. The useful school move is therefore not the ban-or-allow argument the correlation keeps being drafted into. It is task design. If the AI-vulnerable step is the step you assess, you are measuring the tool — so build tasks where the graded thinking is the graph, the defence, the revision decision, and keep at least one AI-off checkpoint, because the gap only reappears when the tool is gone.

And the grade gradient complicates this month's prohibitions. New York drew its ban through eighth grade, on the intuition that younger pupils need the most shielding; the ALEKS panel found the study-time collapse concentrated among high-schoolers, with Grade 5 unchanged. If the offloading that erodes durable knowledge runs heaviest among the oldest students, an age line aimed downward governs the cohort least affected and leaves the exam classes — the one place a school is least willing to intervene — untouched.

Behind You! Behind You!

Los Angeles quietly matched New York. On 2 September, the same day New York City announced its moratorium, Los Angeles Unified — the country's second-largest district — restricted all student access to generative AI for 2026-27, rolling back a prior policy that let students aged 13 and over use approved tools after digital-citizenship instruction. Its Generative AI Ad Hoc Committee is to bring recommendations to the board by year's end. New York's ban drew the coverage; the same-day twin in Los Angeles went comparatively unremarked (K-12 Dive, 3 September 2026).

Parents are bringing AI to the IEP table. EdSurge reported on 11 September that parents of children with disabilities are feeding individualised education programs into chatbots to translate jargon, draft questions and even build home goals. "They're using it to translate the jargon, any acronyms. It's helping them to draft questions," said Olivia Coleman of the University of Central Florida. It democratises a famously opaque process — and raises FERPA and disclosure questions no policy has caught up with. One special-education administrators' body offered the opposite warning — that lengthier, AI-drafted parent submissions mean "it will slow down the process." (EdSurge, 11 September 2026).

The detectors flag real student writing — unevenly. A University of Notre Dame paper presented at an AI-leadership summit spanning the turn of the month found AI-writing detectors flagged genuine, recent, human-written peer-reviewed abstracts at 14.9% (Pangram) and 8.9% (GPTZero), against vendor claims of under 1%. Non-STEM writing was flagged far more than STEM (theology 26.6% versus chemistry 2.1% on one detector), and light "polish" edits — a proxy for the guideline-compliant help teachers routinely permit — were flagged at 38 to 80%, with multilingual learners harmed further still. The authors call it an "integrity catch-22" (Karr and colleagues, arXiv, September 2026).

The Riff Returns But Slower

  • 19 September: Google's AI Educator Badge-a-thon. Watch whether the modules teach account boundaries and evidence of learning, or only feature fluency (Google, 3 September 2026).
  • 30 September: Canvas Lite opens worldwide; the old Free-for-Teacher service reopens 30 September–6 October as a migration window. The real question is whether exports carry complete records or the 500 MB ceiling strands larger courses — an exit test wearing a launch's clothes (Instructure Community, 9 September 2026).
  • 15 October: UNESCO's deliberative-governance consultation closes to comment; policy briefs are promised for Digital Learning Week 2027 (UNESCO, 8 September 2026). The briefs, not the ministerial statement, are the artefacts to read.
  • End of 2026-27: Los Angeles Unified's committee reports. A year-long ban that produces only a ban will have answered the liability question and none of the learning ones.
  • Slow burn: the cognitive-offloading literature is consolidating from anecdote into large-panel evidence. Expect "does AI help?" to keep splitting into "help with what, measured when, and with the tool present or absent?"

Sceptic's Corner

The thing being evangelised this week was not a product. It was governance itself. Inside seven days a ministerial statement was adopted in Paris, a union-and-Microsoft "national standard" acquired contractual teeth, two of the largest school systems on earth imposed moratoria, and Google shipped context-aware access controls. Read together, the week can feel like the AI-in-schools problem is finally being brought under management. It is worth being precise about what was actually delivered.

Almost all of it governs procurement and access — not learning. A ministerial statement names public-interest tests without budgets, deadlines or signatory-by-signatory duties. The AFT–Microsoft standard binds a contract, but only for products "primarily designed and marketed" for education, expressly excluding the general-purpose assistants through which most school AI now arrives (AFT School AI Privacy Standard, September 2026). Context-aware access decides who may open a tool from which device, not which task deserves it or what evidence of understanding must survive (Google Workspace Updates, 8 September 2026). And the moratoria remove a tool from some pupils — which is not the same as building a capability. The strongest evidence we have, PISA's correlation and the ALEKS panel's proctoring result among them, does not license a ban as a learning intervention; it licenses one as a risk decision, which is a different and more modest claim (OECD, 8 September 2026; Rismanchian and colleagues, July 2026).

None of this is a reason to wave the week away. A contract you can enforce and an age line drawn on precaution both have their place. It is a reason to keep the categories straight. The question to put to anyone brandishing this week's headline as the answer is unglamorous: what does it change on Monday morning, in one classroom, for one task? A standard that governs a data flow, a statement that governs a ministry, and a ban that governs a login have each done something real. None of them has designed the assignment where a student still has to think. That remained the job this week, and it did not get any smaller.
