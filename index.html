Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Tuesday, 20 October 2026

Top story

Gemini's classroom apps now have a shorter route to publication.

Google announced on 19 October that interactive apps made in the Gemini app can be exported directly into Google Sites. Export creates a new, private draft, where the creator can add material before publishing and sharing. Google lists the feature as available now on web, Android and iOS for both release tracks, covering Workspace customers and other account holders with Gemini access. There is no new administrator control: export depends on Sites being enabled and the user having permission to create and edit sites; existing Sites and Drive policies govern the result (Google Workspace Updates, 19 October).

The private draft is the reassuring detail. A teacher's experiment does not become a public resource merely because they export it. The consequential detail is that creation and distribution now sit much closer together. A functioning simulation can move from a conversation into the familiar school website environment without someone copying code or rebuilding it. That removes a real obstacle for teachers who can describe a useful learning interaction but cannot package an application.

For Ed Tech Coaches and teachers in international schools, the interesting example is a small model that exposes a misconception: change a variable, predict the outcome, then explain the discrepancy. Publication gives that model a stable home alongside the lesson instructions. But a polished page and a responsive slider prove very little about whether the underlying relationship is correct. A graph can be beautifully wrong, and a quiz can reward an answer its author never intended. The relevant review is disciplinary as well as technical.

My read is that this moves generated learning resources into the same editorial category as a worksheet or shared assessment: identifiable author, intelligible purpose, checked examples, and an audience chosen deliberately. The new export path makes that standard easier to apply because there is a draft to inspect. It also makes the old excuse that an experiment is only a chat harder to sustain once students can open it from a class resource page.

Other stories

  • A new OECD chapter asks schools to evaluate the teacher and the AI together. Inge Molenaar's contribution to Co-constructing Visions for the Future of Teaching, published on 19 October, argues that treating an AI product as the sole cause of learning gains overlooks the teacher who deploys it. Her proposed unit of analysis is the combined system. She cites research in which 49% of learner-facing AI solutions incorporated scientific knowledge about learning, and 33% of teacher-facing solutions drew on scientific understanding of teaching. The latter reference is explicitly under review; neither percentage is a fresh classroom outcome from this publication (OECD, 19 October). This is a future-vision essay, so its strength is the question it sharpens. A tutor that works with expert teacher intervention may fail when deployed as unattended homework; an ordinary tool may work well because the teacher designs excellent follow-up. Procurement conversations tend to ask whether the product works. The harder, more transferable question is what the adults had to do for it to work, and whether that work is affordable and teachable across a department. An evidence claim that omits those conditions leaves a school guessing about the intervention it is actually buying.

  • Researchers are trying to stop simulated students from being implausibly good students. A preprint submitted on 14 October by Yilin Jiang and colleagues introduces HACHIMI-II, which models a learner's history and predicts their next action instead of simply asking a capable language model to impersonate a student. The authors argue that generic role-play lets the model's own competence leak into the simulation, flattening differences between learners and losing their characteristic mistakes. Across two dialogue datasets, they report reproducing errors with matching knowledge type, location and mechanism 1.8–5.1 times as often as the strongest baseline (arXiv, submitted 14 October). This measures fidelity to existing student behaviour, not learning improvement, and the work is a preprint. Its practical importance lies upstream of classroom adoption: a tutor rehearsed against unusually cooperative, knowledgeable artificial pupils can look better than it will with children. Modelling authentic errors is a useful way to make that rehearsal harder. It still cannot establish that a child learned, felt safe, or understood a hint. The school-facing question behind the technical result is excellent: when a supplier says its tutor has been tested, how much of the testing involved actual learners, and how much involved another model playing one?

  • Anthropic has published a cheaper way for Claude to flag its own potentially harmful conversations. Its 16 October safeguards paper describes a short assessment after a response, using the conversation the model has already processed. For Claude Fable 5, Anthropic reports catching 78% of conversations flagged by a stronger prompted classifier, and 94% of its high-priority flags, at a 1% false-positive rate. It estimates less than 2% additional inference compute across its production API traffic distribution. These are comparisons with another automated classifier, not percentages of real-world harms prevented. The assessments feed offline investigation, alongside other safeguards; Anthropic explicitly says the technique is insufficient against a dedicated attacker (Anthropic Safeguards, 16 October). There is a useful procurement distinction here between detecting a concerning exchange and stopping one before a student sees it. Cheap, broad monitoring could help a vendor notice new misuse patterns that narrow filters miss. It does not turn retrospective review into immediate protection. A school's reading of a safety score needs the sequence: what was detected, against which reference, when the flag arrived, and who could act. A very high percentage can answer one of those questions while leaving the other three untouched.

  • Jisc's Google partnership joins staff development to a buying framework. Announced on 16 October, the two-year collaboration covers workshops, webinars, leadership engagement and practitioner communities around tools including Gemini and NotebookLM. It also includes a dedicated reseller framework for Google Workspace for Education. Jisc says it is assembling an initial cohort across institution types, sizes and levels of AI maturity, with collaborative evidence gathering intended to inform later guidance (Jisc, 16 October). This is a higher and further education initiative, not a new entitlement for international schools. The pattern travels nonetheless: adoption support and procurement are being packaged together. That can reduce the fragmentation of isolated training sessions followed by separate licensing negotiations. It also means the evidence programme sits beside a commercial route for the same supplier. Neither fact discredits the programme, but they make the eventual evaluation design consequential. The most useful findings would identify where the tools added little, what implementation cost, and which practices transferred to less experienced institutions. A collection of confident early adopters describing their favourite use cases would answer a much easier question. For schools watching from outside the arrangement, those less flattering findings may be the most valuable export.

On the radar

  • Gemini in Sheets has reached iOS, with the education add-on specified. Google's 19 October announcement says users can ask questions about spreadsheet data, obtain analytical insights and create charts. The rollout began on 15 October for both release tracks and can take up to 15 days. Google AI Pro for Education is listed; this is not a statement that every Education account receives it. Administration follows existing Gemini enablement at domain or organisational-unit level (Google Workspace Updates, 19 October). The change to watch is where analysis happens: a phone makes a quick interpretation more convenient, while leaving the need to inspect the range, formula and missing data exactly where it was.

  • UNESCO's latest AI governance account puts data quality beside access. Published on 19 October, its report describes a Strategy Lab held on 11 September, rather than a new meeting yesterday. The collaborative's six-pillar discussion covered content, capacity, infrastructure, cost, data and leadership. Its data questions included traceability and whether AI improves learning rather than merely increasing speed. The outputs feed an AI workstream and maturity questionnaire, with discussion continuing at the Global Education Coalition meeting in March 2027 (UNESCO, 19 October). The next useful artefact will be the revised questionnaire: that is where a broad commitment can become something a school can actually answer and challenge.

Sceptic's corner

The easiest reaction to Google's Sites export is to demand another approval step. There is a respectable case for it: generated applications can contain errors, and making distribution easier increases the chance that an unchecked resource reaches a class. But universal approval queues have a cost too. If every small interactive demands a specialist review, the school may recreate the technical bottleneck the feature just removed.

The distinction is between inspecting a resource and pretending one person can certify everything about it. A subject teacher can test whether a model demonstrates the intended concept. A technical colleague can inspect the sharing and behaviour. Neither can establish learning value by looking at the finished page. Molenaar's OECD essay makes the better argument: the teacher's use belongs inside the thing being evaluated.

That changes the standard of success. An imperfect-looking interactive that makes students predict, argue and revise may earn its place. An elegant one that lets them click through to the expected answer may not. Those are hypotheses about a lesson, requiring observation, not properties of an export button.

The private draft in Google's announcement supplies a sensible pause. The danger is using that pause only to admire the output. The valuable scrutiny concerns what students will have to think about after they open it.

The Week That Was

Over the past ten days, three things moved together: the frontier went behind paywalls, free tiers moved down, and Australia's national evidence body told schools to slow down.

Background, Wednesday 7 to Friday 9 October: OpenAI put GPT-6 with "Intelligent UI", answers built as interactive diagrams, calculators and forms, into paid ChatGPT tiers on 7 October, with Free and Go users following the next day on the smaller GPT-6 Luna (OpenAI, 7 October). On 9 October Google went the other way. Personal Gemini accounts without a paid plan lost Flash and Pro as choices; Auto now sends most of their prompts to Flash-Lite (9to5Google, 3 October; Google's help page). Google scopes the change to personal accounts. For schools, a student's personal Gemini just got weaker, which changes how it compares with a school account.

Tuesday 13 October: Gemini 4 Argon, gated since 30 September, reached the public, or the paying part of it: Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers in the Gemini app, and the paid API tier at an introductory $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens until 30 November (Google, 13 October). Gemini for business followed on 14 October (Google Cloud, 14 October). None of the announcements mentions Education editions.

Same day: Google delayed Workspace skills "until further notice", while Gemini-app skills began rolling out to users of all ages, finishing by mid-November. On the second point Google is plain: there are no admin controls for skills in the Gemini app (Google, 13 October). Whatever students build there, nobody can switch off centrally.

Wednesday 14 October: The Australian Education Research Organisation published two explainers on AI in schools. Its five considerations are evidence-based teaching, monitoring risks to learning, clear expectations, assessment, and a culturally responsive lens. Its candid note on the evidence: mostly recent, short-term, exploratory and conducted outside Australia (AERO, 14 October). Chief executive Jenny Donovan went further with ABC News: use AI sparingly, avoid it in the first three years of primary school, and introduce it in high school (ABC News, 14 October). The written guidance asks only for greater caution before secondary. The bright line came in the interview.

Also Wednesday: the attorneys general of Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho, Montana, Iowa, Kansas, West Virginia and Nebraska filed separate lawsuits against OpenAI, alleging inadequate safeguards for minors and other vulnerable users and citing its rogue agents. They want profits made while minors were harmed handed back, but named no damages figure (Bloomberg Law, 14 October; AP via Fortune, 17 October). OpenAI says it has built age-specific protections and wants industry-wide rules. These are allegations, not findings, but discovery is where vendor safety claims finally get tested.

What Does It All Mean?

AERO's headline word was "sparingly". Is quantity the right dial for a school to turn?

AERO's own document suggests not. Its stated concern is whether AI use supports or displaces the thinking learning requires, not whether it improves immediate performance (AERO). That is a question about manner, and the best large dataset agrees.

David Strömberg, Victor Lei and Yanhui Wu followed 26,811 Chinese secondary students in grades 7–12 over 30 months (CEPR discussion paper, 2026). Adopting generative AI raised homework scores by 18% and cut completion time by 30%. Monthly exam scores then fell by 20% within six months. The losses were concentrated in roughly 80% of users whose pattern looked like outsourcing: very short completion times with high scores. Users who kept their homework time close to non-users' saw small losses. Same tool, same access, different outcome.

Dose matters less cleanly than the word implies. A meta-analysis in Educational Psychology Review, published 3 October, pooled 47 randomised trials (Ren and colleagues). Effects on academic outcomes were large (Hedges' g = 0.68). Effects on cognitive outcomes were small and not significant (g = 0.17). Academic gains peaked at about 22.79 hours, then levelled off with a slight decline. These were designed interventions, not open chatbot use, and the gap between those two numbers is the real warning: scores can move while thinking doesn't.

Then there is how little anyone knows. A Stanford SCALE chapter by Cristina Barnard, Lily Fesler and Susanna Loeb reports that a review of more than 800 papers found only 20 high-quality causal studies. None were US K–12 studies of student-facing tools, and many measured performance while students still had the AI or shortly after (SCALE). AERO's evidence note cites the same Fesler review. The age line, then, is caution reasonably applied to an evidence gap, not a finding.

For a high school, AERO's advice points towards introducing AI deliberately rather than rationing minutes. The cheapest monitor is the one Strömberg's data supplies: high-quality work arriving in implausibly little time. Teachers already notice this. The practical move is to treat it as a learning signal rather than an integrity charge, and to follow a tool-assisted task with a short unaided check.

Behind You! Behind You!

Anthropic opened a Singapore office and signed an MOU with the Ministry of Digital Development and Information. The 14 October announcement covers safety evaluation, research on AI adoption and responsible use across the public and private sectors. Education is not mentioned. Singapore ranks second of 121 countries for Claude.ai usage relative to population, and more than 1,500 GovTech staff use Claude Code. Governments are now signing with labs directly. Whether school-sector deals follow is the thing to watch.

Anthropic gave six METR evaluators near-employee access. Under the 15 October arrangement, METR receives company hardware, office space and internal tools, and Anthropic cannot veto its publications, only require redaction of predefined categories such as customer data. It describes this as the highest of three access tiers. For schools taking vendors' safety claims on trust, "who checked, with what access, and can they publish?" is now a reasonable question to ask of every supplier.

OpenAI marked Brazil's Teachers' Day with Study Mode and a Portuguese educator course. The 15 October post launches AI for Educators in Brazilian Portuguese on OpenAI Academy. Its own model lesson is telling: after using Study Mode on evaporation, each student writes an explanation and a discussion question without referring back to the conversation. Even the vendor's exemplar ends with the tool switched off.

Gmail's AI Inbox reached paid Workspace users with no admin switch. The 15 October update lists AI Pro for Education among eligible editions, but only for US users with EN-US as their language. Rapid Release began 12 October; Scheduled Release starts 26 October. It is the week's second Google feature arriving without a control, after Gemini-app skills.

The Riff Returns But Slower

  • 26 October: Gmail AI Inbox begins on Scheduled Release domains (Google, 15 October).
  • 17 November: Gems move to Settings in the Gemini app; education Gems stop working no earlier than 1 June 2027. Google's transition help page still shows the old Workspace-skills dates (Google).
  • Mid-November: Gemini-app skills finish rolling out; Workspace skills await a revised timeline (Google, 13 October).
  • 30 November: Argon's introductory API price ends, rising to $4 and $20 per million input and output tokens (Google, 13 October).
  • 1 December: Microsoft 365 Copilot becomes purchasable without a base Microsoft 365 subscription, including for education customers (Microsoft, 14 October).
  • Slow burn: the eight state suits, and whether more states join, as the attorneys general said they might (Bloomberg Law, 14 October).

Sceptic's Corner

Two announcements this window used the same word. OpenAI's headline was "GPT-6 and Intelligent UI for everyone". Google's page title was "Gemini 4 Argon is now available for everyone". Both are true for a definition of everyone.

Argon in the Gemini app means AI Pro or Ultra; AI Pro costs $19.99 a month (9to5Google). Free personal accounts moved down to Flash-Lite the same week. ChatGPT's free tier gets GPT-6, but on Luna rather than Sol (OpenAI). The gap between a student who pays and one who doesn't widened while the headlines said the opposite.

Now the strongest case against the obvious alarm. If Strömberg's data is right, the paying student has mostly bought a faster way to skip the thinking. Frontier capability is not the scarce input in a Grade 10 essay. Effort is.

So the equity problem is real, but it isn't really about models. It is about which students have an adult-designed reason to use any model well. A school account is not an equaliser just because it exists. It is one only when the tasks around it require the student to think.
