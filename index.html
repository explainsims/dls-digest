Latest brief

Weekday read of the ed-tech beat — what has landed recently, hype stripped, school-relevance asked.

Friday, 18 September 2026

Top story

Google’s new classroom generator passes its own checks on the first try just 3.5 per cent of the time.

Google Research unveiled Learning Interactives on 17 September: a system that turns a teacher’s topic, grade level and intended objective into a playable STEM simulation or mini-game. The teacher approves the generated learning objectives; Gemini then constructs five progressive challenges, the interface, hints, feedback and a worked solution. A public library now contains more than 30 teacher-reviewed examples across mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, computer science and earth science, while Workspace for Education schools can apply for a pilot (Google Research, 17 September 2026).

The technical report is refreshingly less theatrical. A one-shot generated interface passed Google’s visual, mechanical, telemetry and solution checks only 3.5% of the time. Ten critique-and-repair rounds lifted that to 69.3%. In a usability study, 12 US STEM teachers submitted 36 requests; Google withheld three outputs as inadequate, and the remainder averaged above 8.1 on its usability scale. Two independent teachers then rated outputs from 40 further prompts, accepting 86%—with physics and chemistry stronger than biology (Google Research technical report, 16 September 2026). Those are promising production and review figures, not evidence that pupils learned more.

For an international school, the interesting object is the pipeline, not the gallery. A teacher-authored objective becomes generated code, is screened by automated critics, selected from several candidates and only then reaches a learner. That is much closer to a governable Ed Tech Coach prototype than asking a model for a game and trusting the first colourful result. But Google still generated five candidates to choose one and has not tested learning efficacy in real classrooms. The sensible pilot question is therefore whether a generated interactive produces transferable understanding after the screen is closed—not whether it looks active while a pupil drags the slider.

Other stories

  • McGraw Hill bought an AI coach that listens to lessons. The publisher announced on 17 September that it has acquired TeachFX, which records classroom audio on a phone or laptop, uses diarisation to analyse interactions, and returns feedback on practices such as questioning and prompting pupils to explain their thinking. School leaders can receive aggregated instructional trends; a separate MCP integration can turn TeachFX feedback into lesson plans through Claude for Teachers. McGraw Hill places the deal inside an estimated US$6 billion professional-learning market and says several independent studies found better average pupil performance and stronger teacher morale, but it did not name those studies in the release or disclose the transaction terms (McGraw Hill, 17 September 2026). The pedagogical idea is plausible; the governance object is still a recording of children and teachers crossing into analysis, aggregation and potentially another model. Consent, retention, leader visibility and whether coaching data can become evaluation data need answers before the microphone turns on.

  • Gemini Notebook has become a voice tutor, lecture recorder and quiz engine at once. Google’s 15 September update adds real-time spoken conversations grounded in notebook sources in nearly 100 languages, a mobile audio recorder due from the week of 21 September, interactive reports combining summaries with quizzes and flashcards, and shareable one-minute video overviews in more than 80 languages. The entitlement details are uneven: voice begins with AI Ultra users aged 18 and over before broader release, while source notes and editing are supported for under-18 users in English; interactive reports and new quiz formats roll out to all users over the next few weeks (Google, 15 September 2026). In a Google-first international school, the practical boundary has moved from a research notebook to captured classroom speech and adaptive practice. Before calling that one workflow, separate who may record, who owns the recording, which account retains it, and whether quiz performance is visible evidence or merely a private rehearsal signal.

  • NUS is building seven education tools around classroom problems rather than one universal assistant. The National University of Singapore described the programme on 15 September; three tools are already in course pilots. Brainstorm uses Socratic prompts during topic and hypothesis formation, Verify asks students to compare several plausible AI answers and justify which is strongest, and Engage captures classroom discussion so instructors can revisit themes, gaps and questions. The pilots span science, business, psychology and social-science courses, and the teams combine faculty, academic developers, education technologists and IT staff (NUS, 15 September 2026). NUS reports designs and early use, not learning outcomes, and Engage raises the familiar question of what happens to recorded speech. Still, the grain size travels well: one cognitive bottleneck, one deliberately bounded behaviour and one named course is a sturdier prototype brief than adding a general chatbot to every subject and looking for uses afterwards.

  • A plausible AI explanation may have little to do with the score it explains. A Technical University of Munich summary published on 16 September revisits an ACL study from July that analysed 6,005 fifteen-minute segments drawn from more than 1,600 elementary mathematics lesson transcripts. Fine-tuned language models scored teacher feedback more accurately than prompted general models, with average error around one point on a seven-point scale, but clustered heavily in the middle and never used the extreme scores in one leading model. More troublingly, the sentences that LLMs named as decisive usually changed the score only slightly when removed, while SHAP-selected sentences caused larger, more stable changes across model types; the two approaches overlapped on only about one or two of their top ten sentences per transcript (TUM, 16 September 2026; ACL paper, July 2026). This was one rubric dimension on text transcripts, not a full account of teaching. It is nevertheless a clean warning for feedback dashboards: a polished rationale is another model output to test, not an audit trail.

  • The University of California has put AI inside a formal risk appetite. In its first report to the Board of Regents, published 16 September, UC’s system-wide steering committee organised work across five areas and said the university was the first to address AI explicitly in a Digital Risk Appetite statement—a declaration of how much technology risk it will accept and where. The ten-campus system is also seeking coordinated arrangements with multiple model and cloud providers to avoid single-vendor dependence; Google has supplied more than US$32.7 million across 61 UC projects (University of California, 16 September 2026). This is the institution reporting its own first and its own programme, not an external assessment. The useful move for a school is smaller: write down where experimentation is welcome, where human review is mandatory and where the risk is unacceptable. A list of principles says what an organisation values; a risk appetite says what it will actually permit.

On the radar

  • Cornell’s AI pilots are allowed to use less AI. The university announced on 17 September that discipline-based teams of three to five faculty will redesign a core skill or course sequence for spring 2027 through spring 2028, supported by embedded graduate researchers and student fellows. Proposals may increase AI use or deliberately avoid it, and results are due in 2028; a US$2 million gift supports the wider initiative (Cornell, 17 September 2026). Watch whether the evaluations publish baseline measures, failed designs and student-learning results, not just funded showcases.

  • The EU’s leaked child-safety plan became a formal proposal. The Commission adopted the KIDS Act proposal on 17 September, confirming safety-by-design duties for services offering social media, video, games, AI companions or chatbots to minors. Embedded chatbots could not switch on automatically, and companions or chatbots could not simulate relationships likely to foster emotional dependency; very large platforms would submit plans for independent audit, with a proposed 90-day enforcement route (European Commission announcement and official explainer, 17 September 2026). It now goes to Parliament and Council; watch the final treatment of education services rather than treating the proposal as law.

  • Singapore has published an implementation gate beside its AI principles. The Ministry of Education’s Student Learning Space page, updated 10 September, frames school AI through agency, inclusivity, fairness and safety, then directs teachers running a small local trial to an implementation guide and checklist (Singapore MOE, updated 10 September 2026). The checklist itself sits behind the ministry intranet, so the public page cannot show its teeth or establish how demanding the gate really is. Watch for a public version or an equivalent school checklist that turns the four nouns into owners, controls and evidence requirements.

Sceptic's corner

The claim worth testing is that a generated simulation is active learning because the pupil manipulates it. Google’s design is considerably better than a decorative chatbot: the teacher approves objectives, difficulty rises in stages, hints preserve some struggle, feedback responds to action and the system tests its own code repeatedly. That is a serious attempt to build cognitive friction back into a technology usually designed to remove it.

But activity is not the same as thought. A learner can drag sliders, clear levels and follow hints while learning the interface rather than the concept. Google’s own results measure generation success, expert acceptance and teacher-rated usability. They do not yet measure retention, transfer to an unfamiliar problem, misconception repair or what pupils can explain without the simulation. The 86% acceptance rate therefore tells us that many outputs looked usable to reviewers, not that 86% taught effectively.

The usable judgement is neither reject the tool nor infer learning from interactivity. Pilot one simulation against a conventional alternative; collect a short pre-task, delayed transfer task and student explanation; inspect wrong paths as carefully as completion; and count the teacher time needed to verify the objective, model and feedback. If the learning survives away from the screen, the generator has earned another round. If only the interaction looks lively, it has generated an activity—not evidence.

Latest weekly digest

Filed Sunday, 13 September 2026. Sunday-paper read of the week — synthesis, not a re-read of the dailies.

Week ending Sunday, 13 September 2026 · 1,415 words · ~6 min read

The Week That Was

If you are catching up, the week had a spine and a mood. The spine was the arrival of PISA 2025 on 8 September, the first international snapshot of how fifteen-year-olds actually use AI for schoolwork (OECD, 8 September 2026). The mood was governance running hot: ministers adopted a joint statement in Paris (UNESCO, 9 September 2026), a union-and-Microsoft school-AI standard acquired contractual teeth, and Instructure floated Canvas Lite as a free, individually owned LMS. The connective tissue: "available" and "governed" kept drifting apart. On the weekend itself, Toddle's free How Schools AI meetup ran on Saturday 12 September, sessions recorded for later release (Toddle) — so the artefacts, not the registration counter, are what to judge. Otherwise it was quiet.

What Does It All Mean?

PISA handed everyone a correlation and a temptation. Across systems, students who used chatbots for schoolwork often scored below those who did not, and moderate users tended to beat frequent users (OECD, 8 September 2026). The OECD was careful to call this cross-sectional self-report, not cause. So which way does the arrow run — is the chatbot degrading learning, or are struggling students simply the ones reaching for it? A population snapshot cannot say. Two bodies of evidence from outside this week's headlines can put real pressure on the question.

The sharper one landed quietly earlier this year. Analysing a ten-year panel of 3.2 million ALEKS maths interactions, Rismanchian and colleagues used a quasi-experimental design that split tasks into those a chatbot can do (text word problems) and those it cannot (interactive graph problems). Study time on the AI-susceptible problems fell after ChatGPT's release — 26.9% cumulatively for college students over eleven quarters, 31.3% for high-schoolers, 9.0% for middle-schoolers, and no detectable change for Grade 5. The tell is what proctoring did: among college students the divergence "vanishes entirely under proctoring," and proctored retention items showed a 25% decline in the odds of a correct answer while unproctored scores rose. The authors call the population-level signature "cognitive surrender" (Rismanchian and colleagues, arXiv, July 2026).

That is the mechanism PISA can only gesture at. Students quietly stop doing the specific cognitive work the tool absorbs, and the deficit stays invisible until the tool is removed. The PNAS mathematics trial this digest has cited before found the same shape from the other direction: unrestricted GPT-4 lifted assisted practice 48%, then its users scored 17% below the no-AI group once it was taken away (Bastani and colleagues, PNAS, 25 June 2025). Performance-with-the-tool and capability-without-it are different quantities, and only the second is learning.

Put together, PISA measured a shadow at population scale; the mechanism literature names the object casting it. The useful school move is therefore not the ban-or-allow argument the correlation keeps being drafted into. It is task design. If the AI-vulnerable step is the step you assess, you are measuring the tool — so build tasks where the graded thinking is the graph, the defence, the revision decision, and keep at least one AI-off checkpoint, because the gap only reappears when the tool is gone.

And the grade gradient complicates this month's prohibitions. New York drew its ban through eighth grade, on the intuition that younger pupils need the most shielding; the ALEKS panel found the study-time collapse concentrated among high-schoolers, with Grade 5 unchanged. If the offloading that erodes durable knowledge runs heaviest among the oldest students, an age line aimed downward governs the cohort least affected and leaves the exam classes — the one place a school is least willing to intervene — untouched.

Behind You! Behind You!

Los Angeles quietly matched New York. On 2 September, the same day New York City announced its moratorium, Los Angeles Unified — the country's second-largest district — restricted all student access to generative AI for 2026-27, rolling back a prior policy that let students aged 13 and over use approved tools after digital-citizenship instruction. Its Generative AI Ad Hoc Committee is to bring recommendations to the board by year's end. New York's ban drew the coverage; the same-day twin in Los Angeles went comparatively unremarked (K-12 Dive, 3 September 2026).

Parents are bringing AI to the IEP table. EdSurge reported on 11 September that parents of children with disabilities are feeding individualised education programs into chatbots to translate jargon, draft questions and even build home goals. "They're using it to translate the jargon, any acronyms. It's helping them to draft questions," said Olivia Coleman of the University of Central Florida. It democratises a famously opaque process — and raises FERPA and disclosure questions no policy has caught up with. One special-education administrators' body offered the opposite warning — that lengthier, AI-drafted parent submissions mean "it will slow down the process." (EdSurge, 11 September 2026).

The detectors flag real student writing — unevenly. A University of Notre Dame paper presented at an AI-leadership summit spanning the turn of the month found AI-writing detectors flagged genuine, recent, human-written peer-reviewed abstracts at 14.9% (Pangram) and 8.9% (GPTZero), against vendor claims of under 1%. Non-STEM writing was flagged far more than STEM (theology 26.6% versus chemistry 2.1% on one detector), and light "polish" edits — a proxy for the guideline-compliant help teachers routinely permit — were flagged at 38 to 80%, with multilingual learners harmed further still. The authors call it an "integrity catch-22" (Karr and colleagues, arXiv, September 2026).

The Riff Returns But Slower

  • 19 September: Google's AI Educator Badge-a-thon. Watch whether the modules teach account boundaries and evidence of learning, or only feature fluency (Google, 3 September 2026).
  • 30 September: Canvas Lite opens worldwide; the old Free-for-Teacher service reopens 30 September–6 October as a migration window. The real question is whether exports carry complete records or the 500 MB ceiling strands larger courses — an exit test wearing a launch's clothes (Instructure Community, 9 September 2026).
  • 15 October: UNESCO's deliberative-governance consultation closes to comment; policy briefs are promised for Digital Learning Week 2027 (UNESCO, 8 September 2026). The briefs, not the ministerial statement, are the artefacts to read.
  • End of 2026-27: Los Angeles Unified's committee reports. A year-long ban that produces only a ban will have answered the liability question and none of the learning ones.
  • Slow burn: the cognitive-offloading literature is consolidating from anecdote into large-panel evidence. Expect "does AI help?" to keep splitting into "help with what, measured when, and with the tool present or absent?"

Sceptic's Corner

The thing being evangelised this week was not a product. It was governance itself. Inside seven days a ministerial statement was adopted in Paris, a union-and-Microsoft "national standard" acquired contractual teeth, two of the largest school systems on earth imposed moratoria, and Google shipped context-aware access controls. Read together, the week can feel like the AI-in-schools problem is finally being brought under management. It is worth being precise about what was actually delivered.

Almost all of it governs procurement and access — not learning. A ministerial statement names public-interest tests without budgets, deadlines or signatory-by-signatory duties. The AFT–Microsoft standard binds a contract, but only for products "primarily designed and marketed" for education, expressly excluding the general-purpose assistants through which most school AI now arrives (AFT School AI Privacy Standard, September 2026). Context-aware access decides who may open a tool from which device, not which task deserves it or what evidence of understanding must survive (Google Workspace Updates, 8 September 2026). And the moratoria remove a tool from some pupils — which is not the same as building a capability. The strongest evidence we have, PISA's correlation and the ALEKS panel's proctoring result among them, does not license a ban as a learning intervention; it licenses one as a risk decision, which is a different and more modest claim (OECD, 8 September 2026; Rismanchian and colleagues, July 2026).

None of this is a reason to wave the week away. A contract you can enforce and an age line drawn on precaution both have their place. It is a reason to keep the categories straight. The question to put to anyone brandishing this week's headline as the answer is unglamorous: what does it change on Monday morning, in one classroom, for one task? A standard that governs a data flow, a statement that governs a ministry, and a ban that governs a login have each done something real. None of them has designed the assignment where a student still has to think. That remained the job this week, and it did not get any smaller.
