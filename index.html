Wednesday, 28 October 2026

Top story

AI can remove the disagreement that group work was meant to teach.

The Digital Education Council and Pearson's 27 October public announcement of Designing AI for Collaborative Learning describes students arriving with similar AI-prepared ideas and asking AI to settle disagreements instead of working through them together. The report was already the subject of a 29 September member briefing; yesterday's announcement is the fresh development, rather than evidence that the research was conducted this week (DEC announcement, earlier briefing).

The public resource page describes an analysis of more than 80 global case studies, mapping 15 approaches against seven mechanisms through which collaborative learning works. One example puts AI in the role of an opposing negotiator, with students developing their response together. The page also recognises the cost: students may practise answering objections while outsourcing the generation of objections. The complete report requires registration or member access; the accessible material does not establish the prevalence of these behaviours across schools, or a causal effect on learning (report overview).

The useful distinction for international schools is between dividing a product among several people and needing several people to think. A group can submit something polished without anyone having to change their mind. That possibility predates AI; a private adviser available to every participant makes it easier to miss. A history discussion in which students must reconcile conflicting interpretations has a different learning purpose from a team simply assembling slides. The same permission to use AI could have different consequences in each.

My read is that collaboration belongs inside the assessment description. What must students explain to one another? Where must they weigh competing evidence? Which decision must they own together? Those questions give the teacher something more revealing to listen for than equal speaking time. A smoothly completed task can be a success, while a smoothly avoided argument can be the lesson that never happened.

Other stories

  • Gemini can now change the notebook it previously only helped you think about. Google's 23 October announcement makes Gemini Notebook a Connected App inside Gemini: users can create, edit or delete notebooks and sources, save notes, and query existing material through chat. Gemini can also select the connection automatically for relevant prompts. Google lists it as available now to Workspace and personal accounts. Organisational access requires both Gemini and Gemini Notebook to be enabled; the connection is on by default, while individual users in the European Economic Area start with it off (Google Workspace Updates). For a teacher's research collection, the change is from retrieval to maintenance: an instruction can alter the evidence base that a later answer will use. That is useful when adding a new curriculum source, and consequential when a casual request removes an old one. An Ed Tech Coach demonstrating the feature could show the notebook before and after the conversation, including which sources actually changed. The interesting competence is understanding the resulting collection, rather than remembering the command. Availability in a vendor's release note also leaves the school's actual account settings and age restrictions to be checked; it does not establish that every student can use every connected action today.

  • Apple's creative apps are getting assistants and an external-agent doorway, with November on the calendar. Apple's 27 October Creator Studio announcement adds contextual assistants in Keynote, Pages and Numbers, plus Model Context Protocol support through which external AI tools can read content, edit it and perform actions. Keynote also gains an open, JSON-based interchange format, .keyx. Updates are due in November, rather than already installed. The assistants are beta features in US English on Apple Intelligence-capable devices running version 27 of the relevant operating system; MCP requires macOS 27 (Apple Newsroom). For an Apple school, the quieter development is that a presentation becomes something another tool can inspect and manipulate, instead of a final file carried between applications. That could help adapt a teaching resource, but the quality question remains specific: did the revised deck preserve the mathematical notation, source attribution and intended sequence? Those are sensible checks for a classroom example when the update arrives. The announcement describes capability and compatibility, without demonstrating that an assistant can make those editorial decisions reliably. A beautifully rearranged explanation can still rearrange the reasoning out of it.

  • A new study tested whether an AI-supported programme left participants able to perform without AI. Yuda He and colleagues' Scientific Reports article, published in accepted form on 26 October, compared a structured training programme with career guidance matched for topics, duration and facilitator contact. Twelve intact cohorts of education graduates in Guangdong, 186 people in total, were assigned as whole groups. Blinded experts rated recruitment materials produced under supervision without AI. Among the 168 with post-test data, the adjusted difference favoured the AI-supported programme by 5.38 points on a 100-point scale; the reported standardised effect was 0.60 (accepted article). There are substantial qualifications: raters had no prespecified rubric, the allocation was by intact cohort, and the design cannot separate AI's contribution from structured practice and feedback. This is graduate preparation, not secondary-school learning. Still, its outcome choice is worth carrying into teacher discussion. An assisted practice session and an unassisted performance answer different questions; a better-looking draft during the session answers neither by itself. A school trial could define the independent performance first, then judge whether the support helps learners reach it. The article is an early accepted version, subject to further editing, and offers a promising association with that testable shape rather than a general endorsement of AI training.

  • A four-part vocabulary challenges the single AI strategy box. In 26 October EDUCAUSE Review, Szymon Machajewski distinguishes AI supporting educational operations, AI used deliberately in teaching and learning, AI as part of a discipline's professional practice, and general AI literacy. His argument is that progress in one layer does not establish readiness in the others: staff might automate routine work and teach prompting while students remain poorly prepared for how AI changes their field (original analysis). This is a proposed framework illustrated through higher education, rather than an evaluated intervention. It nevertheless supplies a useful question for international schools' professional learning: which capability would a session actually develop? Learning to format resources more quickly does not demonstrate that a teacher can design an effective AI-supported learning activity. Equally, an excellent lesson about hallucinations does not settle what counts as competent AI use in design, programming or journalism. Secondary schools need not turn subjects into job training to recognise that distinction. An authentic example from a field can expose what its practitioners must still know, verify and take responsibility for. The framework earns its keep if it makes those gaps visible; four renamed columns in a strategy document would be a rather efficient way to miss the point.

  • Buffalo is funding the work between broad AI principles and advice for young children's learning. The University at Buffalo announced an additional $500,000 from the US Institute of Education Sciences on 27 October for its Center for Early Literacy and Responsible AI. The work will connect state and regional agencies with researchers, identify evidence gaps, and develop practical guidance for educators and families. Planned collaborators include U-GAIN Reading and the National AI Institute for Exceptional Education; a convening is planned for 2027–28 (university announcement). These are funded intentions, not a finished guidance document or proof of improved literacy. The useful signal for a whole-school conversation is the explicit combination of child development, literacy, inclusive education and family concerns. A policy written around an older student's chatbot use will not automatically answer what an adult should do with an AI-generated reading text for a six-year-old. Vocabulary, cultural assumptions, decoding difficulty and the adult's mediation all matter to that example. The announcement supplies a research programme to watch, while leaving those judgements with educators today. Waiting for evidence need not mean waiting to ask precise questions about the reading experience a child is actually being offered.

On the radar

  • Apple's M6 MacBook Pro has a delivery date; classroom value still needs its own workload. The 27 October announcement sets preorders for 28 October and availability for 6 November. The 14-inch model starts with 16GB unified memory, configurable to 32GB, and Apple advertises faster local AI processing using its own benchmarks (Apple). Those comparisons do not establish a school upgrade case. For media or computing departments, the useful follow-up would be a representative project on the machines already in use: enough memory for the intended model or editing task, tolerable waiting time, and sustained work through a normal lesson. The launch calendar can inform planning without setting the replacement calendar.

Sceptic's corner

There is a tempting overcorrection in the collaboration story: if AI removes productive disagreement, configure it to disagree, and the educational problem is solved. DEC's public example is more careful than that. Its adversarial-peer approach also flags the possibility that students stop producing their own counterarguments (report overview). A debate can become more demanding while leaving a different part of thinking unpractised.

That is not a reason to reject the activity. A novice sometimes needs a supplied objection before learning to spot one. The question is what happens later, when the scripted opponent is absent. Can the group notice an assumption, formulate a challenge and revise its view without a machine setting the agenda? Counting the number of rebuttals in the assisted discussion would not answer it.

The Guangdong study offers a useful contrast in measurement by assessing performance without AI after the programme, although its combined intervention and unstructured expert ratings limit what can be inferred (accepted article). Schools do not need to reproduce a research trial to preserve that distinction. They can ask whether a learner can explain a choice, handle an unfamiliar example or raise a relevant objection after the support is removed. The result may justify keeping the scaffold longer, changing it or retiring it. An engaging AI conversation is a learning opportunity. What the learner can subsequently do is the evidence that gives it educational weight.

The Week That Was

Google shipped at its usual clip, most usefully insight summaries that give teachers "greater visibility into how students interact with assigned notebooks" (Google, 19 October). The research ran the other way: at the University of Maryland, offering undergraduates a course-integrated AI tutor lowered their grades (IZA, October 2026). That gap is this week's question.

Since Friday's brief: Texas sued OpenAI and Sam Altman in Collin County on Friday 23 October. It wants an injunction requiring OpenAI to block minors from its product and to get any new model approved by a court-appointed evaluator (Texas Tribune, 23 October). Among the harms Paxton's press release lists: "users have lost critical thinking skills". The Tribune notes that Elon Musk, an OpenAI rival, is the largest single donor to Paxton's Senate campaign. Google also made Gemini Notebook a connected app inside the Gemini app, on by default outside the European Economic Area wherever both are enabled (Google, 23 October).

Saturday 24 October: Fortune ran a commentary arguing that every child will get an AI tutor but only families who can pay will keep human teachers (Fortune, 24 October). Its author founded school and IT-education networks, which is worth knowing. Sunday was quiet.

What Does It All Mean?

Last week's answer was that manner matters more than dose. Maryland raises the next question: who sets the manner? Is an AI tutor something a school switches on, or something a teacher designs?

Read past the headline, a fall of up to 0.37 standard deviations in final grades, and the Maryland paper half-answers it (IZA). The tutor had a mode that guides students toward an answer instead of supplying one. The default was direct instruction, and only two instructors ended the term with tutor mode on. About 15% of students offered the tool used it even once. Of their requests, 73.8% sought information, explanations or solutions; 0.7% sought feedback on their own work. The authors allow that the tool's arrival may have shifted perceived permission to use AI generally, so the result may measure a signal as much as a product.

The trials where AI tutoring worked look different. At Harvard, Greg Kestin and colleagues built their physics tutor's prompts around active engagement, cognitive load and growth mindset, and walked students through each problem in sequence. Students learned more, in less time, than in an active-learning class (Scientific Reports, June 2025). A six-week after-school programme in Nigeria produced gains of about 0.3 standard deviations; the World Bank team's verdict is that the tool works as a tutor when implemented "with teacher support" (World Bank, January 2025). Stanford's Tutor CoPilot put the AI behind the human: students whose tutors had it were 4 percentage points more likely to master topics, and 9 points with lower-rated tutors (Wang and colleagues, arXiv).

The cleanest high-school test isolates the variable. Hamsa Bastani's team gave nearly 1,000 maths students in Turkey either a plain ChatGPT-style interface or a tutor that "includes teacher input and is designed to guide students with hints rather than directly giving answers" (Wharton, August 2024). On the unaided exam, the plain group scored 17% below students who never had AI. The tutor group avoided that loss (PNAS, July 2025).

So the working answer: AI tutoring helps when someone has designed what it does, when, and what follows. Made merely available, it becomes an answer machine, and the defaults decide the rest.

That matters for schools running Gemini, because Google's defaults are generous. The Gemini student hub, with study notebooks, flashcards and quizzes, reaches Education users of all ages, governed only by the admin console's generative AI settings (Google, 1 October). That is Maryland's condition at scale. A teacher-assigned Gemini Notebook is nearer Harvard's. The number worth tracking this year is not accounts provisioned but tasks designed, each followed by a short unaided check.

Behind You! Behind You!

Only one US state requires AI tools to be checked for their effect on learning. CRPE's annual State of the American Student report counts 36 states with published or endorsed AI guidance, most of it advisory (CRPE). The only one requiring AI tools to be vetted for academic outcomes is Maryland (The 74, 21 October), home of the university behind this week's sobering tutor trial. International schools have no state doing even that; for them, the only vetting layer is the school itself.

Anthropic handed the Agent Skills standard to a neutral foundation. On 22 October it donated the format to the Agentic AI Foundation, the Linux Foundation body it co-founded with Block and OpenAI. Anthropic says more than 70 agent platforms, including ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot, have integrated it. Google's own transition page says Gemini skills use "the open, Markdown-based SKILL.md format" (Google). So a teacher's skill built in Gemini should travel between tools. "Should" is doing work there; test one before promising anyone.

Google Vault can now export Gemini conversations. Under the 22 October update, admins on Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus can filter exports by user or organisational unit, up to 5,000 users, and by date range. It covers Gemini in Gmail, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Slides and Meet when conversation history is on; retention rules and litigation holds come later. Gemini chats inside Docs and Gmail are now retrievable records. Who may request them, and why, deserves a written answer before the first request.

Meet's speech translation now covers 63 languages. The 22 October update allows any combination of them, with automatic language detection. In schools it needs the Google AI Pro for Education add-on; Scheduled Release domains start on 2 November, and base plans cap sessions at 90 minutes. Google doesn't list the 63, so check the languages a school's families actually speak before anyone promises a parent a translated conference.

The Riff Returns But Slower

  • 1 November: Microsoft's AFT-negotiated standards start applying to schools it contracts with: no training AI on student or educator data, bar a narrow exception for data that could help protect students, plus third-party audits, according to the AFT. As of September, Google had not said whether it would follow (AP via KTAR, 14 September). For a Google school, that silence is the item.
  • 2 November: Meet speech translation begins reaching Scheduled Release domains, with visibility taking up to 15 days (Google, 22 October).
  • Early November: Vault API support for Gemini exports, due within 14 days of the interface rollout (Google, 22 October).
  • 17 November: Gems move to Settings in the Gemini app; Gemini-app skills finish rolling out by mid-November (Google).
  • Slow burn: the state suits against OpenAI. Texas's court-appointed evaluator is the remedy to watch; if any state wins something like it, pre-release review stops being voluntary (Texas Tribune, 23 October).

Sceptic's Corner

Next time someone says the answer is simply a well-designed tutor, offer three facts.

First, design reliably prevented harm; it did not reliably produce learning. Bastani's guard-railed tutor lifted practice scores by 127%, yet its students "scored about the same on the exam as the control group", who never had AI (Wharton).

Second, much of the positive evidence comes from builders marking their own homework. The Harvard team built the tutor it tested, and the paper notes that its authors overlap with those behind the active-learning method it beat (Scientific Reports). A September preprint on GCSE science found a solid effect (g = 0.33) for the Medly platform against self-directed revision; Medly funded the evaluation, and attrition was 30.7% (arXiv, September 2026).

Third, the horizons are short: two weeks at Harvard, four in England, six in Nigeria. Nobody yet knows what a year of well-designed AI does to a fifteen-year-old's ability to work without it.

So the defensible claim is narrower than the pitch. Designed AI use can avoid the damage open access does. Whether it beats a good teacher given the same minutes has been shown, so far, mainly in one Harvard physics course. Guardrails are a seatbelt, not an engine.
