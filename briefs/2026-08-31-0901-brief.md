## Top story

**The risky part of teacher AI has moved from the prompt to the dataset.** On 28 August, Anthropic amended its July launch post for Claude for Teachers. Examples that had described analysing classroom data now specify de-identified diagnostics, exit tickets and notes; the company also clarified that processing identifiable student records requires school or district authorization. The same update announced a separate schools-and-districts offering for institutions that want Claude to handle student records on their behalf ([Anthropic, updated 28 August 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-for-teachers)). That is a useful correction. It is also an admission that a teacher-only account, a FERPA-configured service and institutional authority are three different things.

The contrast arrived the same day in an OpenAI Academy profile. A Davis School District digital-learning director is described as uploading a full year of an online school’s results, asking the system to find struggling subjects and teachers, and preparing to train principals to analyse their own school results. The piece also imagines converting special-education assessments into individualized learning goals. It does not say whether the uploaded records were de-identified, which OpenAI service tier processed them, what agreement governed the transfer or who authorized the use ([OpenAI Academy, 28 August 2026](https://academy.openai.com/public/blogs/how-ryan-hansen-uses-ai-to-turn-data-into-better-feedback-in-schools-2026-08-28)). None of that proves mishandling. It shows how quickly a persuasive workflow can outrun the governance detail in the public account of it.

For international schools, the useful Monday move is not to add another approved-logo list. Put a data gate in front of staff-facing AI tasks: name the data category, minimize it, record the institutional authority, identify the contracted environment, set access and retention, then require a human decision before feedback or a student plan moves downstream. A tool can be safe enough for lesson planning and still be the wrong place for identifiable diagnostics. The unit of approval is the job plus the data, not the chatbot.

## Other stories

- **The strongest current case for AI tutoring is still AI behind the tutor, not AI instead of one.** Education Week’s 28 August report draws attention to a Stanford SCALE brief that maps tutoring by “relational intensity.” The underlying evidence is awkward for the autonomous-tutor pitch: in a supplemental-maths platform study of 181,000 students, only 5% reached the recommended 30 minutes a week and 41% never logged on; teacher, school and district factors explained 57% of usage variation. In two district trials, 40–47% of students assigned an AI-only platform never used it, while those who did averaged two to five minutes weekly. Human check-ins improved engagement but did not produce a reading-achievement gain. By contrast, an AI system giving suggestions to human tutors was associated with four percentage points more topic mastery, rising to nine points for students of lower-rated tutors ([Education Week, 28 August 2026](https://www.edweek.org/technology/when-does-ai-help-most-with-tutoring-what-emerging-research-says/2026/08); [Stanford SCALE, 20 August 2026](https://scale.stanford.edu/research-in-action/ai-tutoring-not-monolith)). For international schools, any tutoring pilot needs scheduled dosage, a named adult relationship and an AI-free outcome measure; account provision is not implementation.

- **A general-purpose model asked to be helpful behaved mostly like a solver.** A KAIST preprint posted on 24 August analysed 14,637 model responses from 203 students using GPT-4o-mini in an introductory university AI course. The system had no teaching instruction beyond a generic helpful-assistant prompt. Researchers classified 55.2% of responses as complete, task-specific solutions and 40.3% as detailed explanations; prompting, hinting and minimal assistance together made up less than 5%. The classifier reached 75.3% accuracy on a held-out set of 150 human-labelled responses, with most errors at the explaining-versus-solving boundary. Assistance level was associated with what students did next, but added little predictive information about three later AI-free exams beyond prior achievement and dialogue behaviour ([Lee and colleagues, 24 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22993)). It is one course, one small model and observational follow-up—not a causal learning verdict. It gives Ed Tech Coaches a testable acceptance criterion: if a school tutor claims to scaffold, sample its actual replies and count how often it leaves the next cognitive move to the student.

- **An Alabama district is buying four more voice robots for English learners; the useful claim is speaking time, not embodiment.** Alabaster City Schools said on 29 August that it is expanding a Telo AI pilot for 2026–27 after using a voice-first device for English practice. The vendor says students receive about one extra hour of speaking each month. The district says teachers retain the curriculum and learning objective, while the device adapts conversation around existing materials; it also states that images, audio and transcripts are not retained, and student data is not used for model training. No comparison-group result, language-growth figure, cohort size or independent evaluation is provided ([Alabaster City Schools, 29 August 2026](https://www.acsboe.org/article/3100634)). That makes this an implementation signal, not evidence that a robot improves acquisition. Still, the screen-free, spoken-interaction design is worth separating from the hardware theatre. A comparable language pilot in an international school would measure substantive student speaking minutes, correction quality, teacher intervention and delayed oral transfer—not whether the device was engaging or whether the vendor’s dashboard moved.

- **Thailand’s new education-AI accelerator has put evidence in the graduation requirements, at least on paper.** OpenAI and Thailand’s Ministry of Higher Education, Science, Research and Innovation announced on 28 August an eight-week cohort of ten startups, five in education. Teams receive US$2,000 in API credits, technical guidance and weekly work on design, testing, evaluation, privacy, security, cost and fundraising. By November’s Demo Day they are expected to show a working product or substantial upgrade, evidence from representative users, initial evaluation findings and a path to implementation. One participant, Curico, plans childcare-centre pilots in Bangkok, training for more than 200 teachers, an AI-assisted grading test at Chulalongkorn University and a family-learning platform targeting more than 1,000 monthly users ([OpenAI, 28 August 2026](https://openai.com/index/supporting-next-generation-ai-startups-thailand/)). These are vendor and programme targets, not achieved outcomes. For international schools, the regional value will be in the evaluation artefacts: what the teams measured, which failures stopped a rollout and whether local-language and classroom conditions changed model performance. Demo Day applause is not the deliverable to borrow.

- **A small US fund is paying ten education systems to turn responsible-AI principles into operating practice.** Norwalk Public Schools’ community partner reported on 29 August that the district will receive US$23,000 from the US$250,000 inaugural EDSAFE AI Spark Fund, plus hands-on technical assistance. The programme’s stated work includes student-information protection, vendor-privacy review, preservation of human judgement, equitable standards for tool selection, educator AI literacy and family transparency. Norwalk previously joined EDSAFE’s District Policy Lab; this round is meant to move from policy and institutional knowledge into implementation, with lessons from all ten recipients documented for wider use ([Carver, 29 August 2026](https://the-carver.org/blog/2026/8/29/norwalk-public-schools-selected-for-national-ai-initiative-and-carver-applauds-the-focus-on-safe-equitable-innovation)). The award is too small to prove scale and the account comes from a local partner, not an evaluation. Its practical grain is right. International schools should watch for the promised decision logs, approved-use patterns, family materials and failed tests: artefacts showing how a principle changed a deployment are worth more than another polished framework.

## On the radar

- **One university is making AI literacy an enrolled experience rather than an optional workshop.** The University of Alabama’s AI Experience opens today and runs through 28 October: students are automatically enrolled in a self-paced course, while faculty and staff may opt in, with interactive modules and digital badges covering responsible use and practical applications ([University of Alabama, 31 August 2026](https://calendar.ua.edu/event/ua-ai-experience)). The listing gives no assessment design or success measure. Automatic enrolment solves reach but not transfer. The useful follow-up would compare completion, behaviour on real coursework and later unaided judgement—not badges issued or satisfaction recorded at the finish line.

- **OpenAI’s last announced K–12 Skills Jam of the US summer lands in Las Vegas on Friday.** The 4 September session closes a series advertised to reach more than 1,600 teachers, administrators and district leaders through hands-on workshops and continuing Academy access ([OpenAI, 8 July; event 4 September 2026](https://openai.com/index/k-12-educators-practical-skills/)). Continuing access creates a plausible route beyond one-day enthusiasm, but no follow-up evidence is published yet. The number to request after the roadshow is not attendance. It is how many participants changed a task, tested it with students or rejected a proposed use—and what evidence informed that decision.

## Sceptic's corner

The reassuring privacy phrase this term is *enterprise-grade*. Training is off. A data-processing addendum exists. The account is configured for education. Each of those is useful, and none answers the operational question raised by Friday’s two vendor pages: who decided that this particular dataset could be used for this particular purpose?

Anthropic’s correction is unusually instructive because it separates the layers. A verified teacher can have a protected account without having authority to process identifiable student records; institutional authorization belongs to a different lane ([Anthropic, updated 28 August 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-for-teachers)). OpenAI’s case study, meanwhile, describes a genuinely plausible school-improvement workflow but leaves the reader unable to reconstruct the data boundary ([OpenAI Academy, 28 August 2026](https://academy.openai.com/public/blogs/how-ryan-hansen-uses-ai-to-turn-data-into-better-feedback-in-schools-2026-08-28)). The omission may belong only to the article. That is precisely why a customer story cannot serve as a procedure.

The easy overreaction is to forbid staff from using school data in any AI system. That throws away legitimate analysis inside properly contracted environments and encourages quiet workarounds. The better rule is narrower and harder: approve a defined job, minimum dataset, environment, owner, access group, retention period, review step and deletion path. Re-run the gate when any one of those changes.

The staffroom line is this: a safe account is a property of the service. A lawful, educationally sound use is a property of the whole workflow. Do not ask the logo to answer for the workflow.
