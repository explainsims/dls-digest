## Top story

**New York's school AI restrictions have reached the awkward business of deciding which features count.** In reporting published on 23 October, Chalkbeat obtained the Education Department's list of 38 flagged products and contacted the companies behind them. The department says the restrictions concern student-facing generative AI features, rather than necessarily banning whole products. Several suppliers dispute why their tools were listed. Others have disabled features and regained permission to operate. That is the new development here: an account of implementation, more than a month after the initial announcement ([Chalkbeat, 23 October](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/10/23/nyc-schools-ai-ban-list-38-education-technology-products/)).

The examples resist a clean approved-or-banned label. Amira, used in more than 200 city schools, was stopped outright because officials said it could not function without AI. Magma Math says it disabled AI-generated hints on 4 September and received permission to continue on 5 October. Wordly disputes its inclusion, saying its translation tool does not use generative AI. These are reported company positions and departmental explanations, not an independent technical audit. They illustrate why an inventory of product names cannot quite do the job of an inventory of what each product does.

For international schools, the transferable question is what a teacher can actually enable inside an already familiar tool. A school might reasonably approve a worksheet platform while holding back its new conversational tutor. That distinction needs to survive the sign-in, the class setup and the next supplier update. Otherwise staff inherit the impossible job of interpreting a policy from whatever buttons happen to appear that morning.

The practical measure of governance is whether the rule can be explained and reproduced: this function, for this age group, with this data, under these controls. A blanket yes conceals variation; a blanket no can remove useful functions along with disputed ones. Neither position saves the institution from understanding the software. The quieter lesson from New York is that drawing a boundary is only the beginning of making it hold.

## Other stories

- **Google Sheets is gaining a useful statistics feature without asking anyone to prompt a chatbot.** Its 22 October announcement introduces native box-and-whisker charts from raw numeric data, including selectable inclusive or exclusive quartile calculations and controls for outliers, inner points and mean markers. Excel imports retain these charts, and Sheets exports them as editable Excel charts. Rapid Release rollout started on 22 October; Scheduled Release starts on **6 November**, with up to 15 days for visibility. Google lists all Workspace customers and personal accounts, with no separate administrator control ([Google Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/create-box-and-whisker-charts-in-google-sheets.html)). For teaching, that lowers the mechanical cost of comparing distributions while leaving the interesting decisions intact. Which observations belong together? Does a difference in medians hide a much wider spread? Why did changing the quartile convention alter the picture? Those questions are available to students who can inspect the data and the chart settings. The same caution applies to school dashboards: an outlier marker identifies a position in a distribution, without explaining the person behind it. A modest charting improvement can support better statistical discussion than a fluent automated interpretation, provided someone still asks what the plot permits them to conclude.

- **Apple has opened a school purchasing route for app subscriptions sold by the seat.** From 22 October, organisations using Apple School Manager or Apple Business can buy seats for auto-renewable subscriptions through one in-app purchase and assign them through existing device-management tools. Seats can be added or removed as needs change. Developers control availability, although multiseat purchasing is enabled by default unless they have previously changed the setting. This is distinct from consumer group purchases: sandbox testing for those starts in November, with the App Store launch planned for winter ([Apple Developer, 22 October](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=yfo21auu)). For an Apple school, the meaningful change is the possibility of institutional ownership where a useful subscription previously sat on a teacher's individual account. It does not establish that a particular app participates, offers suitable terms, or handles student information appropriately. Nor does convenient seat management answer what happens to work when a subscription ends. The sensible comparison is the whole lifecycle: purchase, assignment, renewal, a staff departure and eventual exit. A tidier checkout is welcome. It should make those questions easier to administer rather than make them disappear behind the purchase button.

- **A newly posted workplace study counts the labour between an AI draft and something safe to send.** Najeeb Gambo Abdulhamid and colleagues submitted their Kenya-and-Nigeria study on 22 October. It draws on 21 interviews with 27 workers in 21 small and medium-sized businesses, conducted from **July to October 2023**. The publication is new; the tools and experiences described are from that earlier period. Participants reported checking claims, repairing unusable references, comparing tools and correcting language or local-context failures before circulating outputs. The authors treat the two countries as situated cases, without claiming a representative picture of either region ([preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31344)). This is qualitative workplace evidence, not a school trial or a measured productivity effect. Its useful contribution for teacher professional learning is a fuller unit of work. Timing the generation of a resource stops the clock too early if the teacher then spends the saved minutes checking examples, rebuilding sources and correcting assumptions about the learners. Conversely, that checking may make the finished resource better than the previous one. A fair evaluation needs both possibilities visible. Asking a teacher to bring the first draft, the repaired version and an account of the changes would reveal more than another satisfaction score about how easy prompting felt.

- **Google Vids is getting ordinary editing controls that make explanatory video more workable.** Two 23 October announcements add independent audio extraction and clip playback-speed adjustment. Detached audio can be trimmed or moved across scenes without disturbing the video; speed ranges from **0.25× to 2×**. Audio extraction began a gradual rollout on 19 October, speed adjustment on 16 October, both taking up to 15 days across release tracks. Google lists all Workspace customers, Workspace Individual subscribers and personal accounts ([audio extraction](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/extract-audio-tracks-in-google-vids.html); [playback speed](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/adjust-video-clip-playback-speed-directly-in-Google-Vids.html)). A teacher explaining a procedure can keep narration running while the visual moves to the relevant detail, or slow a short sequence rather than repeat a whole recording. Students making explanations gain an editorial choice worth discussing: what does the audience need time to notice? These are editing capabilities, with no learning gains demonstrated in the announcements. In a science explanation, altered speed also needs to be made explicit whenever timing is evidence. A clip made easier to watch should not quietly become misleading data. That distinction itself makes a worthwhile media-literacy conversation, alongside crediting footage and preserving the meaning of the original event.

- **Blackboard's latest institutional conversation puts the burden of complexity on the agenda.** Its 23 October account of a UK higher-education executive roundtable describes overlapping systems, unnecessary licences, course-template checking and interest in portfolios and assessment across a longer learning journey. Participants also discussed making data useful to the people expected to act on it. This is a supplier's summary of a discussion, with no reported intervention results or demonstrated savings ([Blackboard Community](https://community.blackboard.com/public/blogs/beyond-efficiency-rethinking-the-future-of-higher-education-2026-10-23)). The school-relevant tension is between consistency and fit. A common course structure can help families find the assignment; forcing every learning experience into an identical sequence can create its own workarounds. Equally, a specialist tool may solve one department's problem while adding another login and another support obligation for everyone else. Neither the largest platform nor the smallest collection of subscriptions wins automatically. The useful evidence would follow a few real tasks across the system: where a teacher repeats an entry, where a student loses the thread, where someone must repair the handover. Simplification earns its place when those journeys improve. Counting fewer products is an administrative result, not yet an educational one.

## On the radar

- **Frederick County's student-AI restrictions now have an implementing regulation.** The district says Regulation 124-01 was adopted on 22 October, following its 7 October board policy. Its public explanation restricts student AI use while allowing specific curricular needs, explicit AI-literacy instruction and uses required by an Individualized Education Program or Section 504 Plan ([district update](https://www.fcps.org/academics/digital_learning_experience/artificial_intelligence_ai)). The adoption notice establishes the regulation's existence, rather than showing how it works in classrooms. The implementation worth watching is whether staff can explain those exceptions consistently in ordinary lessons, and whether families understand what remains available. A restriction's practical meaning emerges through those decisions.

## Sceptic's corner

The reassuring claim running through today's announcements is that a human remains in charge. It is a necessary starting condition and a remarkably incomplete description of a working arrangement.

My criterion for workable oversight is more demanding than putting a person at the end of a workflow: the reviewer needs the authority and information to interpret, override or reject an output before it affects someone. The difficult words are *information* and *before*. A teacher receiving a polished recommendation with no usable trail may have formal permission to reject it while lacking any realistic means of deciding whether to do so. A review that arrives after the message has gone out cannot protect its recipient.

My test would be mundane. Can the responsible adult explain one consequential claim, show the evidence that supports it and describe what would cause them to stop the process? Can they do that within the time the workflow actually allows? If the answer requires an afternoon nobody has, the review condition exists on paper while the system runs on trust.

That does not mean every use needs a committee. A rough brainstorm and a recommendation about a child's support deserve different scrutiny. It means the person doing the checking needs a task they can complete, evidence they can inspect and a real chance to change the outcome. Putting a human in the loop is easy. Giving that human a workable job is the part worth specifying.
