## Top story

**England's least experienced teachers are its heaviest users of AI, and the gap holds on every task except the two that are already templated.** Teacher Tapp data covering the 2025-26 academic year, published 31 July, found 68% of early-career teachers — those working through the Early Career Framework — had used AI for lesson planning or preparation in the previous month, against 54% of classroom teachers not on the ECF. ECTs were twice as likely to have used it for continuing professional development or training, at 10% against 5%, and three times as likely for timetables and seating plans, at 6% against 2%. AI use for communication with parents about individual students stood at 18% among ECTs and 11% for other classroom teachers; for marking or feedback, 12% and 7%. ECTs were also less likely to report no AI use at all, at 18% compared with 23% ([EdTech Innovation Hub, 31 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/englands-early-career-teachers-lead-experienced-peers-in-classroom-ai-use-teacher-tapp-data-shows)).

Two categories break the pattern and they are the instructive ones. Curriculum development was level at 15%, and 33% of ECTs against 32% of other teachers used AI to write pupil reports. Where the output is bounded and formulaic, everyone reaches for it equally; where a teacher is alone with a blank page, the fourteen-point gap opens. The rest of the dataset suggests why. In December 2025, 58% of ECTs said teaching and learning had largely stopped because of poor behaviour, against 42% of non-ECT classroom teachers; by 10 July, 49% of teachers with under five years in the classroom reported disruption, falling to 43% at five to ten years, 42% at ten to twenty, and 31% beyond twenty. The cohort adopting fastest is the cohort under most strain. That is not a digital-natives story — ECTs are not younger than the tools. It is a story about who has the least slack, and it should worry anyone whose AI professional learning runs as a voluntary after-school session, because that delivery model is aimed precisely away from the people already using the stuff.

## Other stories

- **The US state wave that started with phone bans has turned on school-issued devices, and the numbers are specific.** Illinois Governor JB Pritzker signed Senate Bill 2427 on 28 July, requiring districts to adopt bell-to-bell restrictions with a lighter touch at high school — Senator Cristina Castro cited students who "do dual (high school and college) credit" — with policies due before the 2027-28 school year ([Capitol News Illinois, 28 July 2026](https://capitolnewsillinois.com/news/pritzker-signs-school-cellphone-ban-to-avoid-student-distractions/)). The sharper development is the next wave. Iowa now caps digital instruction at "60 minutes per day for students in kindergarten through fifth grade, with exceptions for special education, assessments and computer science"; Utah directed its state board to write grade-specific rules "including restrictions on screen time in kindergarten through third grade and prohibiting one-device-per-student ratios in elementary school"; Los Angeles Unified bars instructional screen time before second grade, with limits that "increase incrementally to" 60 minutes per subject each week for middle school students and 90 minutes per subject each week for high school students ([Stateline via Route Fifty, 24 July 2026](https://www.route-fifty.com/management/2026/07/states-move-limit-classroom-screen-time/414990/)). Instructure's Tracy Weeks pushed back on the framing: "The policy focus has been on minutes, and that's the wrong conversation to be having, because not all minutes are created equal." Minutes are a crude instrument; nobody offered legislators a better one. A school that cannot say what its devices are *for*, per grade, is going to have an answer written for it.

- **A national education ministry lost 607,000 records, and the police database breached alongside it gave up passwords.** A group calling itself ExfilSquad took more than 740,000 records from UK education and police systems — roughly 607,000 from the Department for Education's online help desk and Turing Scheme portal, and another 135,000 from the Police National Legal Database. The exposed data covers names, telephone numbers, email addresses, job titles and work addresses belonging to parents, educators, police officers and government officials, and in the PNLD case passwords as well; financial information was not accessed. A DfE spokesperson said "We have robust processes in place to protect information and took swift action to contain this incident," adding that "The information involved is limited to customer service contact details relating to individuals and organisations. No other data has been accessed." The National Crime Agency said "We are aware of an incident affecting the Department for Education and are working with partners to understand the circumstances and impact" ([EdTech Innovation Hub, 30 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/hackers-obtained-around-607000-records-from-systems-operated-by-the-uk-department-for-education)). Note where the breach landed: not the student information system, but the help desk. Support tooling accumulates the same contact graph as the systems it supports while sitting outside the review those systems get — and, as the police database shows, it sometimes holds credentials too. Audit the ticketing platform with the same seriousness as the SIS, and ask what it stores beyond contact details.

- **Coursera put $100 million into a new company founded by its own co-founder, betting that tutoring agents replace courses.** The investment goes to LearnVector, founded by Andrew Ng, which plans to use AI agents to build individualised learning experiences that adapt as people practise and master skills. Coursera CEO Greg Hart framed the thesis directly — "For decades, learning has largely followed a one-to-many model" — and described the target as software that will "adapt to each individual, help people practice new skills, and stay with them until they've truly mastered the material," explicitly contrasted with "Rather than a search box or chatbot that answers questions and moves on…". Hart's closing line was "AI doesn't diminish the need for learning, it expands it," and the plan leans on Coursera's content library, learner data from over 300 million users, and its university and industry partnerships ([EdTech Innovation Hub, 31 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/the-newly-formed-company-plans-to-use-ai-agents-to-create-individualized-learning-experiences-that-adapt-as-people-practice-and-master-new-skills)). Adaptive-mastery claims have a long and unimpressive history, and $100 million buys engineering rather than evidence. What makes this one worth tracking is the data asset behind it: 300 million learners is the largest corpus anyone has pointed at this problem, so if the approach fails here the failure will be informative.

- **Switzerland's publicly funded open model learned to see and hear, and it is still Apache 2.0.** Apertus 1.5 was released on 24 July by EPFL, ETH Zurich and the Swiss National Supercomputing Centre, trained on the Alps supercomputer at CSCS in Lugano. The update adds image and audio understanding alongside text, with improved reasoning, stronger instruction following and better tool use, released under Apache 2.0 for research and commercial use alongside Apertus Mini, a suite of 16 compact models. EPFL's Martin Jaggi described the approach as incremental: "Rather than introducing an entirely new model, Apertus 1.5 incorporates feedback from the first release and adds several new capabilities." Deployments named include the Canton of Ticino translating government documents and the Basel news outlet Bajour, whose editor Samuel Hufschmid said "With Apertus, we know what we're building on." ([EPFL, 24 July 2026](https://actu.epfl.ch/news/apertus-15-building-the-next-generation-of-open--2/) · [EdTech Innovation Hub, 31 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/swiss-ai-initiative-adds-image-and-audio-capabilities-to-apertus-15)). Neither announcement states the sizes; the model card describes "a family of 8B and 70B parameter language models" ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/swiss-ai/Apertus-v1.5-70B)). An 8B multimodal model under a permissive licence is the size that runs on hardware a school could actually own, which makes this the most realistic answer yet to whether student work can be processed without sending it to a vendor — not because it is as good, but because the question of where the data goes stops being someone else's to answer.

- **OpenAI is hiring undergraduates as paid organisers, which is a distribution strategy wearing a community-programme badge.** The Student Collective opened applications on 28 July for Campus Leads across the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, India, Japan and South Korea, closing "August 10, 2026, at 11:59 p.m. PT". Selected students commit "Four to six hours per week" from September 2026 to June 2027 and must run four workshops per semester, weekly Studio Hours, one campus-wide showcase per semester and ongoing community support. In return: a stipend, a ChatGPT subscription, Codex credits, event funding, merchandise, training, and a possible headquarters visit in June 2027. Applicants must be 18 or over, enrolled as of August 2026, expect to graduate after December 2027, hold work authorisation, and must not be an ambassador or intern for a competing AI programme. No prior AI experience is needed; OpenAI says it prioritises "curiosity, willingness to learn and an ability to bring people together" ([EdTech Innovation Hub, 29 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/jxmljsgsw8sb5z32zbz9sthtdp4cy1)). The exclusivity clause is the giveaway — a genuine AI-literacy programme would not care which other tools a student evangelises. Worth reading as the university-tier version of what will eventually arrive in schools as a student-ambassador offer, and worth having a view on before it does.

## On the radar

- **The connector standard underneath AI assistants had its largest revision, and quietly deprecated the thing schools would have used.** MCP 2026-07-28 shipped on 28 July, moving the protocol to a stateless core — "No handshake or sessions", with each request carrying its own protocol version and client identity, so any request "can now land on any server instance behind a plain round-robin load balancer". It hardens authorisation around OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect, binding client credentials to the issuer that minted them with "No reuse across authorization servers" — and "Dynamic Client Registration itself is now formally deprecated in favor of CIMD" ([Model Context Protocol, 28 July 2026](https://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2026-07-28/)). Plumbing, but this is the layer that will decide whether an LMS can be safely exposed to an assistant.

- **OpenAI is buying academic mindshare at the top of the funnel too.** A programme announced 30 July offers free GPT-5.6 and Codex access to 100,000 researchers through 2027, beginning with 10,000 in summer 2026, part of a commitment of "more than $250 million". Eligibility is restricted to "selected, recognized degree-granting colleges or universities with a high level of research activity", and "Approved participants can invite up to four collaborators, but each must work at the same institution". OpenAI's stated rationale: "We believe the benefits of frontier AI should not be concentrated in a few companies and well-resourced labs. Researchers know their fields best." ([EdTech Innovation Hub, 30 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/openai-opens-free-gpt-56-and-codex-program-for-100000-researchers)). Note the eligibility filter concentrates access rather than distributing it.

- **Public money is building the AI research instruments that private labs already have.** Rice University took a $19.9 million, four-year NSF award on 30 July to build READINESS — Revolutionizing AI-Driven Autonomous Experimentation for Next-Generation Semiconductor Synthesis — with SUNY Polytechnic Institute, the University of Texas at Austin and the Astera Institute, under principal investigator Jun Lou. It sits inside a $380 million NSF national network investment, with up to $20 million in matching funds available ([EdTech Innovation Hub, 30 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/rice-university-secures-199-million-nsf-award-to-build-ai-powered-materials-laboratory)). A comparable $20 million NSF award to the University of Tennessee, dated 28 July, appears in the same outlet's news feed. The line to watch is whether autonomous-experimentation infrastructure ever reaches undergraduate teaching, or stays a research instrument.

## Sceptic's corner

The claim in circulation this week is that Claude broke post-quantum cryptography. The headlines say cracked. Anthropic's own post is more careful: "To be clear, neither of these results has a practical impact on today's computer systems; no production software will have to change as a result" ([Anthropic, 28 July 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/discovering-cryptographic-weaknesses)).

Test it properly. On HAWK — a NIST third-round candidate, not deployed — Claude Mythos Preview found a previously unexploited symmetry that cut the expected cost of recovering the smallest key from 2^64 operations to 2^38; finding, developing and verifying that attack took about 60 hours in total. That matters for standardisation, because doubling the key size to compensate "eliminates many of the reasons making the scheme (as it currently stands) an attractive PQC signature candidate." The AES result carries the scarier headline and is much weaker: a 7-of-10-round research variant, extending prior work that already assumed an attacker could request the encryption of 2^105 chosen plaintexts. Cryptographer Matthew Green's verdict is blunt — "So TL;DR: no wildly new mathematical results here" — while insisting "This does not make the result bad!" ([Matthew Green, 29 July 2026](https://blog.cryptographyengineering.com/2026/07/29/some-notes-about-anthropics-new-results/)).

The genuinely transferable finding is in the methodology, and it is not about cryptography. Anthropic reports that "it took two researchers nearly a month to gain confidence that the method it discovered is correct" for an attack the model produced in a week, and that "the vast majority of our time over the past few months has been in verifying the correctness of Claude's results." Green's version: "just because a model spits out an apparent new result, this _does not mean the result is real_." Generation got cheap and verification did not. Any assessment design that hands students a fast generator and leaves a human to check the output has inherited the exact ratio now bottlenecking a frontier lab with unlimited money.
