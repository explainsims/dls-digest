## Top story

**A curated notebook can now follow a teacher into the document they are writing.** On 23 September, Google said Gemini in Docs can use an existing Gemini Notebook as a context source: a writer types `@` in the side panel or bottom bar, selects the notebook, and gets a draft grounded in its sources with inline citations. Google lists the feature as available now on Education Plus and through the Google AI Pro for Education or Teaching and Learning add-ons. It requires Gemini for Workspace in Drive to be enabled by the administrator and Workspace smart features to be enabled by the user ([Google Workspace Updates, 23 September](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/ground-ai-prompts-in-google-docs-on-existing-sources-from-Gemini-Notebook.html)). The switch from a standalone research space to an assistant at the point of writing is the news; Google has not published classroom learning results for this integration.

The classroom use case is plain enough: a department could keep approved readings, a unit outline and a rubric in a notebook, then ask for a lesson outline or family-facing explanation in Docs that points back to those materials. For international schools, that would make the notebook's *contents and ownership* a more important governance decision than the prompt. A citation may identify a source while still misreading it, and a notebook that includes superseded policy, unlicensed extracts or identifiable student work would make a neat-looking document from material that should not have been there. The teacher still needs to open the cited passage, check the claim and decide whether the output fits the learners.

This also sharpens a distinction missed in some of the launch chatter. Google's separate 22 September announcement made **study notebooks** available to school-managed accounts outside the European Economic Area where both the Gemini app and Gemini Notebook are enabled for the user's group or organisational unit; those offer progress-check quizzes, short lessons and a mastery dashboard, with a gradual Rapid Release rollout that began on 17 September. The Docs drafting feature has its own plan eligibility and settings ([Google Workspace Updates, 22 September](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/study-notebooks-in-gemini-are-now-available-for-Google-Workspace-accounts.html)). A school should test the two entitlements separately before telling teachers that the entire notebook-to-document workflow is ready for every account.

## Other stories

- **CUNY published a five-step AI-use checklist that begins before the prompt.** Jeremy Caplan, director of teaching and learning at CUNY's Newmark Graduate School of Journalism, asks users to ideate independently, label AI-generated material, verify claims, protect privacy and assess tools sceptically; CUNY published the checklist on 22 September ([CUNY, 22 September](https://www.cuny.edu/blog/ai-ethical-checklist-one-facultys-quick-guide-for-higher-education/)). The first step is the useful counterweight to a product interface that tries to begin every task with a chat box: students write down what they think before asking for assistance, leaving a trace of their own reasoning. The framework comes from one faculty member's higher-education practice, not a tested K–12 intervention, and its privacy rule is deliberately conservative. At an international school, it could be adapted into a one-page prompt for assignment design, but teachers would still need examples by subject and age: what independent first attempt looks like in algebra is different from what it looks like in a language discussion. A slogan about keeping humans in control becomes useful only when the assignment records *which* human decisions must remain visible.

- **Elon is giving departments time and support to decide where AI belongs, including where it does not.** A 22 September university account describes its FIELDS initiative: 21 departmental teams and 88 faculty began a yearlong programme after an August workshop, with teams of three to five, a designated fellow in each department and monthly cross-team meetings. The work is allowed to redesign assessments to show learning without technical assistance or to build AI literacy into a discipline; a showcase is planned for May 2027 ([Elon University, 22 September](https://www.elon.edu/u/news/2026/09/22/fields-initiative-kicks-off-as-faculty-teams-explore-teaching-in-the-age-of-artificial-intelligence/)). No improved-learning result is claimed yet. The lesson for international schools is organisational rather than collegiate: a central policy cannot decide every department's authentic work, but leaving every teacher alone with the question is also a design choice. Small subject teams, time on the calendar and a shared record of what they tried would produce more usable guidance than another platform demonstration. The May showcase is the point to check whether the programme reports changed tasks and evidence of learning, rather than a catalogue of clever prompts.

- **Google made AI video generation broader, while the school entitlement remains specific.** Google announced on 23 September that Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash is in Google Vids, with scene extension, duration control and 1080p generation or upscaling. Its Workspace release note says the rollout starts on 23 September and takes one to three days; the education eligibility list names Education Plus and the Google AI Pro for Education or Teaching and Learning add-ons. Google's consumer blog says clips generated with this model carry an imperceptible SynthID watermark, and it advertises no-cost creation for Google-account holders ([Workspace release note](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/gemini-omni-11-flash-now-in-vids-with-improved-extension-quality-1080p-and-duration-control.html); [Google product post, 23 September](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/workspace/gemini-omni-in-google-vids/)). Those are different claims from unlimited school-managed access. For a media class, smoother video is useful material for teaching editing and disclosure, not proof that a generated scene is documentary evidence. At international schools, test entitlement and generation limits on a managed teacher account before designing a lesson around it; keep the source-media and attribution rules explicit even when the exported clip has a machine-readable mark.

- **Anthropic's biology claim has a laboratory step and a conspicuous unknown.** On 23 September the company described Claude agents searching DNA-sequence data for unusual reverse transcriptase systems. Anthropic says roughly 950 agents used 210 million tokens over 21 hours, and that human scientists subsequently tested a candidate they call array-associated reverse transcriptases. The team says the system's function is still unknown and has released a preprint; the announcement is an early result, not a demonstrated gene-editing application ([Anthropic, 23 September](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-discovers-novel-enzyme-system)). That combination is more instructive for science teaching than the headline that AI “discovered” a biological machine. A candidate emerges from database search, then domain experts check the sequence, run experiments and publish methods so others can challenge the interpretation. Science teachers could use the sequence as a case study in hypothesis formation and verification, while being clear that the reported work is vendor-led, unreviewed as a journal article and not evidence that an unsupervised model can run a wet lab.

- **Anthropic's new Opus tier lowers the price of a model that can carry out longer work.** Claude Opus 5.5 was announced on 22 September at US$4 per million input tokens and US$20 per million output tokens, each 20% below Opus 5, while cache reads fall to US$0.20 per million. Anthropic estimates a 40% lower cost for typical workloads because of token efficiency as well as those prices; that is its test, not a guarantee for every school's task. The release describes improved performance in agentic coding and knowledge work and stronger prompt-injection resistance in its own evaluations ([Anthropic, 22 September](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-opus-5-5)). The school relevance lies in the combined trend: a longer-running assistant becomes cheaper to operate before organisations have necessarily specified what it may read or change. At international schools, that argues for costing a bounded workflow per completed task and checking its permission boundary and output quality; a price cut alone is not a reason to grant an agent access to student records or publishing tools.

## On the radar

- **A higher-education association is building an AI-readiness self-assessment rather than a product list.** The National Association of Higher Education Systems announced its advisory board on 22 September, after a July launch, with five workgroups spanning teaching, student success, data and privacy, governance, and operations. Its promised public outputs are a self-assessment, implementation playbook and case studies; they are still being developed ([NASH, 22 September](https://nash.edu/2026/09/nash-launches-first-national-ai-advisory-board-built-exclusively-for-public-higher-education-systems/)). This is a university-system initiative, so its scale will not transfer whole to a school. Watch whether the released instruments ask who owns each decision, what evidence changes a permission and how an institution retires a failed pilot; those questions would travel.

- **Google now lets administrator roles expire automatically.** Its 23 September Workspace update says super administrators can give users, groups or service accounts a role for a defined period, including a preset such as 30 days or a custom expiry, after which access is revoked. The primary administrator must retain permanent super-admin rights; the feature is listed as available now to all Workspace customers ([Google Workspace Updates, 23 September](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/assign-temporary-administrator-roles-in-the-Google-Admin-console.html)). This is a useful external control to watch when schools give short-term vendor or project access. It does not answer what permissions a temporary role should contain, and expiry is no substitute for reviewing the actions taken while access was live.

## Sceptic's corner

This week's seductive phrase is **“grounded in trusted sources.”** Google's new Docs integration promises inline citations from a selected Notebook, and its study-notebook announcement says lessons can be grounded in uploaded class material ([Google Docs release note](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/ground-ai-prompts-in-google-docs-on-existing-sources-from-Gemini-Notebook.html); [study-notebook release note](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/study-notebooks-in-gemini-are-now-available-for-Google-Workspace-accounts.html)). The improvement is real: a teacher can inspect a named passage, and a student can work within the curriculum rather than a random web answer. But a source is not trusted because somebody uploaded it, nor is a sentence true because a citation sits at its end. A stale rubric, a mistaken exemplar or a permission mistake is now capable of being repeated across many polished documents and personalised lessons.

The operational test is less glamorous than the demonstration. Who chose the source set? Who can edit it? When was each document last reviewed? Does the citation actually support the *specific* claim in the output? Can a learner challenge the answer and still see the original text? CUNY's new checklist insists on independent ideation and verification before accepting AI material ([CUNY, 22 September](https://www.cuny.edu/blog/ai-ethical-checklist-one-facultys-quick-guide-for-higher-education/)); those acts become more important when the interface feels academically dressed. For international schools, a notebook should have a named curricular owner and a review date before it becomes the default context for classwork. The staffroom line: grounding is a way to make errors *traceable*, not a certificate that the errors are gone.
