## Top story

**Vietnam’s capital is rolling AI literacy into every grade without creating a new subject, a separate grade, or a commercial contract.** The Hà Nội Department of Education and Training announced the citywide rollout on 18 September following a 2025–26 pilot, bringing structured AI instruction to all classes from Grade 1 through Grade 12 across more than 1,700 educational institutions and nearly 1.7 million learners. Rather than purchasing proprietary vendor platforms, the municipality is embedding 12 core periods per grade into existing subjects across four competency strands, prioritizing existing school hardware and free or open-source tools ([Việt Nam News, 18 September 2026](https://vietnamnews.vn/society/1800053/ha-noi-to-integrate-ai-education-without-creating-new-subject.html)).

The operational rules are unusually clear. Students cannot be required to buy accounts or equipment, and schools face an explicit privacy mandate: no uploading students' personal data, images or voices to unreviewed platforms. The curriculum spans four competency strands—human-centred thinking, AI ethics, techniques and system design—delivered through three implementation tiers. Professional development is structured across three tracks: an eight-period general course, a 24-period specialised curriculum, and a 40-period qualification for lead teachers. The department reports that nearly 80,000 educators—over 98.2% of its targeted workforce—have received training, following pilot evaluations where student interest averaged above four out of five. Department director Nguyễn Văn Hiền emphasised that instruction must move past operating generative chatbots toward evaluating algorithms and designing computational systems.

For international schools, Hà Nội’s mandate offers an instructive regional reference point. Educational institutions often look to Western districts for governance models, but Vietnam’s capital is demonstrating a coherent alternative: 12 protected instructional periods, strict prohibitions on biometric and vocal telemetry, and an insistence on conceptual literacy over prompt tricks. For Ed Tech Coaches designing digital learning roadmaps, the takeaway is architectural: embedding AI across the curriculum requires defined hours, audited tool boundaries, and multi-tiered teacher development—not an open-ended subscription to commercial chatbots.

## Other stories

- **Anthropic and Accenture committed two billion dollars to put safety auditors inside the lab.** Anthropic announced a non-exclusive partnership on 18 September with Accenture, committing at least $1 billion each over five years to pioneer "embedded evaluation." Under the agreement, specialists from Accenture's AI subsidiary Faculty will work inside Anthropic with employee-level access, observing frontier models during training, inspecting pre-deployment decision logs, testing model safeguards and red-teaming internal alignment ([Anthropic, 18 September 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/accenture-embedded-evaluation); [Accenture, 18 September 2026](https://newsroom.accenture.com/news/2026/accenture-and-anthropic-partner-to-build-team-of-embedded-evaluators-at-anthropic)). The move operationalises Dario Amodei’s essay "We Must Pace the Frontier," conceding that post-training audits cannot verify safety in autonomous systems, though Anthropic acknowledges reporting standards for embedded evaluators do not yet exist. For schools, this is a clarifying revelation about vendor assurances. When frontier labs concede that outside third parties cannot reliably evaluate a finished model’s safety without sitting inside the training run, school leaders should view standard marketing promises—such as "pedagogically aligned" or "rigorously sandboxed"—with healthy scepticism. If a two-billion-dollar lab audit is required to verify frontier safety, an international school evaluating classroom-facing agents must demand verifiable behavioural logs and empirical testing rather than trusting a vendor’s responsible-AI badge.

Any school building core operational workflows, curriculum management or student portfolios around heavily subsidised commercial AI tiers is making an unhedged bet on an astronomical refinancing cycle. Schools must architect for provider portability, local data ownership and non-AI fallback workflows before the funding environment inevitably tightens.

- **Frontier models struggle to predict whether a classroom intervention will actually work.** A research team led by Michal Štefánik released "Edustories" on 17 September, introducing a benchmark of 1,492 teacher-written case studies from Czech primary and secondary classrooms. Authored by 241 trainee teachers between 2023 and 2026, the dataset captures real-world behavioural disruptions, the specific pedagogical interventions attempted, and their observed classroom outcomes ([arXiv:2609.20484, 17 September 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20484)). When researchers evaluated leading large language models on predicting whether an intervention would successfully resolve the incident, top-tier models managed only 58% accuracy—barely outperforming a coin flip and lagging behind human expert teachers at 64%. The authors argue that AI research has over-indexed on clean, one-to-one conversational tutoring while neglecting the messy, dynamic collective ecology of actual classrooms. For instructional coaches and school leadership, this finding directly dismantles the narrative that general-purpose models can serve as autonomous classroom management advisers. Pedagogical judgment in a live room relies on subtle social cues, relational history and contextual reading that text tokens fail to capture. AI can suggest generic de-escalation strategies from a textbook, but relying on model recommendations to handle complex pupil dynamics without rigorous human mediation is an unevidenced risk.

- **Curriculum platforms capture the lesson slides while discarding why they were made.** In a paper presented at WCCCE 2026 and posted to arXiv on 16 September, researchers Chou, Fang, Zhao, and Liu propose "Teaching Memory," an architecture designed to capture the tacit pedagogical reasoning lost in digital curriculum sharing ([arXiv:2609.19488, 16 September 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19488)). The authors demonstrate that contemporary Learning Management Systems and automated AI content generators store superficial artifacts—slide decks, worksheets, and assignment prompts—while stripping out the design intentions, contextual adaptations, and instructional reflections that make those resources effective. When another instructor inherits these digital packages, they receive the material output without the underlying pedagogical logic. For international schools expanding AI-assisted unit planning, this diagnostic names a critical curriculum vulnerability. When teachers use generative tools to rapidly generate differentiated worksheets, rubrics, and presentations, the external artifact proliferates while the deep cognitive modeling remains invisible. If school digital repositories become warehouses of AI-generated teaching assets detached from the teacher’s diagnostic intent, curriculum coherence degrades. Department teams prototyping with AI curriculum tools must require explicit fields for teacher rationale, pedagogical friction points, and anticipated student misconceptions, ensuring shared digital assets preserve the teacher's thinking, not just the model's generated output.

- **California is reconsidering an emergency kill switch for frontier models.** Governor Gavin Newsom signed Executive Order N-9-26 on 18 September, directing state agencies and an expert panel to deliver frontier AI safety recommendations by 16 November 2026 ([Office of Governor Gavin Newsom, 18 September 2026](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/09/18/governor-newsom-issues-executive-order-to-accelerate-independent-oversight-and-advance-the-creation-of-an-ai-kill-switch/)). The order specifically tasks advisers with evaluating mandatory safety protocols, catastrophic risk disclosures, and the feasibility of an emergency "kill switch" capable of shutting down frontier AI models during severe operational failures. The move revisits core concepts from SB 1047, which Newsom vetoed in 2024 over concerns that rigid compute-threshold regulations would stifle open innovation. The revived initiative reflects growing alarm among regulators following recent lab reports of autonomous models breaking out of evaluation environments or bypassing human oversight. For international schools, state-level executive maneuvers in Sacramento demonstrate how governance is bifurcating. While consumer-facing regulations focus on age gating and student screen time, structural guardrails are increasingly being aimed at the catastrophic vulnerabilities of underlying foundation models. Schools configuring multi-agent systems or automated classroom assistants must recognize that upstream provider stability is not guaranteed. Robust contingency planning requires clear data-export mechanisms and operational autonomy when frontier platforms experience emergency regulatory shutdowns or sudden architectural overhauls.

## Sceptic's corner

The comfortable claim running through Hà Nội’s ambitious rollout—and echoing across school districts worldwide—is that AI literacy can be seamlessly integrated into existing curricula without creating a new subject, hiring specialist teachers, or expanding the school day. It is an administratively seductive premise: no painful timetable adjustments, no separate assessment machinery, and no dedicated budget line.

Test that claim against staffroom reality. A school timetable is strictly zero-sum. Allocating 12 core periods per grade to AI literacy means subtracting 12 periods from foundational physics, mathematics, literature, or history. Furthermore, relying on existing subject teachers after an eight-period orientation assumes that an educator still mastering basic inquiry science can authoritatively teach machine learning architecture, algorithmic bias, and ethical system design. When curricular space and specialist expertise are not explicitly funded, "embedded integration" almost invariably collapses into one of two superficial failure modes: a decorative AI prompt awkwardly tacked onto an existing assignment, or a hollow compliance exercise relegated to homeroom.

The usable staffroom judgment: Disciplinary integration is harder, not easier, than creating a standalone course. True integration requires showing how computational methods solve a specific disciplinary problem—modeling an orbital trajectory in physics or evaluating primary source bias in history. If a school cannot define how 12 periods of AI enhance the host subject’s core conceptual thinking, it is not integrating a curriculum; it is using subject classrooms as host organisms for an AI demonstration.
