# Week ending Sunday, 2 August 2026

## The Week That Was

The date everyone had circled is today. From **2 August 2026** the European Commission can finally *enforce* the AI Act against general-purpose model providers — demanding documentation, running evaluations, and fining a lab up to 3% of global annual turnover or €15 million, whichever is higher — and the Article 50 transparency duties, chatbot disclosure and AI-content marking among them, go live alongside ([EU AI Act enforcement](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/enforcement-of-chapter-v-under-the-eu-ai-act/)). What did *not* land today is the tell: the high-risk education obligations once due now were pushed to December 2027 by last Monday's Digital Omnibus ([Reg 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng)). The model-makers got two more years; the clauses pointing at the classroom didn't move.

Otherwise: Opus 5 turned the reasoning budget into a dial, the Hugging Face breach ran all week, and a quiet stack of student-wellbeing research piled up — most of it, as it happens, pointing the same way.

## What Does It All Mean?

The question the week left on the desk is a plain one. **If a chatbot now reliably produces the finished artifact — the essay, the lab write-up, the worked solution — what is a grade on that artifact actually measuring?**

Start with the week's most quietly damning pairing. A late-July Sungkyunkwan University study of 88 students found that those who used AI during problem-solving outscored classmates who used it only to understand concepts — yet the heaviest AI-reliant students posted the *lowest* real test scores, all while reporting markedly higher "perceived learning" and satisfaction ([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-uncovering-multidimensional-effects-generative-ai.html)). Performance up, learning down, and — the dangerous part — the learner's own gauge reading backwards.

It is not a new effect. Fan and colleagues, in the British Journal of Educational Technology, gave it a name: "metacognitive laziness." Their ChatGPT group wrote the best essays of any condition and showed no better knowledge gain or transfer than students left to struggle ([Monash / BJET](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/beware-of-metacognitive-laziness-effects-of-generative-artificial/)).

Now the mirror at the other end of the sophistication scale. Last week's cryptography headline was really about verification. Anthropic reported a model producing a novel attack in about a week, then noted that "the vast majority of our time over the past few months has been in verifying the correctness of Claude's results" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/discovering-cryptographic-weaknesses)). Matthew Green's gloss: "just because a model spits out an apparent new result, this does not mean the result is real" ([Green](https://blog.cryptographyengineering.com/2026/07/29/some-notes-about-anthropics-new-results/)). Generation got cheap; verification did not. A frontier lab with unlimited money now spends its scarce hours checking, not producing.

That is precisely the asymmetry the students walked into from below. When the fluent output is nearly free, the entire value — and the entire difficulty — migrates into the checking: knowing whether the answer is right, where it is thin, what it quietly assumed. Which drops the assessment problem on the desk. A task scored on the polish of its output now measures the tool's capability, not the student's judgement, and this week's evidence says the two have come apart.

The instinctive fix — detection — is the wrong one, and the field is already conceding it, shifting weight toward in-person, oral and process-based assessment ([Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/depth/are-universities-returning-person-exams-combat-ai-cheating)). The better move follows from the diagnosis: grade the verification layer — the interrogation of the output — because that is the thing AI cannot hand over, and the thing the crypto lab just confirmed is now the whole job. And it all lands into a governance vacuum. Stanford's 2026 AI Index found four in five students using AI while just 6% of teachers call their school's policy even clear ([Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/education)).

## Behind You! Behind You!

**The rigorous study the week's adoption surveys drowned out.** Amid the usual "teachers save weeks with AI" reporting, a Wharton team — Alp Sungu, Benjamin Lira Luttges and Angela Duckworth — ran the thing this field rarely gets: a randomized controlled trial. Across 193 teachers and some 2,800 students in a Turkish school chain, giving teachers a GPT-4o assistant left students *less* motivated — classes rated less interesting, less important — and lowered exam scores for students whose teachers were already weaker ([FutureEd](https://www.future-ed.org/when-teachers-rely-on-ai-student-engagement-drops-study-finds/)). Sungu's read: teachers "might be using AI as a crutch ... and that lowers the quality of their teaching." A 2025 working paper, not yet peer-reviewed — but a kind of evidence the cheerful adoption counts are not.

**A tool students use changed underneath them.** Google renamed NotebookLM to Gemini Notebook (rollout from 16 July), moved it onto Gemini 3.5, and — the part worth flagging — now runs each notebook on its own secure cloud computer that can write and execute code for deeper analysis ([Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/07/notebooklm-now-gemini-notebook.html)). A research aid that has quietly gained a code interpreter is a different data-governance object than the one on last term's approved list.

**The half of the Classroom redesign that actually touches students.** The new role-based Classroom homepage was noted mostly for its dashboards; the under-reported half is the AI layer. From 27 July, students hovering over an assignment get a "Learn with Gemini" menu of study actions, and a "Class learning tools" module surfaces teacher-assigned Gemini notebooks and Gems — appearing only where Gemini is switched on for the org unit ([Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/07/redesigned-google-classroom-homepage-with-tailored-views-based-on-users-role.html)). Gemini is now one hover from every assignment.

## The Riff Returns But Slower

- **10 August** — OpenAI's Student Collective campus-lead applications close. The schools version of the ambassador model is coming; worth a settled view before it arrives.
- **2 December 2026** — the EU AI Act's two new intimate-imagery prohibitions bite, alongside machine-readable marking of general-purpose systems already on the market. The high-risk education obligations now wait until 2 December 2027.
- **"Coming weeks"** — OpenAI's promised technical report on the Hugging Face breach. The document to actually read, against the four gaps observers already named: disclosure thresholds, containment standards, unmonitored monitoring, and a capability-over-defence bias.
- **Slow burn** — the open-weight frontier keeps cheapening. Kimi K3 and Switzerland's multimodal Apertus 1.5 both landed this week; the visible effect for schools is downward pressure on hosted-inference prices, not anything a school would self-host.
- **Slow burn** — the evidence base is thin but no longer only correlational: it now holds a genuine RCT (above), resurfaced by this week's coverage. Watch whether the autumn's full Jed Foundation report brings method to match.

## Sceptic's Corner

If you read only the safety headlines, this was the week the grown-ups arrived. NVIDIA convened a 37-member Open Secure AI Alliance ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/07/nvidia-forms-37-member-open-secure-ai.html)) and more than 1,100 frontier-lab employees signed a letter asking Washington to help "pace" AI development ([Trending Topics](https://www.trendingtopics.eu/1100-employees-at-openai-anthropic-meta-and-google-call-for-ai-slowdown/)). Microsoft began paying eighteen university labs to red-team its models ([Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/07/27/enhancing-ai-security-through-global-ai-red-teaming/)), the EU's enforcement powers over model-makers went live today ([EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/enforcement-of-chapter-v-under-the-eu-ai-act/)), and even the connector plumbing hardened with a security-focused revision of the MCP standard ([Model Context Protocol](https://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2026-07-28/)). A staffroom optimist could reasonably conclude the guardrails are finally going up.

Now stand where a school stands. Every one of those mechanisms points at the vendor, the frontier lab, or the government — and not one touches the two AI risks that are actually inside a school this term. The first is deepfakes of classmates: Common Sense Media found 44% of teenagers have seen AI-generated sexual imagery, and a quarter of those said it depicted them or someone they know ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/teens-and-explicit-deepfakes-in-the-age-of-ai)). The second is quiet over-reliance: the Jed Foundation found nearly three in five students turn to AI when sad, stressed or lonely ([Jed Foundation](https://jedfoundation.org/the-jed-foundation-jed-releases-preliminary-research-on-how-teens-are-using-artificial-intelligence-ai-and-what-it-means-for-their-mental-health/)). No alliance, letter or enforcement power that shipped this week moves either number.

The asymmetry underneath is worse than an oversight. The offensive capability escaped a lab a fortnight ago ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/21/openai-says-hugging-face-was-breached-by-its-pre-release-models/)); the defensive Gemini model built to find vulnerabilities is gated behind a government whitelist ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/21/google-releases-three-new-gemini-models-but-no-3-5-pro/)); and the four companies actually building the agents arriving on education tiers — OpenAI, Google, Meta, Anthropic — were the names missing from that alliance. The scaffolding is real, but it is going up around the vendor floor of the building, several storeys from the classroom.

So the usable judgement: a school waiting for "the industry to sort out safety" before it writes a deepfake-incident protocol or a pathway for the student talking to a chatbot at one in the morning is waiting for the wrong contractor. The two controls that matter this term are ones the school writes itself — and nothing that made the safety headlines this week gets them written.
