## Top story

**A school-AI standard has acquired contractual teeth—and a boundary schools must read before celebrating.** The American Federation of Teachers, United Federation of Teachers and Microsoft announced a 31-page AI Safety & Privacy Standard on 9 September. Microsoft says that, from 1 November, every US school district can add its protections to a new or existing Microsoft agreement without waiting for renegotiation or renewal. The standard contains ten binding principles: no model training on student or educator data except for a narrow safety-and-security purpose; data minimisation; customer ownership, export and deletion controls; human review for consequential decisions; security and breach response; family-facing explanations; accessibility and independent study; protection against feature creep and lock-in; and duties that survive the contract. Its fact sheet adds usable numbers: providers must report breaches within 72 hours, while active and backup deletion cycles may run up to 180 days ([AFT, September 2026](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/2026/NAfAI-School_AI_Privacy_Standards.pdf); [Microsoft and AFT, 9 September 2026](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2026/09/09/aft-uft-and-microsoft-announce-national-ai-safety-privacy-standard-for-schools-to-protect-students-families-and-educators/)).

This is more serious than another voluntary principles page. The framework is a binding memorandum between the National Academy for AI Instruction and a signing provider; an opted-in district gets a contract against which that provider can be held. It reaches prompts, outputs, files, behavioural signals, metadata, memory, location, audio, video and biometrics. Persistent memory must be visible, erasable and switchable; high-risk uses require meaningful human review; AI companions are prohibited; and material new features trigger a privacy-impact assessment and customer notice. The two-year agreement must be renewed, and providers must make the protections available to eligible US education customers within 90 days of signing ([AFT, September 2026](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/2026/NAfAI-School_AI_Privacy_Standards.pdf)).

Now read the definitions. Coverage is limited to authenticated products primarily designed and marketed for students, educators or administrators. The document expressly excludes general-purpose productivity, collaboration, communication, search, cloud, development and workplace-assistance products, even when an education organisation licenses them. It also defines eligible customers as US educational entities. That leaves a potentially large gap around the very assistants, browsers, office suites and agent tools through which everyday school AI is arriving. For international schools, the standard is not a ready-made legal shield; it is a sharp procurement challenge. Ask each supplier to map every deployed feature to the clause that covers it, name every exclusion, state the data region and deletion clock, and explain what happens when a general-purpose feature becomes student-facing. The headline is contract enforceability. The work is coverage.

## Other stories

- **UNESCO’s ministerial statement has moved yesterday’s consultation lane from intention to an adopted eight-part position.** More than 25 ministers and designated representatives adopted the statement in Paris on 8 September; UNESCO published its account on 9 September. The priorities include auditable and portable systems under public oversight, teacher participation in adoption decisions, developmentally appropriate use, learner and teacher data rights, multilingual and inclusive evaluation, total-cost accounting, interoperability and pooled regional governance capacity. Providers are asked to show evidence of educational benefit before deployment and accept accountability for harm ([UNESCO, 9 September 2026](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/education-ministers-call-education-remain-common-good-age-ai-unescos-digital-learning-week?hub=722)). That is a useful procurement spine, especially because it explicitly covers free tiers and donations. It is still a joint statement, not an enforcement regime: the announcement does not name signatory-by-signatory obligations, budgets or deadlines. Use its eight tests as questions now; do not report them as protections already delivered.

- **Google has added a real access-control layer to Gemini Enterprise, but access control is not learning governance.** From 8 September, Workspace administrators on eligible plans can apply context-aware access policies to Gemini Enterprise by organisational unit or group, using signals such as device security and geographic location across personal and managed devices. The gradual rollout is scheduled to complete by 15 September; Education Standard and Plus are eligible, but the school must also have purchased Gemini Enterprise ([Google Workspace Updates, 8 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/context-aware-access-controls-are-available-for-Gemini-Enterprise-in-the-Admin-console.html)). For Ed Tech coaches, this is worth placing beside age and role controls: the domain can say that a tool works for this cohort only from a compliant device or permitted location. It still says nothing about which task deserves AI, what source data a user should supply, or what evidence of learning must remain. Security policy can narrow the doorway. It cannot design the lesson.

- **A 19-school implementation study found the scarce resource was collective teacher judgement, not another demo.** Digital Promise reports on a school-year partnership around Coursemojo involving 16 instructional coaches and 41 middle-school English teachers across more than three dozen classrooms, 19 schools and three sites. Interviews, focus groups, activity logs and co-design sessions found uneven fidelity—from intended use to little use—and a recurring need for protected collaboration time, early inclusion of special-education and multilingual-learner colleagues, alignment with existing priorities, and teacher scaffolding when pupils interpreted AI feedback ([Digital Promise, 9 September 2026](https://digitalpromise.org/2026/09/09/implementing-ai-in-schools-what-teachers-need-to-make-it-work/)). This is implementation evidence, not impact evidence: the article supplies no comparative learning outcome, and the tool developer was part of the partnership. Still, the design lesson travels. A small school pilot should fund shared planning and review time, record non-use as data, and judge whether feedback changes revision—not merely whether the feature ran.

- **England’s qualifications regulator is drawing a bright line between AI assistance and AI judgement.** In oral evidence given on 8 September, Ofqual’s chief regulator said AI may support quality assurance or help identify anomalous marking, but expert humans should retain sophisticated high-stakes judgements on extended writing and artistic work; he did not expect unassisted AI marking to be permitted soon and questioned whether it ever should be. In the same hearing, a teachers’ union representative said roughly three quarters of surveyed members had used AI at work but only 7% reported an overall workload reduction ([UK Parliament Education Committee, 8 September 2026](https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/18040/html/)). The workload figures are testimony without the survey instrument or sample details in the transcript, and the regulatory position concerns England. The distinction is nevertheless useful for schools: machine assistance may surface a case for review; it should not become the unappealable reviewer.

- **Singapore Management University is sequencing an agentic-AI rollout faculty first and course by course.** Its law school announced on 9 September that it is the first Asian member of Legora’s Legal AI Scholars Program. Faculty receive licences and training from the vendor’s regional engineering team and the university library before student access expands only where faculty identify a curricular fit; proposed uses mirror professional work in legal research, document review and drafting ([Singapore Management University, 9 September 2026](https://news.smu.edu.sg/news/2026/09/09/singapore-management-university-partners-legora-bring-agentic-ai-its-law-school)). The discipline-specific, staged architecture is more credible than a campus-wide switch-on, and it offers an APAC example of professional fluency being embedded inside existing courses. But this is a partnership announcement from the partners, with no learning, integrity or workload results yet. The sequence is worth borrowing; the success claim is not yet available.

## On the radar

- **Coursera’s Project Helix is a platform preview, not a shipped learning system.** Announced on 9 September after Coursera combined with Udemy in May, it proposes one enterprise layer joining adaptive paths, labour-market signals, continuous assessment, practical observation, credentials and a portable skills record across more than 30,000 instructors and content partners. Broad availability is only expected in the first half of 2027 ([Coursera, 9 September 2026](https://investor.coursera.com/news/news-details/2026/Coursera-Announces-Project-Helix-a-New-AI-Native-Platform-Connecting-Skills-Discovery-and-Personalized-Learning-to-Verified-Capability-and-Business-Outcomes/default.aspx)). Watch whether portability and verified capability survive contact with product terms, employer data and actual interoperability.

- **MIT’s AI Educators Pilot has a sensible build pattern and no outcome evidence yet.** Nineteen higher-education instructors from seven institutions spent a week in July adapting modelling-with-machine-learning materials to their own disciplines through demonstrations, exercises and collaborative redesign; participant feedback will shape later iterations and a broader educator network ([MIT, 9 September 2026](https://news.mit.edu/2026/mit-schwarzman-college-computing-launches-pilot-help-educators-teach-ai-across-disciplines-0909)). Watch for the resulting course artefacts and classroom evidence, not the workshop photo.

- **A UNESCO regional survey puts a number on the governance lag.** Its new study covers 200 higher-education institutions in 19 Latin American and Caribbean countries: 87% reported AI use somewhere, 74% in teaching and learning, but only 26% reported a formal AI strategy ([UNESCO IESALC, 9 September 2026](https://www.iesalc.unesco.org/en/articles/new-unesco-iesalc-study-reveals-widespread-ai-adoption-higher-education-across-latin-america-and)). Watch the full methods and institution-level variation before turning a regional institutional survey into a universal maturity benchmark.

## Sceptic's corner

The seductive phrase this morning is national standard. It sounds like a law, a certification and a blanket product guarantee rolled into two words. It is none of those things. It is a contract framework from a union-led academy, signed by Microsoft, that US districts can request for a defined class of education products. Its strongest clauses are genuinely strong. Its perimeter is just as real.

That perimeter matters because school AI no longer arrives only in a product labelled for schools. It arrives in office suites, search, browsers, general assistants, coding environments, LMS plug-ins and staff workflow tools. The agreement deliberately excludes several of those general-purpose categories. A provider could therefore satisfy the standard for one named education product while a school’s practical exposure sits elsewhere. The right due-diligence question is not whether the vendor supports the standard. It is which exact service, feature, account type and data flow are contractually inside it.

UNESCO’s newly adopted statement points in the same direction from the public-interest side: portability, auditability, evidence before deployment, total ownership cost and the ability to change course. Put the two together and the test becomes pleasantly unglamorous. Produce a feature inventory. Mark the contract coverage. Record the retention and deletion route. Name the human who owns each consequential decision. Define the educational evidence required to continue. If a supplier cannot answer at that resolution, a badge, standard or ministerial endorsement elsewhere in the brochure is decoration.
