## Top story

**England wants edtech suppliers to show their safety work.** The Department for Education [opened a consultation on 10 November](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/introducing-an-edtech-and-ai-product-safety-certification-scheme) on independent safety certification for education technology and AI used in English schools and other education settings. The department wants defined standards, a proportionate way for suppliers to show they meet them, and less duplicated work for education settings making purchasing decisions. The [consultation closes on 4 February 2027](https://consult.education.gov.uk/edtech-regulation-policy-team/education-technology-safety-assurance-certificatio/consult_view/). The government announced it [alongside stronger phone-free school guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/strengthened-guidance-to-take-the-next-leap-to-phone-free-schools), in the same statement that asks schools to keep phones inaccessible throughout the school day.

The design question is what, exactly, gets certified. A school can license a product for teachers, leave student accounts off and keep outside sharing disabled. Another school can buy the same licence, connect it to its learning platform and let students upload work. The supplier's name on the invoice is identical. The information the tool can see, the people it can contact and the decisions it can influence are different. A useful certificate would state which ages, features, settings and data flows the testing covered, and what change would trigger a new review. Otherwise a vendor can change a feature in February while a school is still relying on the evidence it saw in October.

Ed Tech Coaches can test the proposed scope by following an ordinary assignment from the learning platform into a separately licensed tool. A teacher should be able to see whether a tool can keep student uploads, whether those uploads are visible to others, and who is expected to intervene when a student reports a problem. Evidence that answers those questions once could spare dozens of schools the same round of vendor emails. A school would still need to decide whether its chosen configuration matches what was tested and whether the product serves its curriculum.

## Other stories

- **Brazil spells out decisions that stay with educators.** On 10 November, the Ministry of Education [announced its approval of the National Education Council's opinion](https://www.gov.br/secom/pt-br/acompanhe-a-secom/noticias/2026/11/governo-federal-estabelece-diretrizes-para-uso-responsavel-da-ia-na-educacao) covering public and private institutions across educational levels. The framework sets four risk levels, with classification taking account of the intended use, data involved, autonomy and potential impact on students. It would rule out automated emotion inference, AI grading of essays and other authored work that requires interpretive judgement, and sanctions based solely on a detector. It would also restrict direct, autonomous or unsupervised student access through Grade 5, with planned, educator-mediated use allowed. The government says the prohibitions apply upon publication of the resolution, with 12 months from that point to adapt policies, governance and contracts. The useful distinction for a school writing its own policy is between what a tool can do and what staff may delegate to it. A system that drafts a practice exercise and a system that grades a student's argument both generate text. Only one of those actions determines what the student's work is worth.

- **England's higher-education regulator asks what an assessment proves.** The Office for Students and Advance HE [published findings on 11 November](https://www.officeforstudents.org.uk/publications/making-ai-work-for-students-reflections-on-institutional-practice/) based on conversations with more than 300 students and staff from over 120 institutions, primarily in England. Participants described confusion about permitted AI use, and the report warns that high-quality submissions from unsupervised tasks may tell assessors little about the student's own knowledge. The regulator [says it will require valid, reliable assessment](https://www.officeforstudents.org.uk/news-blog-and-events/press-and-media/universities-should-help-students-develop-ai-skills-while-protecting-confidence-in-degrees/), with secure, AI-free assessment needed in many cases. It also expects courses to develop relevant skills, and the report allows for carefully designed assessments that incorporate AI. In secondary school the distinction can fit inside one project. An observed source analysis can show whether a student can weigh evidence independently. A later task can ask the same student to critique an AI-generated analysis and account for every correction. The two scores then tell a teacher two different things. The report is a qualitative account from higher education, so its value here is in the assessment design rather than a prediction of how often school pupils use AI.

- **Google's new frontier model comes with an introductory price and an expiry date.** Google [released Gemini 4 Carbon on 10 November](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-4-carbon-now-available/), with paid API access through Google AI Studio and Gemini for business. For the model intended for public access, Google lists US$2 per million input tokens and US$10 per million output tokens through 30 November, then US$4 and US$20 respectively. It also announced a consumer rollout to Google AI Ultra subscribers in the Gemini app and AI Mode in Search. That last route is a consumer entitlement, and the release gives no student Workspace entitlement. The billing change is the immediate story for anyone prototyping a school utility. Before a team switches the model behind a tool, run it on a small set of fictional school documents with known answers. Compare the cost per usable result with the current model, including retries and the time a teacher spends correcting the output. A cheap-looking first run in November is a poor basis for a December service budget.

- **The chemistry lab acquired a second voice, and anxiety rose.** In an [11 November paper in *Frontiers in Education*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1893180/full), Anshan Normal University researchers compared two Grade 9 classes at one secondary school in eastern China over 16 weeks. Forty students used an emotion-feedback system with cameras and voice sensors alongside inquiry-based chemistry instruction. Thirty-eight received the comparable instruction with conventional teacher interaction. The researchers report that self-rated lab anxiety increased in the AI-supported class and stayed stable in the comparison class. One student described uncertainty over whether to follow system prompts or the teacher's directions. The two-class quasi-experiment and reliance on self-report limit what the result can tell us about other schools or tools. It does give a teacher a concrete question to ask about any classroom system that claims to read how a pupil feels. When a student, the teacher and the system give different accounts of what's happening, who decides what happens next? That disagreement matters in a chemistry lab, where a pupil may need to listen for a timely safety instruction while also managing an unfamiliar procedure.

- **ASEAN's education figures put access and completion on different pages.** The UNESCO Institute for Statistics [summarised its regional report on 10 November](https://www.uis.unesco.org/en/news/education-asean-opening-school-doors-delivering-learning-all). It puts the primary out-of-school rate at roughly 14% in 2000 and 5% in 2023, with lower secondary falling from about 24% to 11% over the same period. Yet nearly one in three adolescents of upper secondary age was out of school in 2023, and median upper secondary completion in 2024 was about 55%. The institute also identifies inclusive digital transformation as part of widening access to lifelong learning. The figures describe different populations and measures, and the report points to gaps linked to geography, wealth and disability. A school working with a regional partner on digital learning can apply the same discipline at a smaller scale. Count who can access a resource, who manages to finish an activity and who can use what they learned. The third measure could be a short offline task. A platform's records may give the project a detailed picture of the people it reaches while leaving out the people with the weakest connection.

## On the radar

- **1EdTech puts repeatability on the agenda.** Its undated [listing for the 12 November webinar](https://www.1edtech.org/events/1edtech-labs-show-me-the-evidence-what-should-genai-in-education-have-to-prove) names Learning Data Insights founder John Whitmer and 1EdTech strategy lead Michael Feldstein. The organisers describe a review of more than 250 studies on scoring, question generation and feedback, with fairness rarely examined and repeated-run consistency examined still less often. That's the organisers' account of the review. The question worth listening for is what counts as a meaningful inconsistency. Two phrasings of a revision comment can help equally. Two scores for the same essay under the same rubric can leave a student with a different outcome because the teacher clicked twice.

- **Singapore's AI-for-education meeting opens next week.** The National Institute of Education at NTU [hosts the conference on 16–18 November](https://www.ntu.edu.sg/nie/news-events/events/detail/2026/11/16/default-calendar/ai-for-education-conference-2026). The organisers [released the schedule on 29 October](https://aife2026singapore.org/). The [Monday programme](https://easychair.org/smart-program/AIFE2026Singapore/2026-11-16.html) schedules a workshop led by UCL's Kaśka Porayska-Pomsta on examining ethics through the stages of AI development, and one from the University of Georgia's Xiaoming Zhai on agent-supported science learning. They offer two ways of asking where a lesson's decisions sit. In science, for example, an agent could help a pupil articulate a hypothesis, suggest one or choose a test for it. Each would leave the pupil with a different decision to make. The session materials may make those choices visible enough for a science department to discuss.

## Sceptic's corner

An authorship detector can get a high score on one task and leave a teacher guessing about the task that brought them to it. In a [9 November preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.12487v1), Nils-Jonathan Schaller and colleagues drew on a study alongside PISA 2025 in which 2,429 15-year-olds in German secondary schools wrote English argumentative essays, with or without access to a chatbot. The study logged keystrokes, clipboard actions and chat. That gave the authors an unusually good record of which words had come from where. On the comparison between human and AI text, the stronger of two research detectors reached a whole-text macro-F1 of 0.96. With mixed text and text written after chatbot use without traceable AI wording added to the AI class, the same detector scored 0.80. These are scores from two defined test sets, with one age group, two tasks and one chatbot in the underlying student study.

Picture the pupil who asks for a counterargument, dismisses the answer, then writes an essay independently. A detector sees the essay. A teacher can ask the pupil which counterargument they considered and why they rejected it. The two procedures recover different evidence. This is where the policy and the task brief do work that a classifier cannot. If the assignment permits brainstorming, a pupil should know how to record that kind of help. If the learning goal is to generate and evaluate counterarguments independently, an observed example gives the teacher evidence about that specific ability.
