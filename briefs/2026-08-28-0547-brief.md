## Top story

**AI made the answers more conventional; a short thinking game made the ideas less so.** A randomized trial released on 27 August assigned 1,053 first-year economics, management and finance students across 13 classes to one of four conditions: ChatGPT Edu access using GPT-4o, a short causal-reasoning exercise, both, or neither. Students then had 45 minutes to write a 180-word recommendation for increasing awareness and use of their university’s merchandise. ChatGPT access lifted the evaluators’ score by 0.862 points from an estimated control score of 2.09 on a five-point scale. It also increased the number of ideas and coherent logic, and made answers more similar to three expert recommendations ([Asirvatham et al., August 2026](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/novices-and-llm-august-2026.pdf); [OpenAI, 27 August 2026](https://openai.com/index/what-students-gain-from-chatgpt-critical-thinking-training/)).

The causal-reasoning exercise changed something the standard marketing rubric did not reward. It raised mechanism identification by about 0.55 standard deviations and falsification logic by about 0.85 SD; it also produced more diverse ideas within each answer and across the cohort. GPT alone expanded the volume of ideas but did not clearly increase between-student diversity. When the two treatments were combined, students retained the causal-reasoning gains while gaining the AI-assisted polish. This is useful evidence of complementarity, but not evidence of learning: the study measured one supervised output immediately after a short intervention, not retention, transfer or unaided performance.

For international schools, the uncomfortable part is the rubric. If a polished, conventional answer earns the marks while a mechanism-rich, falsifiable answer wanders outside the expected solution space, students are behaving rationally when they optimize for polish. A practical move for EdTech Coaches is to take one existing task and score the product, the reasoning trail and an AI-free oral or written transfer separately. Keep AI available where it helps students generate and organize; make originality, causal explanation and boundary conditions visible in the assessment. Otherwise the machine is not flattening thought on its own. The marking scheme is paying it to.

## Other stories

- **MIT has moved from course-by-course AI rules to questioning the structure of higher education.** A committee report released with an institute-wide letter on 25 August says MIT should create AI-aware educational processes, strengthen the residential and human community, and build continuous review rather than a one-off policy response. The immediate recommendations include re-evaluating assessment, re-emphasizing hands-on learning and giving every class an explicit AI-use policy suited to its purpose; the institute is also developing guidance, pilot funding and communities of practice ([MIT, 25 August 2026](https://orgchart.mit.edu/letters/ai-and-education-watershed-moment-mit)). The transfer to schools is not “copy MIT.” It is the governance cadence: a yearly policy cannot keep pace with capability, while teacher-by-teacher improvisation produces an incoherent student experience. Schools need a small, repeatable review loop—what students must know unaided, where AI is permitted, and what evidence will show the design still works—rather than a grand policy rewrite each time a model improves.

- **The Philippines is putting Copilot in front of one million public-school teachers, but the outcome evidence is still attached to a different tool.** Microsoft and the Department of Education announced on 27 August that teachers will receive access to Copilot, building on a training programme that Microsoft says drew nearly 75,000 live attendees and more than 175,000 registrations. The same release reports a 21-week Reading Progress pilot covering 3,431 students in eight regions: 8,381 reading submissions, more than 2,100 teachers trained, 91.5% teacher satisfaction and 780 reassessed students showing progress, including 319 who moved between Phil-IRI bands ([Microsoft Source Asia, 27 August 2026](https://news.microsoft.com/source/asia/2026/08/27/deped-and-microsoft-partner-to-advance-philippine-education-in-the-ai-era-equip-1m-teachers-with-microsoft-copilot/)). Those figures are vendor-published and do not establish a Copilot effect; Reading Progress is not generative lesson planning. Still, the regional scale matters. For international schools, the comparison case is implementation architecture: entitlement, training, approved tasks and measures need to be named separately, or usage and literacy gains will blur into one persuasive but untestable transformation story.

- **England’s filtering standard now treats generated content as something school systems must be able to see, not merely a website category to block.** Updated on 25 August, the Department for Education standard tells schools to consider whether their filtering and monitoring can handle real-time, dynamic, personalized and AI-generated content. Reviews should map where browser and in-app generative AI is used, identify technical limitations, include BYOD and managed devices, and be repeated at least annually or whenever new AI tools or major software changes arrive. It also assigns the work jointly to senior leadership, the safeguarding lead and IT support, with governors requiring assurance ([UK Department for Education, 25 August 2026](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/meeting-digital-and-technology-standards-in-schools-and-colleges/filtering-and-monitoring-core-standard)). That is a useful correction to the fantasy that a URL blocklist governs AI. A realistic school test is a matrix across web chat, embedded assistants, mobile apps and take-home devices, checked with known-good and known-bad prompts. Policy coverage without technical coverage is only a sentence.

- **A new Brookings review makes the case that school-surveillance procurement needs an error budget before it gets a safety budget.** Published on 27 August, it spans AI weapon-detection cameras, bathroom audio sensors, online-activity monitoring and predictive risk scoring. Its sharpest example is a 2025 Baltimore incident in which a student’s bag of chips triggered a possible-weapon alert; police were called and the 16-year-old was handcuffed before the alert was cleared. The review also cites research finding surveillance systems more common in large urban schools serving higher proportions of low-income and minority students, while student survey data show only 37% of Black and 40% of Hispanic secondary students felt safe from violence, compared with 53% of white students ([Brookings, 27 August 2026](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/ai-surveillance-in-schools-raises-safety-and-equity-concerns/)). Brookings is commentary, not a new effectiveness trial, but its procurement test travels: demand published error rates, independent validation, a data-sharing boundary and student consultation before a live false positive becomes the evaluation.

- **The most convincing AI-literacy lesson in a US back-to-school survey was a broken map, not a prompt framework.** Associated Press reporting published on 21 August describes Charleston County’s move from policy development and student focus groups into teacher and secondary-student training. An AI tool asked for a world map misspelled Mali as “Mail,” labelled Egypt “Sopth,” and replaced Libya with “Africa.” The district uses failures like this to teach verification, privacy and the limits of confident output. The wider reporting found 37 US states with official school AI guidance; Utah has trained more than 7,000 teachers, nearly one-third of its public-school workforce, and requires district AI policies by July 2027 ([Associated Press, 21 August 2026](https://apnews.com/article/ai-literacy-schools-education-4fb9f2c0240993499870f4f204bf41c1)). The useful grain for international schools is experiential: students should encounter a plausible failure, diagnose it, verify against authoritative sources and decide when not to use the tool. A literacy course that only teaches successful prompting is product induction wearing a curriculum badge.

## Sceptic's corner

OpenAI’s summary says ChatGPT produced better answers while causal training produced broader thinking. That is a fair description of the measured outputs, but “better” here means closer to a conventional answer on a tightly bounded marketing task. The paper itself reports that evaluators penalized mechanism identification and falsifiability because those features moved responses away from the typical solution space. ChatGPT did not merely help novices express an existing insight; on a topic well represented in its training data, it supplied ideas that matched what the rubric expected ([Asirvatham et al., August 2026](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/novices-and-llm-august-2026.pdf); [OpenAI, 27 August 2026](https://openai.com/index/what-students-gain-from-chatgpt-critical-thinking-training/)).

The study randomized 1,053 students in proctored sessions and used human evaluators. Its boundaries matter equally. The treatment covered 13 classes at one university, the task lasted 45 minutes, the output was capped at 180 words, 90.5% compliance with the AI condition was self-reported, and the institution and preregistration record were withheld to preserve anonymity. There was no delayed test and no AI-free transfer task. We know the combined group could produce polished, causally richer recommendations with the tool. We do not know what students learned.

The useful line is not “AI and thinking are complementary,” full stop. It is: AI can improve performance on a conventional product while a small cognitive intervention changes the kind of thinking inside it. If a school wants both, it must assess both—and then check what survives when the assistant leaves the room.
