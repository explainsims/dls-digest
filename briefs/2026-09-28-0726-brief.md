## Top story

**The most “pedagogical” AI tutor in a new trial was the one students liked least.** A preprint submitted on 24 September by researchers at the University of Florida and University of Auckland tested four versions of the same programming assistant with 132 introductory Python students. Each version combined one of two teaching styles—questions only, or brief direct explanations that still withheld complete solutions—with either full access to the problem and the student’s code or access only to the chat history. Students completed one code-writing and two debugging tasks, then rated the tool and explained a bug they had just fixed ([Eastwood and colleagues, arXiv, 24 September 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29995v1)).

The full-context, questions-only assistant scored significantly lowest on perceived help with task completion: 3.53 out of five, against 4.27 for the full-context direct assistant. Twenty-three per cent in that Socratic/full-context group reported going to another chatbot, versus nine to 15 per cent in the other groups; 48 per cent gave a fully comprehending explanation after the task, versus 67 per cent in direct/full-context. **Those latter contrasts were descriptive, not statistically significant**. The firm finding is narrower: this configuration felt less useful. Nor was this a school-year test of learning. The 132 were a selected subset of a university course, offered 0.5 per cent extra credit; comprehension was checked immediately on one debugging question, and the paper is not yet peer reviewed ([study methods and results](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29995v1)).

That distinction matters after last week's Tennessee Khanmigo trial. There, many middle-schoolers had access to a Socratic helper but rarely asked it for help when stuck ([Hechinger Report, 21 September 2026](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-khanmigo-math-ai-tutor/)). This new experiment tests a mechanism rather than a whole intervention: making a bot more context-aware and more committed to asking questions can still produce friction students experience as a dead end. A school does not need to decide that direct answers are superior. It needs to decide what students must do before and after help, and measure whether an approved helper is used at the moment of difficulty rather than escaped for an unrestricted one. A tutor that protects the answer but loses the learner is not a successful guardrail.

## Other stories

- **A California district is writing AI policy with students in the room, and the details are more useful than another principles poster.** Santa Barbara Unified’s task force met on 24 September with 11 students present and split its work into academic integrity, teacher guidance, data privacy and board policy. Local reporter Sofia Wallace observed that one student proposed treating AI-generated material as a source, while teachers argued for explicit disclosure thresholds, teacher control over classroom use and no detection software as the sole basis for an accusation. A member of the privacy workstream noted that the model policy it was adapting covered student data but overlooked teachers and staff ([Santa Barbara News-Press, 25 September 2026](https://www.newspress.com/2026/09/25/students-parents-teachers-want-their-voices-heard-on-artificial-intelligence-in-santa-barbara-schools/)). The district itself lists the committee’s remit as procurement and data privacy alongside academic integrity and professional learning; its next task-force date is 29 October, with a November policy target still a plan, not an adopted rule ([Santa Barbara Unified committee page](https://www.sbunified.org/about/committees)). The useful school-level pattern is to have students test disclosure language against actual assignments before a policy is signed. Otherwise “declare your use” is a sentence adults understand and teenagers interpret five different ways.

- **Chrome is putting an AI study workflow at the browser layer, outside any classroom-specific tool.** In a 24 September product post, Google said Gemini in Chrome can answer questions about audio and video after playback and generate interactive quizzes from the content of a tab. The quiz rollout is in English on desktop in the United States and India, with wider regions promised later; the post does not announce broader availability. The same post promotes split view, tab groups, cross-device scroll-position preservation and reading mode—ordinary access and organisation features packaged beside the AI ones ([Google, 24 September 2026](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/chrome/tips-for-school-and-studying/)). For schools whose students use Google services, the browser is a quieter route into student study behaviour than a new classroom app. Quizzes can support retrieval practice, but the post supplies no learning-outcome evidence, age-specific availability or teacher visibility claims. Before treating a generated quiz as a study intervention, a teacher needs to see whether its questions check the lesson’s concept or merely paraphrase the page, and whether a student can explain an answer without the tab open.

- **Google now lets administrator privileges expire automatically.** Its 23 September Workspace release allows a super admin to grant a role to a user, group or service account for a set period, including a custom expiry; the role is then revoked automatically. Google says it is available now to all Workspace customers, while the primary admin cannot receive a temporary role ([Google Workspace Updates, 23 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/assign-temporary-administrator-roles-in-the-Google-Admin-console.html)). That is a small feature with an unusually direct governance payoff for a school’s Google estate: cover for an absence, an audit or a tightly bounded project need no longer become indefinite standing access because nobody remembered to remove it. It does not prove any existing role is overbroad or justify changing grants without review. It gives a school technology team a better default to evaluate the next time temporary access is genuinely needed, with scope and expiry agreed together.

- **WorldSkills' Shanghai closing day returned to a ten-year plan that puts AI inside skills rather than atop them.** The General Assembly adopted Vision 2035 on 21 September; WorldSkills International's 27 September closing-day account set out its aims to change how skilled careers are valued, accelerate skills development through innovation, and strengthen the member network ([adoption, 21 September](https://worldskills.org/media/news/members-vote-vision-2035-worldskills-general-assembly-2026/); [closing-day account, 27 September](https://worldskills.org/media/news/vision-beyond-shanghai/)). The organisation describes AI and other digital technologies as possible aids to access and industry relevance, while promising to carry competition practice into national strategies and technical and vocational curricula. This is a strategy approval, not evidence that new curricula or credentials exist. For high-school careers guidance, the more interesting question is whether an AI-rich economy widens the definition of aspiration beyond software jobs. If a school’s AI literacy programme teaches only prompting in an office workflow, it misses the student who will use digital tools in fabrication, health technology or a skilled trade. The curriculum translation—and who assesses practical competence—remains to be seen.

## Sceptic's corner

It would be easy to turn the Python study into “Socratic AI tutoring fails.” Its strongest result concerns how helpful students felt the bot was for finishing three tasks. It did **not** find statistically reliable differences between conditions in comprehension, stress or use of outside chatbots, and the comprehension check came immediately after one debugging problem ([Eastwood and colleagues, arXiv, 24 September 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29995v1)). The trial also required participants to use the assigned assistant and offered a small course-credit incentive. That is a good way to isolate interface design, but not a natural measure of whether a student would choose it during a semester.

The rival claim—“students must struggle, therefore the least liked tutor is best”—is no safer. The authors report that students in the questions-only/no-context condition sent an average of 11.1 queries per problem, versus 4.1 in direct/full-context. More exchanges can mean productive thinking, or they can mean a student repeatedly explaining a problem the tool cannot see. Twenty of 132 participants used another LLM, commonly because the assigned one gave inadequate guidance. There is no evidence here that a longer chat produced longer-lasting knowledge ([study results](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29995v1)).

The useful test for a school is smaller and harder than either slogan: choose a task where unaided reasoning matters, compare the student’s explanation before and after help, and check the logs for abandonment or a move to an open chatbot. Keep the teacher able to change the help style when questioning becomes circular. A question is pedagogical only when it moves a learner’s thinking forward.
