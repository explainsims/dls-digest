## Top story

**Canvas's "free" agentic AI hits its paywall in 18 days, and that turns IgniteAI from a feature into a budget line.** Instructure's [April tier announcement](https://www.instructure.com/press-release/instructure-introduces-simplified-canvas-tiers-and-ecosystem-updates-new-next) set the clock: IgniteAI Grading Assistance and the IgniteAI Agent are free for U.S. Canvas customers only **through June 30, 2026**, and worldwide **through September 30, 2026**. After that, continued access requires an upgrade to the new **Canvas Plus** or **Canvas Next** tiers, while a thinner free set — rubric creation, discussion insights, translation, and basic accessibility in Canvas Core — stays included long-term. The "advanced capabilities that require substantial compute" is exactly the language [Instructure used](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/instructure-delivers-on-safe-simple-ai-promise-with-igniteai-and-major-ecosystem-updates-302638807.html) to flag what gets metered.

Why this matters for schools weighing platforms: for any institution mid-comparison this year, this is the first hard data point on what Canvas's AI *actually costs* once the trial ends — and it lands precisely in the window where buyers are modelling total cost of ownership. The grading-assistant and agent are the headline features any vendor demo will lead with, and they're the ones moving behind a tier. That changes the apples-to-apples: AI-first competitors pitch AI as baked-in, while Canvas now pitches it as an upsell.

What it doesn't change: nothing is removed today, and the free-long-term floor (rubrics, translation, accessibility) is real, not bait. But for procurement, the honest comparison is now Canvas Plus/Next pricing versus an all-in competitor number — not the free-trial Canvas that demos beautifully through September.

## Sceptic's corner

Every major LMS and AI vendor this week is selling the same promise in the same words: "safe, simple AI" that tutors rather than answers, that helps students think rather than cheat. Instructure brands IgniteAI as [safe and simple](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/instructure-delivers-on-safe-simple-ai-promise-with-igniteai-and-major-ecosystem-updates-302638807.html); OpenAI's study mode, Microsoft's Study and Learn Agent, and NotebookLM's Learning Guide all wrap themselves in Socratic-tutor language. Test it against operational reality: none of these tools can enforce that a student stays in study mode. Study mode is a toggle the student chooses; the same account, one click away, still answers anything outright. "Anti-cheating AI" is a UI default, not a capability — the product can't tell whether the student in front of it is learning or laundering an assignment, and the vendor isn't claiming it can. What they're actually selling is a *better default path*, which is genuinely worth having, plus a marketing frame that lets a school say it bought the responsible option. The usable judgement: treat "won't help students cheat" as a design intention, not a control. The control is still the task that's set and the conversation that follows — if an assignment can be flattened by a chatbot's answer button, no vendor's study-mode toggle will save it.
