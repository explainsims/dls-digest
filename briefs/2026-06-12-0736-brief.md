## Top story

**A nationally representative teacher poll just put hard numbers on a worry that's been mostly anecdotal: most K-12 teachers think AI matters more than the internet did — and more than half think it's hurting how students think.** NPR and Ipsos [released the poll on 5 June 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/teachers-concerned-about-impact-ai-students-critical-thinking): 545 K-12 teachers on Ipsos's probability-based KnowledgePanel, fielded 27 April–5 May, ±5.0 points. The headline: **74%** say AI has bigger implications for K-12 than past technological shifts; just 10% say it's no different. Underneath that, the unease is specific — **54%** say AI is making it harder for students to learn critical-thinking skills, **55%** see it mostly as a shortcut to avoid work, and **59%** say it's eroding trust between students and teachers ([NPR write-up](https://www.npr.org/2026/06/05/nx-s1-5779757/school-ai-education-students-teachers-poll-critical-thinking)).

Why this matters: this is the assessment-culture conversation, evidenced. **62%** of these teachers already use AI for their own work and **69%** of those report a productivity lift — so the profession isn't anti-tool. The split is on *student* effects, not adoption. That's the exact terrain a coaching programme lives on. And the behavioural signal is the useful part: roughly **4-in-10** have already moved assignments back to handwritten or in-class. Teachers are changing task design in response to AI faster than any policy is — only **33%** report a formal student AI policy at their school, and **52%** say they've had no guidance at all.

What it doesn't change: it's US K-12 and self-report, so the numbers travel as direction, not as any one school's baseline. But **78%** wanting responsible-AI use taught is a mandate to build on, not a problem to manage.

## Other stories

- **OpenAI confidentially filed for an IPO on 8 June.** OpenAI [submitted a confidential draft S-1 to the SEC](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/6/8/tech-giant-openai-files-for-us-initial-public-offering), with reporting putting a possible valuation near $1 trillion and timing as early as September — though the company flagged it "may be a while." It follows Anthropic's own filing. The school-relevant read isn't the share price; it's governance gravity. A company on an IPO path answers to public-market discipline and quarterly expectations, which shapes how the consumer ChatGPT product (900M+ weekly users) gets monetised — and any free education tier is downstream of that pressure. Worth a note in any "is this vendor's free offer durable?" conversation.

- **A new student survey says only 7% of college students are "all in" on AI.** Inside Higher Ed and Generation Lab's [Student Voice survey, published 11 June](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/student-success/academic-life/2026/06/11/why-students-arent-all-ai-and-what-they-want-colleges) (1,038 students, 203 institutions), found 85% have used generative AI for coursework but only **7%** call themselves enthusiasts; **40%** worry about dependence and **25%** actively resist. Just **10%** say their institution handles AI well. It's higher-ed, but it complements the teacher poll usefully: students aren't the uncritical adopters the staffroom sometimes assumes — they want authentic learning preserved through course design (24%), not bolt-on AI modules.

- **The White House handed out its first Presidential AI Challenge awards on 9 June.** [EdWeek covered the ceremony](https://www.edweek.org/technology/white-house-honors-ai-challenge-winners-as-tech-backlash-grows/2026/06), where Melania Trump presented student and teacher winners for AI projects spanning homework help, anti-bullying, and accessibility — set, as the headline notes, "as tech backlash grows." The substance is thin (a competition, not a policy), but the framing is the signal: federal AI-in-schools enthusiasm and parent/community wariness are now running on the same stage at the same time. That tension is the climate any rollout enters.

## On the radar

- **EU AI Act high-risk education obligations are still dated 2 August 2026** — under three months out. The Annex III rules covering education-sector AI (admissions, grading, exam-proctoring) take effect then unless a postponement is formally adopted first; an Omnibus extension has been discussed but isn't law until published in the Official Journal. The current development is the closing window itself: until adoption lands, the August date technically stands, so any vendor selling grading or proctoring AI into the EU is on that clock now.

- **Colorado's AI Act takes effect 30 June 2026** — covering "high-risk" systems including those affecting educational access. US-state law, but it's the first concrete compliance date in this wave and a preview of the data-processing-agreement language vendors will standardise on.

- **New York City's DOE AI playbook is due this month**, and the [reaction is already adversarial](https://www.npr.org/2026/06/05/nx-s1-5779757/school-ai-education-students-teachers-poll-critical-thinking) — parents have packed Panel for Educational Policy meetings demanding a pause on AI deployments until governance is finalised. A live example of the "deploy vs. wait-for-policy" fight playing out in public.

## Sceptic's corner

The poll's cleanest finding deserves the hardest look, because it's the one that'll get quoted in every staffroom: **54% of teachers say AI is making it harder for students to learn critical thinking.** True belief, useful data — but read it for what it is. This is teacher *perception*, self-reported, not a measured change in student cognition. There's no learning-outcome study in this poll; there's a profession's read on its own classrooms, which is valuable as a temperature check and worthless as causal evidence. The same instrument shows **55%** framing AI as "mostly a shortcut to avoid work" — which tells you the lens these answers are filtered through. When you believe a tool is mainly used to dodge effort, you'll attribute any thinking deficit you see to the tool.

The trap is letting "teachers say it hurts thinking" harden into "AI hurts thinking" on its way through a leadership deck. The honest version keeps the gap visible: teachers are worried, and that worry is itself an actionable fact — it's why **4-in-10** have already changed their task design. The usable judgement: cite this poll for *teacher sentiment and the behaviour it's driving*, never as proof of cognitive harm. If someone wants the cognitive-harm claim, that's a different, harder body of evidence — and this poll isn't it.
