## Top story

**Giving students an AI tutor did not make the AI tutor the active ingredient.** An NBER working paper indexed on 17 August and put into fresh public debate by Khan Academy on 24 August reports a two-year cluster-randomized trial across 18 Tennessee middle schools, where below-grade-level students used Khan Academy with Khanmigo during existing daily remedial-mathematics periods. Assignment to the programme raised achievement by 1.3 national percentile ranks per term, about 0.06–0.08 standard deviations over a school year; the researchers estimate 0.14 SD for a full year of active participation. Those are worthwhile gains in a difficult setting, but the authors say they resemble gains from Khan Academy practice without AI assistance ([Oreopoulos and Low, NBER, August 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35620); [Khan Academy, 24 August 2026](https://blog.khanacademy.org/what-i-found-compelling-in-a-new-randomized-trial-of-khan-academy-in-math-intervention/)).

The usage data explains the awkward headline. Ninety-six per cent of students tried Khanmigo at least once, yet the median student used it on only one-third of practice days and in just 17% of exercise sessions where they made an error. Messages were commonly bare answers or clicks on suggested prompts rather than substantive mathematical dialogue. Access was nearly universal; engagement with the tutoring mechanism was not. The trial therefore tested a real implementation rather than the imaginary version in which every learner persistently asks for Socratic help. That realism is the paper’s value.

For international schools, the useful distinction is between assigning a tool, completing practice and actually engaging the instructional mechanism. An older-secondary pilot should instrument all three, then check whether performance transfers when the AI is absent. It also argues against launching a chatbot as a separate destination and calling the job done: help-seeking has behavioural costs, especially for students already struggling. Embed support at the point of error, teach students what a useful help exchange looks like, and measure dialogue quality rather than logins. The evidence says structured digital practice can help. It does not yet say the AI tutor deserves the credit.

## Other stories

- **Google Classroom can now sit behind device, identity, network and location rules.** Google made Context-Aware Access available for Classroom on 26 August, allowing Workspace administrators to set policies by organisational unit or group and evaluate user identity, geographic location, device-security status and IP address before allowing sign-in. The control is available now, but only with Education Standard and Plus ([Google Workspace Updates, 26 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/google-classroom-now-supports-context-Aware-Access-controls.html)). This is a material change for a Google-first school even when Classroom is not the LMS: the learning surface can finally inherit the same conditional-access posture as other Workspace applications. The sensible first use is not a sweeping geography block that strands travellers. It is a small policy for privileged or unusually sensitive workflows, tested against managed and unmanaged devices, expected overseas access and a known-bad control. A rule should earn its friction by protecting a defined risk; otherwise leave the default access path intact. Security rules that cannot explain their remediation path will become help-desk policy by accident.

- **OpenAI has published education-scale usage figures without yet publishing education-scale outcomes.** A 26 August report says privacy-preserving analysis found as many as 70 million ChatGPT conversations each week across all age groups devoted to testing knowledge, including misconception checks and requests for more practice. In the US, classwork and homework prompts reportedly peak above 460 million messages per week during the school year, rise on Sunday evenings and remain above 180 million during summer ([OpenAI, 26 August 2026](https://openai.com/index/learning-never-stops/)). The magnitude makes after-hours AI support an operating reality, not an edge case. It does not tell us whether misconceptions were corrected, practice was well designed or learning endured. For international schools, the useful response is to teach a help-seeking routine—attempt, ask, test, explain—and sample the resulting student thinking. Message volume is a demand signal. A school should not let the vendor quietly promote it into an impact measure.

- **The Hong Kong Canvas investigation found responsible institutions and a vulnerable platform in the same incident.** On 20 August the privacy regulator concluded that four of seven notifying education institutions were affected by the April–May Canvas breach. Confirmed counts were 146,969 students and staff at City University, 4,584 at the Academy for Performing Arts and 2,333 at the Institute of Construction; the number at HKUST remained under verification, with Canvas messages identified as the affected data. The regulator found no evidence those institutions breached Hong Kong’s privacy ordinance: they had conducted pre-assessments, contracted for protections and monitored third-party assurance, while the incident stemmed from platform vulnerabilities ([Hong Kong PCPD, 20 August 2026](https://www.pcpd.org.hk/english/news_events/media_statements/press_20260820.html)). That is not exoneration of the operating model. It is proof that paperwork can be competent while exposure remains real. The no-breach finding concerns institutional compliance; it is not a claim that the platform was safe. Any LMS review needs minimum-data rules, MFA, log access, retention limits and exit mechanics alongside certificates and contract clauses.

- **Parental controls are widely used and widely worked around.** UK regulator research published on 19 August surveyed more than 4,000 children aged eight to 17 and their parents. Ninety-one per cent of parents reported using controls or monitoring tools, but 41% of children said they had tried to circumvent them, and 49% of that group found it easy. Fifty-two per cent said they would find a way around an age check to reach a desired service; among children whose parent had previously helped bypass an age limit, that rose to 64%. Nearly one-third of parents admitted doing so ([UK Information Commissioner’s Office, 19 August 2026](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/08/new-research-reveals-growing-gap-between-parental-confidence-and-children-s-digital-reality/)). The jurisdiction differs, but the behavioural lesson travels. Age gates and device controls are layers, not relationships. School guidance should pair technical defaults with recurring, low-drama conversations about why a boundary exists and what to do when a tool asks for data it does not need.

- **Khan Academy has published a useful antidote to buying “an AI tool.”** In a 24 August practice note, chief learning officer Kristen DiCerbo proposes four tests for selecting a problem: importance, tractability, controllability and evidence base. The target should affect a meaningful outcome, be plausibly improvable within roughly six to eighteen months, sit at least partly inside the school’s influence, and have credible evidence that an intervention could help ([Khan Academy, 24 August 2026](https://blog.khanacademy.org/kristens-corner-august-2026-prioritizing-educational-problems/)). It is vendor-authored and naturally ends with Khan Academy as the example, but the framework is better than the usual feature matrix. For Ed Tech Coaches, turn it into an intake gate: name the learner or teacher problem, baseline it, state the mechanism, set the review window and define the stop condition before discussing products. It also stops a pilot becoming its own reason for continuation. “We need AI” fails all four tests. That is a feature, not a flaw.

- **Google Meet’s refreshed hardware interface is launching before its broad rollout.** Google published the change on 24 August and began Early Preview rollout on 26 August for Neat touch controllers and Poly TC8 devices; Rapid and Scheduled Release domains start on 2 September. Mute and hand-raise move forward, advanced controls sit under “More actions,” and the pre-call screen exposes meeting-code, Webex and Zoom entry ([Google Workspace Updates, 24 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/now-available-refreshed-user-interface-for-Google-Meet-hardware-touch-controllers-on-Neat-and-Poly-devices.html)). Watch support tickets and room behaviour after rollout. Familiarity is the claim; fewer failed starts is the outcome worth measuring.

- **Google is packaging seven separate AI workflows as one student habit.** Its 26 August back-to-school guide links Sheets canvases, Docs study guides, Slides deck generation, Gmail AI Inbox, Meet notes, Vids and Forms quizzes, alongside one-year student offers. US students get AI Pro; eligible students in more than 140 other markets get AI Plus, with a payment method required and automatic monthly charging after the trial unless cancelled ([Google, 26 August 2026](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/workspace/gemini-google-workspace-back-to-school/)). Watch the account boundary: consumer student offers, school-issued Workspace entitlements and smart-feature data settings are not interchangeable, however seamless the marketing tour appears.

- **Independent learning still has a non-AI evidence base.** On 26 August the Education Endowment Foundation distilled three promising approaches for 16–19 learners: metacognition and self-regulation, feedback, and carefully structured collaborative learning. The resource draws on its Teaching and Learning Toolkit and includes professional-development examples ([EEF, 26 August 2026](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/help-young-people-become-more-independent-learners)). This is a useful watch item for older-secondary work: any AI-supported study routine should be able to name which of these mechanisms it strengthens, then demonstrate that students retain the strategy without the assistant.

## Sceptic's corner

Khan Academy’s own reading of the Tennessee trial anchors on the 0.14 SD estimate for students who remained in intervention for the full second year. Sal Khan argues that the more conservative 0.06–0.08 figures understate the programme and notes that the comparison group was already using tools such as IXL, Zearn and DeltaMath ([Khan Academy, 24 August 2026](https://blog.khanacademy.org/what-i-found-compelling-in-a-new-randomized-trial-of-khan-academy-in-math-intervention/)). Both points are fair. Neither makes 0.14 the clean headline.

The randomized assignment estimate is the result least vulnerable to selection after the fact. Restricting attention to students who stayed in intervention changes the population, and scaling active participation asks what might happen under stronger implementation rather than what assignment actually produced. More importantly, the NBER paper says the gains resemble ordinary Khan Academy practice while Khanmigo itself was used infrequently and shallowly. This is evidence for structured practice in a daily intervention period; it is weak evidence that generative tutoring supplied the gain.

The usable staffroom line is simple: an AI feature inside an effective routine does not become the cause of the routine’s effect. Fund the instructional period, rostering, teacher follow-through and practice design first. Then test whether the AI layer adds learning, access or useful feedback above the non-AI workflow. If it does not, keep the routine and drop the adjective.
