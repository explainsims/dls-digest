# Thursday, 14 May 2026

## Top story

**Self-correction from yesterday's brief: there is no 8-K coming. Instructure has been a private company since November 2024 — the mid-June EDGAR watch in the Wednesday and Tuesday briefs was wrong. The disclosure question reroutes through state AG offices, FERPA, and Vietnam's PDPL data-protection framework.**

Yesterday's "On the radar" item had Instructure's 60-day SEC cybersecurity-disclosure clock at day fourteen with a mid-June EDGAR window. That framing carried forward from Day 7. It is wrong, and the error is mine to own: KKR (with Dragoneer Investment Group) completed the take-private acquisition of Instructure on **13 November 2024 at $23.60/share, $4.8B enterprise value**; Instructure common stock ceased trading on NYSE that same day. ([KKR press release](https://www.instructure.com/press-release/kkr-and-dragoneer-complete-acquisition-instructure), [Higher Ed Dive on the deal](https://www.highereddive.com/news/instructure-kkr-acquisition-ed-tech/723082/)). Form 8-K Item 1.05 obligations attach to SEC-registered issuers; a privately-held PE portfolio company doesn't have them. There is no EDGAR window to watch.

The disclosure question doesn't disappear — it relocates. The active obligations now are: (1) state attorney-general breach-notification statutes in every state where affected residents live, with thresholds and clocks set per-state (most are 30-60 days from discovery, several have shorter clocks for student data); (2) FERPA Section 99.5(b) school-official-exception notification chains and any state student-data-privacy acts (SOPIPA in California, Maryland's Student Data Privacy Act, Texas SB 820); (3) for an international-school context in Vietnam, **Vietnam's Personal Data Protection Law framework — Decree 13 (2023), with Decree 356 cited by recent sources as a 1 January 2026 successor instrument (the supersession claim merits in-house verification before it is load-bearing in procurement work)** — with extraterritorial reach over foreign vendors processing Vietnamese citizens' data and reported fines of up to **5% of revenue** for cross-border-transfer violations. ([Hogan Lovells on PDPL](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/vietnam-enacts-landmark-law-on-personal-data-protection-stable-standing-with-stricter-compliance), [DFDL on Decree 356](https://www.dfdl.com/insights/legal-and-tax-updates/vietnam-personal-data-protection-2026-what-foreign-organizations-need-to-know/)).

**Why this matters at SSIS, sharply this week.** The rubric axis I sketched yesterday — "what is the vendor's relationship with its capital-owner on extortion-spend authorisations" — was directionally right but rooted in the wrong evidentiary chain. The case for it is *stronger*, not weaker, once you correct the disclosure path: a vendor whose primary disclosure obligations route through state AGs (heterogeneous, slow, settlement-friendly) and a foreign data-protection regime (the Vietnam PDPL framework under Decree 13 / Decree 356, with reported revenue-percentage fines) is a vendor whose disclosures will land in the press before the regulatory paper trail catches up. The Garbarino letter — still the strongest public artefact this week — is a House Homeland Security request for *briefing*, not a subpoena and not a filing requirement. KKR remains publicly silent on Wednesday's end. ([The Register on the Garbarino letter](https://www.theregister.com/cyber-crime/2026/05/12/congress-investigates-canvas-breach-after-instructure-cuts-deal-with-shinyhunters/5238927)). The procurement-rubric implication: when you ask "what are the vendor's disclosure obligations after a breach," for an SEC-registered issuer the answer is short; for a PE portfolio company the answer is "fifty state AGs, FERPA, and whatever cross-border regime applies wherever the affected user lives." For Toddle — also privately held — the same answer applies. **The structural disclosure-asymmetry question outlasts the Canvas story.**

## Sceptic's Corner

**The "ransom paid, problem resolved" narrative has a structural weakness the field hasn't yet named: the difference between a take-private PE-portfolio vendor and a public-equity vendor is not just disclosure asymmetry — it is *governance asymmetry on extortion spend.*** When a public company pays a ransom, the spend appears in financial statements (eventually), is subject to securities-law disclosure if material, and triggers governance-shareholder fiduciary accountability. When a KKR portfolio company pays a ransom, the spend is internal to the LP-GP relationship; the limited partners (pension funds, sovereigns, endowments) generally don't get itemised line-items on extortion payments. The KKR-Dragoneer LPs may not know how much was paid to ShinyHunters. The rubric implication: when a school evaluates an LMS vendor in PE hands, the question "what governance accountability sits behind the ransom-payment decision" is *structurally different* from the same question for an SEC-registered issuer. Most rubrics don't include this axis because most rubric-writers haven't internalised what the PE take-private wave did to the disclosure landscape. **The 2024 take-private of Instructure changed the breach-response governance question for the entire K-12/HE LMS sector; the 2026 breach is the first event that surfaces it.**

**The Cyber Daily ShinyHunters quote is also a tell about the criminal-economics shape of the deal.** "The Company and it's customers will not further be targeted or contacted for payment" is a *future-promise* sentence — a non-targeting commitment, not a deletion attestation. It does not say "the data has been destroyed." It does not say "we have no further access." It says "we will not bother you again." Read structurally, that is the language of a settled extortion contract: paid party gets a promise of non-recurrence; non-paid third parties (school districts, individual students, downstream re-sellers of stolen data on criminal markets) are not parties to it. The *In re PowerSchool* litigation is partly an attempt to put third-party harm on the legal record after exactly this kind of vendor-attacker bilateral settlement. The Hinds and Peterman dockets are the equivalent next step here. Procurement-rubric language: *a vendor's bilateral settlement with an attacker is not a settlement with the data-subjects whose data was taken*. The rubric should ask vendors to demonstrate what *data-subject*-side remediation they are funding, not what *vendor*-side promises they have secured.

**A note on register for Friday's Toddle webinar.** A school sending a single proxy with a single sharp question is one rhetorical move; a school sending nobody and reading the public materials afterward is another; a school participating in the Q&A under their own name is a third. Each carries different signal to other procurement committees observing the same webinar. The Toddle PYP Refresh title makes this an AI-product webinar — the Q&A may be moderated to keep architecture/security questions out of the public stream. If that filtering happens visibly, it itself is a rubric input. If the Q&A is open and Toddle answers a ransom-policy question publicly, it is the first major LMS-AI vendor to do so in a school-facing format post-Canvas-breach, and that itself is a competitive event worth marking.
