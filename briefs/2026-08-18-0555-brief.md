## Top story

**Google’s Workspace agents can now act across people, not merely draft for one of them.** On 17 August Google said Workspace Studio flows are gaining cross-user collaboration steps: the original version could prepare an email but not send it; the new version can execute actions, with guardrails intended to make those actions attributable, interruptible and reviewable. New flows run through a least-privileged agent identity, carry a unique identifier into audit records, and can be suspended individually or stripped of particular OAuth scopes. Administrators can disable step types and webhooks, cut off Gemini data access, require confirmation before externally sharing data, and apply data-loss-prevention rules to sourced data, used data and output visibility ([Google Workspace Updates, 17 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/new-enterprise-security-controls-for-Workspace-Studio-enable-expanded-collaboration-use-cases.html?hl=en)).

This is the material threshold. A generated draft is content to inspect; a flow that sends, shares or edits is an actor whose identity, permissions and stop mechanism need testing. The controls are available to Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus, but the timing matters: admin settings began rolling out on 17 August; end-user collaboration steps begin on 20 August for Rapid Release and 1 September for Scheduled Release. Beta identity attribution also defaults to showing the flow as the actor, with its owner visible.

For international schools, the good news is that Google has exposed the right control surfaces before broad end-user rollout. The catch is in the footnotes: least privilege, agent context in audit logs and the access-management dashboard initially apply only to newly created flows; existing flows are promised later. That makes Workspace Studio a governed-prototyping candidate, not a general staff convenience. A sound first test is a disposable cross-user flow with one known-good action and one deliberately forbidden external-share action, followed by an audit-log read-back and emergency suspension. If a school cannot reconstruct who caused what, or cannot stop only the offending flow, the agent is not ready to touch operational work.

## Other stories

- **Gemini Notebook can now be copied as a package, and permission to copy is on by default.** A user with access and copy permission can duplicate an entire notebook, including its sources, Audio and Video Overviews, study guides, flashcards, quizzes, slide decks, generation prompts and custom chat configuration. Drive sources travel only when the copier can access and copy the original file; personal chat history and user notes do not. The copy becomes independent and does not receive later changes from the original ([Google Workspace Updates, 17 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/make-copy-of-notebook-in-gemini-notebook.html?hl=en)). That is excellent for distributing a teacher-built study template without giving students a shared workspace. It is also versioning by fork: a corrected source, prompt or misconception in the master will not repair copies already made. A reusable notebook should therefore carry a visible version and review date inside the notebook, while educators should decide consciously whether copying is allowed rather than inherit the default. Copy permission is not just a sharing preference when the package contains generated instructional material.

- **Google has made external Drive exposure easier to query, but only for schools on Standard or Plus.** Drive Inventory Reporting in BigQuery now consolidates direct permissions, group membership and public links into fields that distinguish human users, service accounts and files published to the web. Administrators can cross-reference those signals with data-loss-prevention metadata to prioritise risky files. The calculation is off by default, and Google warns that large groups may extend processing time and delay reports; rollout began on 14 August ([Google Workspace Updates, 17 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/enhanced-external-sharing-insights-now-available-in-Drive-Inventory-Reporting.html?hl=en)). This is the rare AI-adjacent governance story that improves the substrate rather than adding another assistant. A useful first query is a narrow inventory of sensitive folders, separating named external collaborators from public-web exposure and service-account access. A single external-sharing count would flatten three very different risks.

- **Students became better AI critics after practising criticism, not after merely solving a neighbouring problem.** A six-page physics-education study submitted on 12 August gave 24 introductory laboratory groups an o4-mini solution to evaluate. Some groups first solved a related rotational-mechanics problem; others critiqued an AI solution using reflection questions based on the Minnesota Assessment of Problem Solving rubric. The problem-solving-only groups often produced uncritical or misconception-based evaluations, while the rubric-guided groups identified more expert-aligned faults such as skipped numerical procedures and undefined notation. The same study found that well-specified prompts improved completeness, while underspecified and multimodal prompts exposed reasoning and correctness weaknesses ([Borse et al., 12 August](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12533)). Twenty-four groups, one topic and a conference paper do not establish broad learning effects. They do provide a stronger classroom mechanism than generic advice to check AI: students need named dimensions, flawed examples and repeated critique practice.

- **Language inequality begins before the prompt reaches the model.** A preprint submitted on 12 August uses Bengali to map four infrastructure gaps in AI-assisted education: Bengali represents under 0.5% of global web content despite nearly 4% of the world’s population; major multilingual corpora show a reported 67:1 English-to-Bengali training-token deficit; the script incurs a tokenisation penalty; and rural individual internet penetration is 36.5%, against 71.4% in urban areas. The authors argue for treating dataset scarcity as structural and offline-first design as an equity strategy ([Roy and Roy, 12 August](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12278)). This is a preprint and a Bengali case, not a benchmark of every classroom model. The transferable test is still sharp: multilingual evaluation should measure correctness, reading level, token cost and task completion in the languages students actually use, on school connectivity. A vendor language list shows that a system accepts input; it does not show equal service.

- **Photorealism is the wrong success criterion for a scientific diagram.** Researchers behind Princigram argue that generic image models can produce plausible-looking physics diagrams with wrong force directions, coordinate systems, thermodynamic states or equations. Their proposed generator uses a fixed reasoning schema across six physics subdisciplines, trained through 4.3 million curated images, including 115,037 with expert-level annotation. Its companion benchmark turns each held-out diagram into binary questions about named objects, forces and states instead of relying on a single holistic judge score; the authors report improved physical faithfulness on that benchmark and a physics subset of GenExam ([Zhang et al., 13 August](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13112)). This is a system paper from its own builders, not independent classroom validation. Its contribution for school practice is the evaluation shape: a generated science visual needs a checklist of factual relations at final display size. Looking polished is not a near miss; in a diagram, visual plausibility can conceal the error.

## On the radar

- **Google’s Admin Assist has arrived outside Education editions.** The 17 August launch adds a Gemini side panel and search overviews to the Admin console, synthesising help documentation and guiding super administrators through troubleshooting and policy work. It is on by default for eligible Business editions, unavailable to delegated administrators, and not listed for Education ([Google Workspace Updates, 17 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/use-gemini-to-help-manage-google-Workspace-for-your-organization.html?hl=en)). Watch whether education availability follows, and whether answers preserve the distinction between explaining a setting and changing one. For school IT, the subtler question is whether generic help pages can account for local policy exceptions.

- **Japan’s informatics exam appears to have changed students’ self-judgement faster than their competence.** A University of Tokyo preprint uses annual first-year surveys from 2006–2026 to study the 2025 addition of Informatics I to the national university entrance test. It reports a sharp shift in perceived computer-science acquisition, too large to interpret as proficiency growth and better understood as changed criteria for judging acquisition ([Morihata, 13 August](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12924)). The author compares curriculum reforms and direct-entry with gap-year students to isolate exam effects, but the outcome remains self-perception, not tested skill. Assessment can raise a subject’s visibility while making self-report trend lines incomparable.

- **UNESCO’s Digital Learning Week will put synthetic evidence and ghost learners on the same agenda as AI tutors.** The 8–11 September programme in Paris is organised around Facts, Frictions and Frontiers, including synthetic research, autonomous agents, digital sovereignty and analogue or community-led alternatives ([UNESCO, event page accessed 18 August](https://www.unesco.org/en/weeks/digital-learning?hub=66927)). Its framing places public-interest AI and low-tech alternatives beside commercial agents, a useful breadth if it survives the programme. The watch item is whether the meeting produces operational guidance or only a well-named problem set.

## Sceptic's corner

Google’s new agent controls invite a comforting conclusion: once least privilege, audit logs, data-loss prevention and human approval exist, schools can move from experimentation to deployment. Those are necessary controls, and their arrival before the collaboration steps is good product sequencing. They are not evidence that the flow should exist.

The distinction is easiest to see beside the physics study. The stronger student critiques did not come from a more capable model or a more elaborate permission layer. They followed structured reflection against named dimensions, and even then the evidence covers only 24 groups and one problem family ([Borse et al., 12 August](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12533)). A technically governed flow can still automate a weak rubric, send an unhelpful intervention at scale or remove the pause in which professional judgement happens.

An audit log answers accountability after an action; it does not show that the action was warranted.

There is also a version gap inside Google’s own launch. The strongest identity and audit protections initially attach to newly created flows, with existing flows supported later, while the user-facing steps begin rolling out this week ([Google Workspace Updates, 17 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/new-enterprise-security-controls-for-Workspace-Studio-enable-expanded-collaboration-use-cases.html?hl=en)). That is not a reason to reject Workspace Studio. It is a reason to separate three questions vendors prefer to collapse: can the agent perform the action, can the institution govern the action, and has anyone shown that automating the action improves the educational or operational result? This week Google has materially strengthened the second answer. The third remains the institution’s work.
