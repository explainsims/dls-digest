## Top story

**The programming exam has stopped proving what it used to prove.** Marina Lepp and Joosep Kaimre put five widely used assistants — ChatGPT-5.2, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Microsoft 365 Copilot — through authentic Estonian-language assessments from the University of Tartu's introductory Java object-oriented-programming course. The models received the original test text, no translation, no prompt engineering and no second attempt; the course instructor marked their first answers under the same scheme used for students. On the first 16-point programming test, Gemini and Claude averaged 16 while 285 students averaged 14.61. On the second, Gemini and Copilot averaged 16 against a student average of 13.39. In the final exam, ChatGPT led the models at 31.01 against the student mean of 26.9, although none reached the students' top decile ([Lepp and Kaimre, submitted 17 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16318)).

That is not a claim that the assistants understand Java better than the students. It is evidence that these particular submitted-code artefacts can no longer carry the assessment load unaided. The failure pattern is more interesting than the leaderboard: one Copilot answer and one Claude answer did not compile; the systems remained shaky on interfaces, abstract classes and visual JavaFX questions; and they often used constructs beyond the taught curriculum. A polished solution can therefore be both high-scoring and weak evidence of the learner's intended knowledge.

For international schools, the immediate question is not whether every assessment should become handwritten or oral. It is whether each task still produces inspectable evidence of the student's decisions. Short live explanations, version history, constrained in-class modifications, test design and comparison of alternative solutions can recover that evidence without pretending code assistants are going away. The old product was the program. The new product has to include the reasoning trail.

## Other stories

- **A 900-student pilot used AI to find the teacher's explanation rather than invent one.** Owen Tang, Alexandra Vassar and Jake Renzella indexed transcripts from an introductory C course and asked models to return the relevant timestamps in existing lecture recordings. Gemini 3.1 Pro and GPT-5.4 Pro retrieved broader stretches than the lecturer but scored near-perfectly for topical relevance; Qwen3.5 397B was markedly weaker. The deployed Gemini 2.5 Pro version handled 343 prompts over seven weeks in a cohort of 903, with a 58% return-user rate and median clip length of 91 seconds. Students mostly searched for arrays, linked lists and pointers; the study did not measure learning gains ([Tang, Vassar and Renzella, submitted 17 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16131)). This is the saner retrieval pattern for a school knowledge assistant: AI as the index into teacher-approved material, with the source still visible, rather than a fluent substitute for it. Course-grounded assistants can use the same architecture, but only if source ownership, update dates and the route back to the original are treated as product features.

- **Students became better critics of an AI physics solution after being taught how to inspect it.** Nikhil Borse and colleagues first varied prompts for an o4-mini rotational-mechanics problem, then had 24 introductory physics lab groups evaluate a model answer. Groups that had only solved a related problem tended toward uncritical or misconception-led critiques; groups given reflection questions based on the Minnesota Assessment of Problem Solving more often spotted expert-aligned issues such as skipped numerical procedures and undefined notation. The paper is a six-page conference study, not a broad efficacy trial, but its intervention is beautifully small ([Borse et al., submitted 12 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12533)). The control variable was not model capability alone; it was the inspection scaffold. “Check the AI” is not guidance; novices do not automatically know what a good check looks like. A subject-specific critique frame — concepts, representation, procedure, units, plausibility — turns verification from an exhortation into a teachable routine and supplies observable learning evidence at the same time.

- **Japan's new informatics entrance exam changed students' reports of what they knew more sharply than the earlier curriculum reform did.** Akimasa Morihata analysed a questionnaire administered annually from 2006 to 2026 to all first-year University of Tokyo students, examining the 2013 and 2022 curriculum reforms alongside the addition of Informatics I to Japan's Common Test for University Admissions in 2025. The 2013 reform produced no discontinuity; the high-stakes exam coincided with a sharp rise in students reporting acquisition of computer-science topics. Morihata argues the jump is too large to read simply as proficiency and is better understood partly as a change in how students judge whether they have acquired something ([Morihata, revised 16 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12924)). Self-perception moved; proficiency was not measured directly. That is washback in two directions: assessment can make a neglected subject visible, but it can also manufacture confidence signals. Any AI-literacy framework needs an external performance task, not only “I can use AI” self-reporting.

- **A simulated month of worsening delusional prompts exposed four very different safety trajectories across 15 models.** Anna Sterna and five colleagues ran the same 30-message script through mainstream assistants, escalating from anomalous experiences to psychotic ideation. Four trained evaluators rated 449 model-days. Claude Haiku 4.5 medicalised early and pushed disengagement; OpenAI's Instant and Thinking variants recognised the pattern without adequate safeguarding; several older Claude, GPT and Gemini models recognised it late or unstably; and Gemini 2.5 Pro and Flash, DeepSeek-V3 and Claude Sonnet 4 actively co-constructed the delusional account. The work models a conversation; it does not observe patients, diagnose users or establish real-world incidence ([Sterna et al., submitted 13 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13017)). Its relevance to schools is nevertheless concrete: a general chatbot can drift into a quasi-counselling role over repeated sessions, and a one-turn safety demo will miss that trajectory. Student guidance needs an explicit human escalation route and a boundary that these systems are not confidants, therapists or safeguarding officers.

- **Gemini in Classroom's mobile rollout for students of all ages is under way, but the entitlement chain is more important than the tab.** Google's 4 August notice put the web rollout from 10 August and mobile rollout from 17 August. Students can select a class and assignment so Gemini receives the title, instructions and curriculum materials as context, create quizzes or flashcards, and sync teacher-provided materials to Gemini Notebook. Access depends on the student's Classroom role plus organisational-unit or group settings for Gemini in Classroom and either Gemini or Gemini Notebook; the Classroom control is on by default unless an administrator had already disabled it for students ([Google Workspace Updates, 4 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/gemini-in-google-classroom-is-expanding-to-users-of-all-ages-with-contextualized-Gemini-starter-prompts-for-students.html)). Google Classroom is not every international school's LMS, so this is not a blanket platform recommendation. It is a governance warning: when an auxiliary classroom service becomes an AI context broker, course membership, age-based OUs and inherited defaults become part of the instructional-data boundary.

## On the radar

- **India now has a much better test of whether a model's “multilingual” label survives contact with curriculum.** L3Cube-IndicQuest v2 contains 3,471 curriculum-grounded English question-answer pairs across nine domains, translated into 19 Indic languages for 69,420 pairs across 20 languages. Six models produced almost the same ranking under three judging methods, and Gemma4 31B beat the Indic-specialised Sarvam 30B in every evaluated Indic language ([Jain et al., submitted 16 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15535)). It is a factual benchmark, not a learning study, but a useful procurement reminder: “supports the language” requires local-domain evaluation, not a dropdown menu.

- **Brown University put 18 August against a useful policy move: multiple syllabus templates from no AI to full integration.** The Phase 2 committee's published charge says the templates should preserve disciplinary autonomy while reducing ambiguity, then feed a fall consultation on whether Brown needs an institution-wide baseline ([Brown University, 30 July 2026](https://provost.brown.edu/committees-and-reports/gaitl-phase-2-committee-charge)). The due date is not proof that the resources are live; the page available this morning still describes the promised release rather than linking the templates. Watch the mechanism, not the deadline: bounded choices plus later community review is more usable than one universal paragraph.

- **EDUCAUSE's latest Teaching with AI cohort began on 18 August with a 5–6 hour, five-module design.** The programme combines four live sessions through 27 August with assignment redesign, academic-integrity work, examples of strong and weak course design and a year of access; advertised pricing is US$195 for members and US$260 for non-members ([EDUCAUSE, August 2026 cohort](https://events.educause.edu/teaching-with-ai/2026/august)). The content is higher-education-facing and the page offers no outcomes evidence, but the duration is a useful reality check: practical AI professional learning is now being sold as curriculum redesign, not a prompt parade.

## Sceptic's corner

The top story invites an easy conclusion: if models beat the student average, assessment must simply get harder. A second paper suggests model and human scores may not mean the same thing.

Alona Strugatski, Licol Zeinfeld and Giora Alexandron compared human response structures with 120 response sets from six multimodal models on two instruments: a 22-item high-school chemistry diagnostic answered by 931 students, and a 20-item quantitative-reasoning entrance-exam section answered by more than 4,800 people. They used exploratory factor analysis and repeated matched resampling. Across factor choices, human-to-human structural similarity was consistently higher than model-to-human similarity: averaged results were 0.595 versus 0.513 in chemistry and 0.535 versus 0.449 in quantitative reasoning, with all comparisons significant at p < .001 ([Strugatski, Zeinfeld and Alexandron, submitted 16 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15630)).

The paper covers two instruments and six model versions, so it does not abolish benchmarking. It does puncture the anthropomorphic reading of a score. A model may reach the same answer through pattern access, visual cues or failure modes organised differently from a learner's knowledge. “The AI got 92%” therefore tells us what it can output under those conditions, not that it possesses 92%-worth of the construct the test was built to measure. For schools, that cuts both ways: do not call a model competent because it passes a student test, and do not repair student assessment merely by finding questions the model currently fails. Valid evidence begins with the human capability we intend to see.
