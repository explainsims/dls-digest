## Top story

**AI guidance is becoming a training system, not just a policy memo.** The Philippines Department of Education is expanding its Google for Education partnership into Project S.I.N.A.G., a no-cost Google and Gemini AI certification push for more than one million teachers and staff before the new school year ([GMA News](https://www.gmanetwork.com/news/topstories/nation/990219/deped-google-teacher-training-ai/story/)). The timing matters because DepEd already issued a risk-based AI framework in February: AI can support planning, feedback, assessment design, and operations, but facial recognition, social scoring, manipulative tools for minors, and unsupervised high-risk uses are out; every AI system also needs human oversight and a privacy impact assessment ([GMA News](https://www.gmanetwork.com/news/scitech/technology/978018/deped-issues-ai-guidelines-for-basic-education-bans-facial-recognition-tools/story/)).

That is a more mature pattern than the usual guidance-gap story. It says: write the guardrails, then train the humans who have to live inside them. For international schools, the useful signal is not that Google is the vendor. It is the sequencing. A school can have a sensible AI policy and still fail if teachers cannot translate it into lesson-planning, assessment, parent communication, or admin workflows.

For Ed Tech Coaches, this argues for a small certification-adjacent map rather than a heroic one-off workshop: core norms first, then short practice modules, then evidence of use. The dry little phrase "professional development credit" may turn out to matter more than the demo reel. Teachers do what the school makes legible, repeatable, and worth their time.

## Sceptic's corner

The claim to challenge is that AI certification will solve the teacher-readiness problem. It will not. Certification is a tidy administrative unit: countable, badgeable, suitable for reports, and blessedly easier to manage than the messy fact that teachers learn tools through their own subjects, routines, anxieties, shortcuts, and student edge cases.

Still, dismissing certification would be lazy. The right certificate can create a shared baseline: data boundaries, disclosure norms, verification habits, and a vocabulary for talking about AI without requiring every teacher to become a hobbyist. The danger is credential theatre, where a school can say "trained" while teachers still cannot answer the ordinary Wednesday questions: Can I paste this student paragraph into Gemini? Can AI draft a rubric? Can I use it for parent translation? Who checks the output?

So the useful test is operational. A certification is worth having only if it changes the next staffroom decision. If it does not produce clearer permissions, fewer private improvisations, and better escalation habits, it is a lanyard with a login.
