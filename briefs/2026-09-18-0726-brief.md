## Top story

**Google’s new classroom generator passes its own checks on the first try just 3.5 per cent of the time.** Google Research unveiled Learning Interactives on 17 September: a system that turns a teacher’s topic, grade level and intended objective into a playable STEM simulation or mini-game. The teacher approves the generated learning objectives; Gemini then constructs five progressive challenges, the interface, hints, feedback and a worked solution. A public library now contains more than 30 teacher-reviewed examples across mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, computer science and earth science, while Workspace for Education schools can apply for a pilot ([Google Research, 17 September 2026](https://research.google/blog/the-future-of-practice-enabling-teachers-to-create-learning-interactives-with-generative-ui/)).

The technical report is refreshingly less theatrical. A one-shot generated interface passed Google’s visual, mechanical, telemetry and solution checks only 3.5% of the time. Ten critique-and-repair rounds lifted that to 69.3%. In a usability study, 12 US STEM teachers submitted 36 requests; Google withheld three outputs as inadequate, and the remainder averaged above 8.1 on its usability scale. Two independent teachers then rated outputs from 40 further prompts, accepting 86%—with physics and chemistry stronger than biology ([Google Research technical report, 16 September 2026](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/learning_interactives.pdf)). Those are promising production and review figures, not evidence that pupils learned more.

For an international school, the interesting object is the pipeline, not the gallery. A teacher-authored objective becomes generated code, is screened by automated critics, selected from several candidates and only then reaches a learner. That is much closer to a governable Ed Tech Coach prototype than asking a model for a game and trusting the first colourful result. But Google still generated five candidates to choose one and has not tested learning efficacy in real classrooms. The sensible pilot question is therefore whether a generated interactive produces transferable understanding after the screen is closed—not whether it looks active while a pupil drags the slider.

## Other stories

- **McGraw Hill bought an AI coach that listens to lessons.** The publisher announced on 17 September that it has acquired TeachFX, which records classroom audio on a phone or laptop, uses diarisation to analyse interactions, and returns feedback on practices such as questioning and prompting pupils to explain their thinking. School leaders can receive aggregated instructional trends; a separate MCP integration can turn TeachFX feedback into lesson plans through Claude for Teachers. McGraw Hill places the deal inside an estimated US$6 billion professional-learning market and says several independent studies found better average pupil performance and stronger teacher morale, but it did not name those studies in the release or disclose the transaction terms ([McGraw Hill, 17 September 2026](https://www.mheducation.com/about-us/news-insights/press-releases/mcgraw-hill-acquires-teachfx-expanding-commitment-to-great-teaching-with-ai-coaching-tool-designed-by-educators.html)). The pedagogical idea is plausible; the governance object is still a recording of children and teachers crossing into analysis, aggregation and potentially another model. Consent, retention, leader visibility and whether coaching data can become evaluation data need answers before the microphone turns on.

- **Gemini Notebook has become a voice tutor, lecture recorder and quiz engine at once.** Google’s 15 September update adds real-time spoken conversations grounded in notebook sources in nearly 100 languages, a mobile audio recorder due from the week of 21 September, interactive reports combining summaries with quizzes and flashcards, and shareable one-minute video overviews in more than 80 languages. The entitlement details are uneven: voice begins with AI Ultra users aged 18 and over before broader release, while source notes and editing are supported for under-18 users in English; interactive reports and new quiz formats roll out to all users over the next few weeks ([Google, 15 September 2026](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/gemini-notebook/new-study-tools-september-2026/)). In a Google-first international school, the practical boundary has moved from a research notebook to captured classroom speech and adaptive practice. Before calling that one workflow, separate who may record, who owns the recording, which account retains it, and whether quiz performance is visible evidence or merely a private rehearsal signal.

- **NUS is building seven education tools around classroom problems rather than one universal assistant.** The National University of Singapore described the programme on 15 September; three tools are already in course pilots. Brainstorm uses Socratic prompts during topic and hypothesis formation, Verify asks students to compare several plausible AI answers and justify which is strongest, and Engage captures classroom discussion so instructors can revisit themes, gaps and questions. The pilots span science, business, psychology and social-science courses, and the teams combine faculty, academic developers, education technologists and IT staff ([NUS, 15 September 2026](https://news.nus.edu.sg/supercharging-education-building-ai-tools-to-support-teaching-and-learning-at-nus/)). NUS reports designs and early use, not learning outcomes, and Engage raises the familiar question of what happens to recorded speech. Still, the grain size travels well: one cognitive bottleneck, one deliberately bounded behaviour and one named course is a sturdier prototype brief than adding a general chatbot to every subject and looking for uses afterwards.

- **A plausible AI explanation may have little to do with the score it explains.** A Technical University of Munich summary published on 16 September revisits an ACL study from July that analysed 6,005 fifteen-minute segments drawn from more than 1,600 elementary mathematics lesson transcripts. Fine-tuned language models scored teacher feedback more accurately than prompted general models, with average error around one point on a seven-point scale, but clustered heavily in the middle and never used the extreme scores in one leading model. More troublingly, the sentences that LLMs named as decisive usually changed the score only slightly when removed, while SHAP-selected sentences caused larger, more stable changes across model types; the two approaches overlapped on only about one or two of their top ten sentences per transcript ([TUM, 16 September 2026](https://www.edtech.tum.de/from-scoring-to-explanations-can-we-trust-ai-rationales-for-teaching-quality/); [ACL paper, July 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.375/)). This was one rubric dimension on text transcripts, not a full account of teaching. It is nevertheless a clean warning for feedback dashboards: a polished rationale is another model output to test, not an audit trail.

- **The University of California has put AI inside a formal risk appetite.** In its first report to the Board of Regents, published 16 September, UC’s system-wide steering committee organised work across five areas and said the university was the first to address AI explicitly in a Digital Risk Appetite statement—a declaration of how much technology risk it will accept and where. The ten-campus system is also seeking coordinated arrangements with multiple model and cloud providers to avoid single-vendor dependence; Google has supplied more than US$32.7 million across 61 UC projects ([University of California, 16 September 2026](https://www.universityofcalifornia.edu/press-room/university-california-leverages-systemwide-expertise-advance-purposeful-human-centered)). This is the institution reporting its own first and its own programme, not an external assessment. The useful move for a school is smaller: write down where experimentation is welcome, where human review is mandatory and where the risk is unacceptable. A list of principles says what an organisation values; a risk appetite says what it will actually permit.

## On the radar

- **Cornell’s AI pilots are allowed to use less AI.** The university announced on 17 September that discipline-based teams of three to five faculty will redesign a core skill or course sequence for spring 2027 through spring 2028, supported by embedded graduate researchers and student fellows. Proposals may increase AI use or deliberately avoid it, and results are due in 2028; a US$2 million gift supports the wider initiative ([Cornell, 17 September 2026](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2026/09/ai-education-initiatives-emphasize-experimentation-balanced-use)). Watch whether the evaluations publish baseline measures, failed designs and student-learning results, not just funded showcases.

- **The EU’s leaked child-safety plan became a formal proposal.** The Commission adopted the KIDS Act proposal on 17 September, confirming safety-by-design duties for services offering social media, video, games, AI companions or chatbots to minors. Embedded chatbots could not switch on automatically, and companions or chatbots could not simulate relationships likely to foster emotional dependency; very large platforms would submit plans for independent audit, with a proposed 90-day enforcement route ([European Commission announcement](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/eu-kids-act-restrict-social-media-platforms-access-children-eu) and [official explainer, 17 September 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/kids-act-explained)). It now goes to Parliament and Council; watch the final treatment of education services rather than treating the proposal as law.

- **Singapore has published an implementation gate beside its AI principles.** The Ministry of Education’s Student Learning Space page, updated 10 September, frames school AI through agency, inclusivity, fairness and safety, then directs teachers running a small local trial to an implementation guide and checklist ([Singapore MOE, updated 10 September 2026](https://www.learning.moe.edu.sg/about/ai-in-sls/responsible-ai/ai-in-education-framework/)). The checklist itself sits behind the ministry intranet, so the public page cannot show its teeth or establish how demanding the gate really is. Watch for a public version or an equivalent school checklist that turns the four nouns into owners, controls and evidence requirements.

## Sceptic's corner

The claim worth testing is that a generated simulation is active learning because the pupil manipulates it. Google’s design is considerably better than a decorative chatbot: the teacher approves objectives, difficulty rises in stages, hints preserve some struggle, feedback responds to action and the system tests its own code repeatedly. That is a serious attempt to build cognitive friction back into a technology usually designed to remove it.

But activity is not the same as thought. A learner can drag sliders, clear levels and follow hints while learning the interface rather than the concept. Google’s own results measure generation success, expert acceptance and teacher-rated usability. They do not yet measure retention, transfer to an unfamiliar problem, misconception repair or what pupils can explain without the simulation. The 86% acceptance rate therefore tells us that many outputs looked usable to reviewers, not that 86% taught effectively.

The usable judgement is neither reject the tool nor infer learning from interactivity. Pilot one simulation against a conventional alternative; collect a short pre-task, delayed transfer task and student explanation; inspect wrong paths as carefully as completion; and count the teacher time needed to verify the objective, model and feedback. If the learning survives away from the screen, the generator has earned another round. If only the interaction looks lively, it has generated an activity—not evidence.
