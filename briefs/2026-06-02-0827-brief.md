# Tuesday, 2 June 2026

## Top story

**Google's most useful AI update this week is infrastructure, not novelty.** Google Classroom is rolling out learning goals: educators can tag assignments, questions, materials, quiz assignments, and rubrics with standards or skills, then use analytics to see student performance against those goals. AI can suggest relevant goals from assignment and course content, while standards are drawn through 1EdTech / CASE-compatible sources. The rollout began 27 May and is available across Google Workspace for Education Fundamentals, Standard, and Plus ([Google Workspace Updates, 29 May](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/05/keep-track-of-student-progress-with-learning-standards-and-skills-in-Google-Classroom.html)).

For Ed Tech Coaches and international schools, this is a useful shift in the AI conversation. The question is no longer just whether teachers can generate lesson plans or quizzes. It is whether the learning platform can connect curriculum goals, teacher-designed tasks, student evidence, and instructional decisions in a way that teachers can actually use. The caution: standards coverage is still uneven across countries and curriculum systems, so schools should verify local framework support before treating this as a ready-made standards solution.

## Other stories

- **Gemini-assisted Drive organization is now generally available.** Google's "Organize My Files" can suggest where loose Drive files should move, propose new folders, let users review choices, and warn about permission changes before files are moved ([Google Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/06/organize-my-files-in-drive-now-generally-available.html)). For schools, this is a practical workflow signal: AI support is moving into everyday resource management, not just classroom-facing prompts.

- **NotebookLM now syncs Drive sources automatically.** Google says NotebookLM can keep Docs, Sheets, and Slides sources up to date as the Drive files change ([Google Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/05/keep-your-sources-up-to-date-with-automatic-Drive-syncing-in-NotebookLM.html)). This strengthens one of the safest school AI patterns: source-grounded notebooks based on teacher-selected materials.

- **Schoology gains a tighter Gemini LTI / NotebookLM path.** Gemini LTI users in PowerSchool Schoology can now add course content directly as NotebookLM sources ([Google Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/05/gemini-lti-update-include-your-lms-sources-when-using-NotebookLM-in-Powerschool-Schoology.html)). This matters because learning-assistant workflows are moving closer to the LMS and course-material layer, where schools can govern access and context more clearly.

- **An AI Ethics Index for K-12 tools is being built.** Boston University and Just Horizons Alliance announced a three-year project to evaluate AI tools marketed to children and schools, including mental health effects, privacy-law compliance, and other safety dimensions ([Axios Boston](https://www.axios.com/local/boston/2026/06/01/ai-ethics-index-boston-university-just-horizons-alliance)). Schools should watch this because procurement needs independent evidence, not only vendor claims.

- **A phased district rollout offers a useful pattern.** Acton-Boxborough in Massachusetts is moving from working group and readiness assessment, to limited high-school Gemini use at teacher discretion in 2026-27, then broader expansion later ([Acton Exchange](https://www.actonexchange.org/district-announces-phased-plan-for-educator-and-student-use-of-generative-ai/)). The sequence is sensible: readiness, professional learning, limited use, review, then scale.

## Sceptic's corner

There is a trap here: the more AI moves into familiar school systems, the easier it becomes to mistake integration for readiness. A standards-tagged Classroom assignment looks reassuring. A NotebookLM grounded in course materials looks safer than a loose chatbot. A Drive organizer that warns about permissions looks mature. All three may be improvements. None of them answers the school question by itself.

The hard part is that these tools are entering the places schools already trust: Classroom, Drive, NotebookLM, and the LMS. That means weak practice can inherit institutional legitimacy just by living inside the right account. If a school has not decided what counts as evidence of learning, analytics can still make shaky evidence look precise. If teachers have not agreed when AI support should be disclosed, source-grounded help can still blur authorship. If leaders have not mapped which data belongs where, a tidy integration can still normalize the wrong flow.

So the sceptical read is not "block it." Blocking will not hold, and some of this plumbing is genuinely useful. The sceptical read is: do not let vendor integration outrun local judgement. Every embedded AI workflow needs a named instructional purpose, a data boundary, a disclosure expectation, and a human review point before it becomes normal school infrastructure.
