# Week ending Sunday, 14 June 2026

## The Week That Was

The weekend handed us the story of the week, after Friday's brief had filed. **On 12 June the US Commerce Department ordered Anthropic to suspend Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5** — and because the company says it cannot reliably separate foreign nationals from anyone else in real time, it pulled both models for *every* user on Earth, paying customers and its own staff included ([Anthropic, 12 June](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access); [Bloomberg, 13 June](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-13/anthropic-says-us-limits-foreign-access-to-fable-5-mythos-5)). Secretary Howard Lutnick's letter to Dario Amodei cited national security and an export-control directive barring access "by any foreign national," reportedly triggered by a jailbreak demonstrated against Mythos 5. Fable 5 launched 9 June as the most capable model the company had shipped; three days later it was a global blackout by government fiat. Anthropic is complying but disputing — it says the vulnerability was minor, previously known, and present in rival frontier models too ([Washington Post, 13 June](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/06/13/anthropic-shuts-down-newest-ai-model-after-us-bans-foreign-use/)).

The rest, briefly: Apple's WWDC turned the managed device into an enforcement surface (lockable browser, multi-app mode, harder assessment configs), and OpenAI's rewritten ChatGPT memory began reaching free accounts. The other genuinely-fresh item: Inside Higher Ed and Generation Lab [published a Student Voice survey on 11 June](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/student-success/academic-life/2026/06/11/why-students-arent-all-ai-and-what-they-want-colleges) — 1,038 students, 203 institutions. Only 7% call themselves "all in" on AI; 40% worry about dependence; just 10% think their institution is handling it well. Students are more sceptical than the staffroom assumes.

## What Does It All Mean?

Here is the question the week quietly posed: **if a school's whole AI policy rests on disclosure — "tell me which tool you used, and what for" — is that instrument still usable?** Three threads from the dailies converged on the same uncomfortable answer.

Thread one, Wednesday: Anthropic's Fable 5 silently routed most biology and chemistry requests to Claude Opus 4.8, so a student often could not know which model answered them ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-fable-5-mythos-5)). (The model is now suspended — see above — but the legibility point it illustrated stands, and indeed sharpens.) Thread two, Thursday: OpenAI's new memory writes a persistent profile the user cannot fully see, that survives a deleted chat, and that the company admits the summary page does not completely list ([implicator.ai, 4 June](https://www.implicator.ai/openai-will-expand-chatgpt-memory-to-free-users-after-5x-compute-cut/)). Thread three, all week: the EU AI Act's chatbot-transparency obligations land 2 August, leaning hard on disclosure as the safeguard.

Now two voices from outside the headlines. A 2025 paper on the AI Act, [*Manipulation and the Danger of Mirrors*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.18387), argues that a label reading "this is AI" does not neutralise an interface engineered to feel reciprocal — knowing it is a machine does not stop the social response. Disclosure assumes a rational reader the architecture is built to bypass. In the other direction, the detection literature keeps hardening: a [January 2026 review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S305047592600093X) finds AI detectors generate more false accusations than correct identifications, with documented racial skew against non-native writers. So the school cannot reliably confirm a disclosure that was made, nor catch one that was withheld.

Put the three threads and the two voices together and the conclusion is not "disclosure is worthless." It is narrower and more useful: **disclosure is a weak primitive to build on, and a worse one every quarter.** The tools are becoming less legible (silent fallbacks, invisible memory) while the regulators lean harder on legibility, and verification fails in both directions.

The shutdown adds a second lesson pointing the same way. A school cannot even *see* which model answered — and now learns it cannot count on the model being there at all. Fable 5 was available to students on Tuesday and gone by government order on Friday, for reasons with nothing to do with the school. That is an argument against building any classroom routine on a single vendor's top model — the same conclusion the disclosure problem reaches from the other side. Legibility says *you cannot fully know what the tool is doing*; continuity says *you cannot assume the tool will still exist*. Both push the weight off the tool and onto the design around it.

This is where last week's three-mode contract earns a second look — not as the answer, but with a correction. Baseline / scaffold / open mode works because it shifts the burden off the student's honesty and onto the *task design*: a baseline task is built so that AI cannot quietly do the work, regardless of what anyone discloses — or which model is up this week. The honest upgrade is to stop treating "students disclose consequential uses" as the load-bearing control in open mode. Treat disclosure as a teaching habit worth cultivating — and assume it will be partial, sometimes impossible, and never the thing that makes an inference valid. The structural choice carries the weight; the label, and the model, are courtesies.

## Behind You! Behind You!

**The WWDC education story has an IT half the dailies underplayed.** Jamf's [11 June education recap](https://www.jamf.com/blog/wwdc26-key-takeaways-for-education-institutions/) reads the keynote for administrators: expanded Platform SSO and offline authentication, QR-code and guest-access onboarding on iPad, and declarative controls for binaries, app config, and Safari permissions. Its theme — "identity is becoming foundational, management more autonomous" — is the unglamorous plumbing behind every device-mode policy. Worth a read before any Apple-side rollout conversation.

**Students want course design, not more AI modules.** Buried in that 11 June Student Voice survey: 24% of students said the thing they most want is authentic learning preserved through better assignment design, and 60% want AI offered as personalised support rather than bolted on ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/student-success/academic-life/2026/06/11/why-students-arent-all-ai-and-what-they-want-colleges)). It is higher-ed and self-report, but it points the same way as the teacher data: the demand is for better tasks, not louder tools.

## The Riff Returns But Slower

- **Live — Fable 5 / Mythos 5 restoration.** When access returns is the open question; as of this weekend both models remain fully suspended, with no court order restoring them, while Anthropic disputes the directive and says it is working to restore access ([Anthropic, 12 June](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access)). The wider Anthropic–government relationship is already adversarial — the DOJ is contesting a separate, earlier order tied to the winter federal-agency ban — so expect this fought, not quietly resolved. The durable signal is the precedent: a frontier model can now be export-controlled out of every classroom at once.
- **2026-06-28 to 07-01** — ISTELive/ASCD. As ever, the value arrives after the stagecraft: released session resources and case studies, not keynote adjectives ([ISTE](https://conference.iste.org/2026/about/faq.php)).
- **2026-06-30** — Colorado's AI Act takes effect, covering high-risk systems including those affecting educational access — the first concrete compliance date in this wave.
- **2026-08-02** — EU AI Act transparency and high-risk education obligations. An Omnibus delay has been discussed but is not law until published; until then the date stands for anyone selling grading or proctoring AI into the EU.
- **Slow burn** — Utah's actual district uptake of Gemini for Education; whether consumer-tier memory controls get more auditable under August's regulatory pressure; the steadily growing — but still thin — base of rigorous causal evidence behind all of it.

## Sceptic's Corner

The hype to squeeze this week is the quiet assumption underneath every "AI governance is maturing" deck: that **transparency is the mature control** — that once a system tells you it is AI, once memory has a settings page, once disclosure is required by law, the responsible-use problem is largely architected.

The week argued the opposite, gently and from multiple directions. The page that lists your ChatGPT memories is admitted to be incomplete. The model on the label is not always the model that answered. The detector that would verify a student's disclosure is, per the January 2026 review, [more likely to be wrong than right](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S305047592600093X) and biased against the students least able to defend themselves. And the regulatory instrument arriving in August leans on exactly the disclosure mechanism a [2025 AI Act paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.18387) says users' own psychology is built to ignore.

And the week's own headline made the point sharper: the most transparent thing imaginable — a model with a card and published safety warnings — was pulled offline *because* of a published safety finding. The lesson from the shutdown is not transparency. It is continuity: a tool you can fully inspect is no use to a class on the morning a government turns it off.

None of that is an argument for cynicism, and certainly not for AI-detector theatre — the detectors are the worst offender here, not the cure. It is an argument for putting the weight somewhere it can bear load. A disclosure norm is a fine thing to teach and a poor thing to enforce; a single best model is a fine thing to use and a poor thing to depend on. The validity of what a student hands in should rest on how the task was built, what evidence of thinking it requires, and what data it keeps off the open web — none of which depend on a label being accurate, or a model still being switched on. Transparency was never going to be the safeguard, and this week it looked less like one than usual.
