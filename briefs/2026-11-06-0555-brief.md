## Top story

**A Google Classroom assignment can now include a tutor and a chat record.** The 4 November Workspace release details the rollout of teacher-assigned Guided Learning. An educator attaches the activity to an assignment and can choose class materials to give Gemini context. The student opens a focused tab with Gemini's side navigation hidden and new chats disabled within that tab. Google says the rollout began on 3 November for Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus, in all Gemini-supported languages, and can take longer than 15 days to appear. Both the teacher and students need Gemini enabled through their group or organisational unit ([Google Workspace Updates, 4 November](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/11/educators-can-now-add-guided-learning-to-assignments-in-Google-Classroom-to-help-students-get-extra-support.html?m=1)).

The teacher's view changes the character of the assignment. Classroom can show AI-generated summaries for the class and for individual students. Teachers can see transcripts from their assigned activities when Gemini activity is on, while conversations students start separately sit outside the teacher's view. The help page says deleting the Classroom post removes student access to the activity but leaves past conversations in place. It also says generated insights can make mistakes and are intended to assist instruction. Google's guidance reserves them for instructional support and cautions against using them for assessment or placement ([Google Classroom Help for teachers](https://support.google.com/edu/classroom/answer/18866217)).

For Ed Tech Coaches in international schools, consider a student using the tool to work through an explanation of why two circuits behave differently. The teacher could supply the class diagram and then see which misconception to revisit tomorrow. The same chat may also include an improvised disclosure or a personal detail the student would never put on a worksheet. The assignment therefore needs two clear purposes. Students should understand the thinking the tutor is there to support, and they should know who can read the conversation. The platform makes it easier to attach the tutoring activity. It takes a teacher's judgment to define its limits and to check whether the learner can explain the circuit after the chat closes.

## Other stories

- **Australia gives primary AI and secondary assessment different policy boundaries.** In a communiqué dated 5 November following their 2 November meeting, Australian education ministers agreed to restrict student use of generative AI in primary schools and in secondary student assessments. They agreed to introduce, trial or consider limits on screen use and left implementation to states, territories and education sectors. A new taskforce will advise ministers on practical measures, with representation from the independent and Catholic sectors among others. Digital devices and AI stay on the ministers' agenda until at least the end of 2027 ([Education Ministers Meeting communiqué, 5 November, p. 1](https://www.education.gov.au/download/20190/communiques-education-ministers-meetings-2026/46401/education-ministers-meeting-communique-november-2026/pdf)). The distinction matters well beyond Australia. A supervised secondary lesson about the reliability of generated claims and a secondary assessment of a student's own argument can have different rules even in the same subject. For international schools that teach across divisions, a single permissions table can hide that distinction unless teachers and students can see where the boundary changes. Implementation will show whether the restrictions are stated in terms teachers can apply to the task in front of them.

- **State AI guidance talks to adults first.** A University of Washington preprint posted on 4 November compared 36 K–12 AI guidance documents from 35 U.S. states and Puerto Rico. The authors coded privacy and academic integrity as addressed in 97% of the documents each, while 83% gave guidance on evaluating vendors. Only 47% mentioned funding or budget and 47% committed to a review cycle. Only 22% addressed students directly and 33% addressed IT staff directly. The authors found specific examples, including vendor rubrics in Montana, Michigan and North Carolina. The study examined what the documents say, leaving actual implementation outside its scope ([Memon and colleagues, 4 November, Table 1 and sections 4.1 and 5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06762v1)). The transferable question for a school is how a principle reaches each person who has to use it. A teacher needs to know where to take a new product for review. The team doing that review needs to know what evidence counts. A student needs to know how to ask for help when a tool crosses a boundary. An elegant policy can leave all three people guessing.

- **The Philippines puts classroom safety into teacher AI training.** On 5 November Microsoft announced a Philippine launch with the Department of Education, reporting that around 100 senior high school teachers attended a day of training in Makati. With training partner Audentes Technologies, the programme aims to train and credential 2,000 public school educators in AI literacy. Microsoft lists two virtual sessions this month and eight live learning sessions from January through May 2027. The launch also brought in scenario-based materials developed with Cyberlite on oversharing, AI-assisted decisions and emotional attachment. Microsoft says the materials drew on co-design workshops with 131 students aged 12–18 across nine schools in India and Singapore ([Microsoft Source Asia, 5 November](https://news.microsoft.com/source/asia/2026/11/05/microsoft-and-deped-to-credential-2000-filipino-teachers-in-ai-literacy-and-equip-classrooms-for-safe-responsible-ai-use/)). The credential count will describe the programme's reach. The classroom test is whether a young person can recognise when a conversation has crossed into private or emotionally consequential territory and identify a trusted adult to turn to. A fictional scenario lets a class practise that judgment without asking anyone to disclose what they have said to a chatbot.

- **A student journal can show where the thinking happened.** University of Sydney researchers Ali Shakiba and Thomas Chaffey coded 962 self-reported prompts from 105 students in an AI-permissive first-year software engineering course. In their 3 November preprint, 84.2% of recorded prompts showed some contribution beyond delegation under the researchers' coding scheme, while explicit constraints on the AI's output were rare. Students chose which interactions to report, and the researchers coded what was visible in the prompt text. Their measure describes the contribution visible there and supplies no direct measure of learning ([Shakiba and Chaffey, 3 November, methods, results and limitations](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.04783v1)). In a secondary design class, the useful adaptation is a small check on one decision. A student can record what they had already tried, what help they sought, and which result they accepted or rejected and why. A teacher can then ask them to defend that choice against a new constraint. That would put the emphasis on judgment and keep the journal short enough to read alongside the work.

- **Burnaby's recovery and its exposure investigation are on different clocks.** In a 3 November update on its September ransomware incident, the British Columbia district confirmed that attackers had copied and stolen files containing personal information. It said it had received reports of a claimed dark-web release, believed some district files might be included, and had brought in an external expert to establish the extent. The district lists registration documents used to establish residency and records of specialised learning support among the information that may have been affected. It says phones were restored on 28 September and access to MyEducation, including the family portal, on 1 October ([Burnaby Schools, updated 3 November](https://burnabyschools.ca/incident-impacting-operations/)). For international schools, the detail about residency is a reminder that an ordinary enrolment folder can contain identity documents as well as contact details. An incident review can therefore ask what duplicate exports exist and who can open them, alongside the question of when the platform came back. A family needs a credible answer about its own records even after it can sign in again.

## Sceptic's corner

The sales pitch for an AI summary is that it can turn a roomful of conversations into a clear picture of where the class is stuck. The Classroom launch makes that appealing. A teacher planning tomorrow's lesson could see repeated questions before another student puts up a hand. Google's own help describes the themes as a sample from the interactions and says summaries have usage limits. Its [student guidance](https://support.google.com/edu/classroom/answer/18866069) also says students will see a message telling them which conversations their teacher can view. Those facts matter when interpreting who appears in a summary and what they felt comfortable writing.

Consider a class report that flags confusion about fractions. One student typed a detailed question, one discussed the problem with a partner, and one copied a worked example before opening the activity. The summary can surface a question the teacher might otherwise miss. The class still needs a common piece of work if the teacher wants to know how widespread the difficulty is. An exit ticket asking everyone to explain why two differently written fractions represent the same quantity gives the teacher something to compare across the class, including students who had little to say in chat.

There is real value in finding a good question faster. Give the dashboard that job. Evidence that a student understands the idea still has to come from something the student can do and explain.
