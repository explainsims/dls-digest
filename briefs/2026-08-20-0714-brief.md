## Top story

**Students are asking AI to make them think, not to save them from thinking.** A preprint submitted on 18 August builds a four-part account of AI risk in education — cognition, agency, emotional wellbeing and ethics — from learning-science literature and the first, unaided drafts of 49 International Baccalaureate students. The students, median age 17, attended three German-speaking schools in Switzerland and wrote on whether AI changes what it means to learn. Eighty per cent described reliance on AI as reducing thinking. Across the essays, the preferred alternative was remarkably consistent: systems that delay answers, prompt recall, ask questions and support reflection rather than complete the intellectual work ([Favero, Pérez-Ortiz, Käser and Oliver, 18 August](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17451)).

The authors turn that into a useful design rule: AI should scaffold effort rather than substitute for it. Their proposed harm cycle matters because the dimensions can reinforce one another. Offloading thought can reduce confidence in acting independently; lower agency can invite more offloading; surveillance, bias and anxiety can further weaken willingness to take intellectual risks. That is more convincing than treating hallucination, cheating, motivation and privacy as four unrelated policy boxes.

The evidence boundary is equally important. These are 49 essays from a relatively homogeneous Swiss cohort, not an intervention, outcome study or representative survey. The 80% figure records what students believe, not a measured decline in thinking. Even so, the population is unusually close to many international schools' context: older IB learners describing the trade-off before an adult framework was imposed on them. A practical test for any classroom AI activity follows. Identify the cognitive move students must still perform without assistance, the moment the system withholds completion, and the artefact that shows independent judgement afterwards. If those three things cannot be named, the activity may improve output while quietly removing the learning it is supposed to reveal.

## Other stories

- **Google is making Chat the doorway to the rest of Workspace, but the door does not open until 26 August.** Ask Gemini in Google Chat is scheduled for a gradual rollout from 26 August, not live today. It will search Gmail, Drive and Calendar, catch users up on conversations, draft content, create images, schedule meetings and manage tasks from a persistent session in Chat. The old Chat side panel disappears for affected English-language accounts; its conversation history will not migrate, and Gems will remain available only through side panels in other Workspace apps. Access requires Gemini in Workspace for Chat, Workspace Intelligence across apps and user enrolment in Smart Features. It is available to Education only through Google AI Pro for Education, with promotional higher usage limits through 1 October and unspecified limits after that ([Google Workspace Updates, 19 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/ask-gemini-in-chat.html)). For Google Workspace schools, this is less a Chat feature than a permissions map: cross-service retrieval becomes a single user gesture, while three separate administrative and user settings decide whether it works. Before anyone trains colleagues on the shortcut, the useful inventory is what it can search, what it can change, where its history is exported and which limits replace the promotional window.

- **A learning-analytics recommendation can now fail a timetable check before it reaches a student.** SC2R, submitted on 18 August, sits after the familiar at-risk prediction. It turns model features into discrete intervention plans, uses integer programming to select compact actions, represents them in a lightweight RDF vocabulary, then validates timing, effort budget, immutable attributes and resource availability through SHACL rules. The authors test the pipeline offline on the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset at two snapshots before an assessment and report that semantic validation rejects infeasible plans that an optimisation-only approach would retain ([Le, Abel and Laforge, 18 August](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17618)). That is a better architecture for LMS decision support than a risk colour plus an AI-written suggestion: a recommendation to complete three unavailable activities in the remaining week should fail mechanically. But the paper does not show that any plan causes better outcomes, nor that teachers or students would accept it. Schools evaluating early-warning systems should ask for both layers separately — prediction validity and intervention feasibility — and keep the final support decision with a person who knows what the data cannot see.

- **Seesaw is asking AI to grade the reasoning captured in a screen recording.** Show What You Know lets elementary students draw and narrate their problem-solving, then grades the submitted recording against a teacher-provided rubric, produces an overall score and short summary, and indicates how well the concept was demonstrated. Seesaw says rollout began on 5 August and announced it publicly on 13 August; the workflow sits inside its existing platform rather than adding another login. The company also says more than 10 million students used Seesaw to make learning visible this year ([Seesaw press release, 13 August](https://www.einnews.com/pr_news/934008679/seesaw-launches-ai-powered-assessment-experience-to-help-schools-turn-student-thinking-into-actionable-insight)). The proposition is attractive because multimodal explanations contain evidence a multiple-choice item cannot, while reviewing every recording is expensive. The missing evidence is the agreement rate between the AI and skilled teachers across age, accent, language, subject and rubric quality. A school pilot should hide the AI score from teachers initially, double-mark a stratified sample and record disagreement types. Otherwise a richer student artefact may enter through the front door only to be flattened by an unvalidated summary at the back.

- **Southeast Asia's AI-literacy work is being attached to leadership capacity, not dropped in as another student module.** SEAMEO STEM-ED reported on 18 August on a 3–7 August mission in Indonesia with SEAMEO SEAMOLEC, universities, government representatives and regional organisations. The conversations connected PISA 2025 and 2029 competencies — including science, climate, media and AI literacy — with leadership development and institutional collaboration. A related meeting with the ASEAN Foundation explored regional work on STEM, AI literacy and capacity building; SEAMEO also points to SEA-TEP, which trained more than 1,800 educators and reached about 2,400 beneficiaries across Cambodia, Indonesia, Kazakhstan, Malaysia and Thailand from 2023 to 2025 ([SEAMEO STEM-ED, 18 August](https://www.seameo-stemed.org/news-and-events/collaboration-indonesia/)). This is a report of meetings and prior reach, not a funded new programme or evidence of impact. Its value is the unit of change. International-school AI literacy will not hold if it lives only in student competencies; leaders need the capacity to choose tools, resource teacher learning and stop weak initiatives as well as launch them.

- **Google Meet's new shared-screen mode protects the presenter's desktop, but Education editions are absent from the beta.** Room Display mode detects an external display in extended mode and splits Meet into a clean shared view for the TV or projector and a private controller view on the laptop. The private window retains controls, participant list, chat, presentation tools and the Gemini panel, reducing the familiar risk of showing browser tabs or speaker material to the room. Gradual beta rollout began on 19 August, requires a Chromium browser with Window Management permission, has no admin control, and is listed for Business, Enterprise, Frontline, Essentials and Nonprofits — not Education ([Google Workspace Updates, 19 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/elevate-your-google-meet-experience-for-shared-meeting-spaces-with-Room-Display-mode.html)). That makes it a watch rather than a recommendation for international schools. The design is still worth borrowing immediately: any hybrid classroom or professional-learning setup should separate the audience surface from the operator surface by default. Privacy failures in live presentation are usually mundane, which is precisely why interface design beats another reminder to close tabs.

## On the radar

- **Calendar blocking now propagates across the Google account.** A gradual rollout began on 18 August: blocking a Google-account user in Calendar removes the current event, stops future invitations and adds that person to the account-wide blocklist across supported Google products. Invitations from non-Google users still require Gmail blocking, and there is no admin control ([Google Workspace Updates, 18 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/managing-unsolicited-event-invitations-with-user-blocking-in-Google-Calendar.html)). Watch whether the cross-product consequence is visible enough for students and staff; a calendar-spam defence can become a wider communication block than the user intended.

- **The screen-time backlash is moving from parent debate into legislation.** George Washington University reported on 13 August that lawmakers in 17 US states had introduced or debated measures addressing classroom screen time or device use as of July, with other states considering restrictions for younger pupils ([GW Media Relations, 13 August](https://mediarelations.gwu.edu/media-tip-sheet-k-12-schools-are-rethinking-laptop-usage)). It is a university media tip sheet pointing to a third-party tracker, not a legal analysis. The travelling signal for international schools is that device-rich learning increasingly needs an educational-purpose defence at activity level, not merely a digital-strategy defence at school level.

- **A national AI-literacy landscape study was scheduled for public briefing yesterday.** The National Girls Collaborative Project scheduled a 19 August webinar to present findings and recommendations from research into AI literacy across K–12 STEM learning environments. The initiative was developed with Ingenia Learning and STEM Next Opportunity Fund, with AMD Foundation support, and is framed around safe, effective and ethical use plus meaningful youth inclusion in decisions about AI ([NGCP webinar, 19 August](https://ngcproject.regfox.com/ngcpwebinar081926)). The event page does not confirm that the session ran or expose its methods or findings. The useful next check is whether the resulting resources supply observable learner competencies and implementation evidence, rather than another broad list of awareness topics.

## Sceptic's corner

Scaffolding rather than substitution is a strong rule and a dangerously easy slogan. Almost every education chatbot can be described as a scaffold if the vendor points to a question prompt, a hint button or a learning-mode label. The 18 August paper is more demanding: the distinction concerns what happens to cognition, agency, wellbeing and ethics over repeated use, not what the interface says it intends ([Favero and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17451)).

That makes the rule harder to operationalise than it first appears. A system can ask three Socratic questions and then deliver the answer; a student can learn the shortest path through the questions; a teacher can count completed dialogue as engagement; and everyone can leave with a polished transcript and no independent performance. The paper's 49 students offer a thoughtful design preference, but they do not show that a preferred interface changes behaviour or learning. Nor does withholding an answer automatically help: poorly timed friction can burden novices, multilingual learners or students who need accessibility support.

The usable judgement is not whether a product contains scaffolding. It is whether assistance fades, students still make the consequential intellectual move, and performance survives after the tool is removed. Schools should test those three conditions in the task, not accept the vendor's pedagogical adjective. A scaffold is temporary support with a destination; if it never comes down, it is the building.
