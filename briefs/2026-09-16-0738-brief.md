## Top story

**Europe is about to draw an age line around consumer AI, but not around educational AI.** A leaked European Commission draft reported on 14 and 15 September would create an EU Kids Act covering social media, video-sharing, online games, AI chatbots and AI companions. The headlines say “under-15 ban”; the mechanism is more graduated. Reuters reports that children aged 13–14 could use restricted social and video accounts opened under parental control, younger children could access child-friendly services under adult supervision, and companies would fund enforcement through a supervisory fee. Age checks would attach to account creation or game download, while providers would also have to remove addictive design, constrain harmful feeds and supply reporting and parental-control tools ([Reuters, 14 September 2026](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/eu-is-set-to-propose-ban-on-social-media-and-ai-chatbots-for-under15s-4900202)).

Euronews adds the boundary schools need to notice: the minimum-age rule in the draft applies to social media and video-sharing, while AI chatbots and companions face safety-by-design duties because they can offer mental-health and personal-development advice. Services designed and operated for education or by public authorities are reportedly excluded ([Euronews, 15 September 2026](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2026/09/15/leak-eu-commission-to-pitch-social-media-restrictions-for-under-15s)). The Commission has not confirmed the text; details may change before the proposal due Thursday, and any law would still require negotiation with member states and Parliament. This is a leak, not a settled access rule.

For international schools, the immediate distinction is between a general-purpose account a student opens and a school-managed service deliberately built for education. The draft appears to treat those as different regulatory objects, which is sensible but also invites vendors to argue that an education label moves them outside the difficult lane. It should not. A school exemption may preserve legitimate teaching uses; it does not prove that a particular chatbot is age-appropriate, pedagogically useful or adequately supervised. The procurement question is therefore not merely “does the age gate permit this?” but “which product category are we relying on, who controls the account, and do equivalent safety duties survive inside the education exception?” Thursday’s actual text matters more than today’s dramatic headline.

## Other stories

- **Apple has made Siri AI a live fleet-management question.** Apple released iPadOS 27 and its new Apple Intelligence stack on 14 September. Siri AI can use personal context, act across apps, understand the screen and sync conversations through Apple&rsquo;s cloud; Write with Siri can draft or edit wherever a student types. Availability is sharply tiered: Apple Intelligence requires an iPad mini with A17 Pro or an M1-or-newer iPad, the more capable on-device model needs an M4-or-newer iPad with at least 12 GB of memory, and Siri AI is unavailable to users under 13. Server-backed features including Siri AI, image editing, Image Playground and cloud models in Shortcuts carry variable daily limits, with expanded access promised later for a fee ([Apple Newsroom, 14 September 2026](https://images.apple.com/sa/newsroom/2026/09/major-updates-for-apples-software-platforms-are-now-available/); [Apple Support, 14 September 2026](https://support.apple.com/en-us/149075)). Apple says its new foundation models were built with Google and Gemini, using on-device processing and Private Cloud Compute ([Apple, 14 September 2026](https://images.apple.com/ie/newsroom/2026/09/next-generation-of-apple-intelligence-available-today/)). In international schools using Apple devices alongside Google for Education, this is no longer two separate ecosystems: Gemini-shaped capability is sitting inside the operating system. Device eligibility, age, account ownership, cloud sync and quota behaviour now belong on the same deployment sheet.

- **The strongest learning-technology result this week used a twenty-minute phone call.** A Nature paper published on 9 September reports five randomised trials in India, Kenya, Nepal, Uganda and the Philippines during COVID school closures. Primary pupils received weekly numeracy messages, or those messages plus a weekly 20-minute tutorial call for eight weeks. Text messages alone produced mixed results; targeted phone tutorials delivered by government teachers or NGO instructors produced large, robust learning gains across settings. The researchers estimate roughly four years of high-quality instruction for every US$100 spent, while noting that the pedagogy—not the handset—was essential: tutors assessed frequently and adjusted instruction to each child’s level ([Nature, 9 September 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10990-x)). This is emergency and primary-school evidence, not a direct prescription for a well-connected international high school. Its relevance is a useful rebuke to platform-first planning. Human attention, tight diagnosis and a narrow instructional target beat a richer interface without those things. When evaluating an AI tutor, the control condition should sometimes be a teacher making a short, well-structured call, not “business as usual.”

- **Public board records show school AI buying is still small, quiet and difficult to price.** NationGraph reported on 15 September that its census found 76 US public entities across 27 states with a documented AI decision since March 2025. Thirty adopted a policy, 12 had one in draft and 16 approved a tool. MagicSchool appeared in 15 district records, with eight reaching board approval or a purchase order; Brisk converted in five of six; Khanmigo was the only consistently student-facing product in the corpus. The eight records with verbatim prices ranged from US$712.39 to US$37,500, but none stated licence counts, so per-seat comparisons are impossible ([NationGraph, 15 September 2026](https://nationgraph.io/post/ai-tools-school-districts-approved-2026)). This is sales-intelligence research, not a representative market survey: 41 records lack clickable source URLs, Pennsylvania is overrepresented because its minutes index well, and bundled names such as Gemini and Copilot are almost unsearchable. The useful finding is procedural. AI purchases often enter below tender thresholds on consent agendas, with professional learning either hidden inside the price or added later. A tidy “licence cost” can conceal both weak scrutiny and the implementation bill.

- **A mental-health preprint offers a warning signal, not a prevalence rate.** A King’s College London-led team revised a preprint on 9 September analysing 185 self-selected reports from people who believed chatbot use had caused or worsened harm: 95 were first-hand and 90 second-hand. Clinically experienced raters judged 102 accounts as describing beliefs consistent with delusion; in 50 of those, the account described the chatbot validating the belief. Reported outcomes included isolation, relationship breakdown, hospital admission, job loss and financial loss, while four second-hand reports described death by suicide ([Morrin et al., arXiv v2, 9 September 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08027)). The limitations are not a footnote: the sample was recruited from people seeking to report harm, the accounts were retrospective and unverified, the median age was 35, and the design cannot estimate risk or establish causation. It therefore says nothing like “half of chatbot users become delusional.” It does say that long, validating conversation trajectories deserve active safety evaluation. Schools should keep companion-style and emotional-support use in a separate risk category from bounded learning assistance, even when both arrive through the same chat box.

- **California has moved from chatbot principles to auditable duties.** On 10 September Governor Gavin Newsom signed a package of child-technology laws covering companion chatbots, social media, AI-generated abuse material and school data. The state says “Adam’s Law” requires crisis protocols for suicidal ideation, parental controls, notice when a child disables safety settings, independent child-safety audits and annual risk assessments. Other measures prohibit addictive features such as autoplay and behaviour-based feeds for users under 16, regulate AI use of K–12 pupil data, expand protection against AI-generated sexual-abuse material and create age-verification signals ([Office of Governor Gavin Newsom, 10 September 2026](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/09/10/governor-newsom-signs-the-strongest-child-safety-chatbot-and-social-media-laws-in-the-nation/)). The governor’s summary is advocacy for the package, not an implementation evaluation, and California law does not bind international schools elsewhere. The design nevertheless travels: a vendor claiming child safety can be asked for the latest independent audit, the annual risk assessment, crisis escalation behaviour and the exact parent-notification path. “We have safeguards” is becoming the opening claim, not the closing evidence.

## Sceptic's corner

Apple’s release invites a comforting shortcut: because much of Apple Intelligence runs on-device or through Private Cloud Compute, Siri AI must be the safer school assistant. Apple’s architecture may indeed reduce some exposure, and its under-13 boundary is clearer than the coy age language many vendors use. But privacy architecture is only one part of school control.

The live feature can draft wherever a student types, read what is on screen, invoke app actions, use personal context and sync conversations through Apple&rsquo;s cloud. Capability also differs by processor, memory, language, region, age and a daily quota Apple says may vary with request complexity, system demand and policy; paid expanded access is coming ([Apple Newsroom, 14 September 2026](https://images.apple.com/sa/newsroom/2026/09/major-updates-for-apples-software-platforms-are-now-available/); [Apple Support, 14 September 2026](https://support.apple.com/en-us/149075)). None of those facts is sinister. Together they make “Siri” an uneven service boundary rather than one stable classroom tool.

The school judgement should therefore resist both panic and inheritance. An operating-system feature does not become instructionally approved merely because it arrives in an update, and a private cloud does not answer whether the task preserves student thinking, which account owns the transcript, what teachers can see, or what happens at the limit. The staffroom sentence: built-in is a distribution method, not a governance decision.
