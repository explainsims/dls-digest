## Top story

**PISA’s first AI snapshot says usage is widespread; it does not say that chatbots caused the scores.** The OECD released *PISA 2025 Results* on 8 September, covering more than 760,000 fifteen-year-olds in 91 countries and economies. It finds a sobering longer arc: across OECD countries, mathematics scores fell 22 points and reading 28 points between 2015 and 2025, while science—the cycle’s main domain—declined more modestly. Twenty per cent of fifteen-year-olds are now estimated to be low performers in all three subjects, up from 16% in 2022 ([OECD, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2026/09/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_5265bfb1/full-report.html)). The report’s new chatbot questions will attract the loudest headlines. By 2025, a majority of students in most participating systems reported using an AI chatbot for schoolwork; only 14% across OECD countries said they had never or almost never used one for any of the four asked-about purposes: summarising assigned texts, preliminary research, drafting, or helping them learn ([OECD, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)).

The pattern is neither “AI works” nor “AI ruins learning.” For specific tasks, non-users generally outperformed users; moderate users of summarising and preliminary-research tools tended to outperform limited and frequent users. For the broader purpose “to help me learn”, students reporting weekly use performed similarly to non-users after accounting for socio-economic profile, while rare and almost-daily users tended to score lower. The OECD also records differing access, dispositions and help-seeking patterns among the groups, then reasonably concludes only that use is “not a prerequisite for success” ([OECD, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)). This is cross-sectional self-report, not a trial; a student’s prior attainment, task, teacher and reason for opening a chatbot all travel with the result.

For international schools, the useful move is less a blanket rule than an assessment question: what kind of assistance is being invited, at what point in a task, and what evidence of understanding remains after it? A weekly AI check-in, source comparison, decision log or short oral defence will tell schools more than a usage ban or a dashboard count. PISA makes the urgency plain. It does not supply the causal policy answer for an individual school.

## Other stories

- **UNESCO has put age, procurement, safety and total cost into one public consultation instead of treating them as separate IT chores.** On 8 September, alongside Digital Learning Week, UNESCO released a deliberative-governance discussion paper and a set of background papers on digital and AI sovereignty, age-appropriateness, learner safety, procurement, total cost of ownership and explanation. The common question is whether an education system can exercise agency while AI diffuses faster than its regulation and capacity; comments are invited through 15 October, with policy briefs planned for Digital Learning Week 2027 ([UNESCO, 8 September 2026](https://www.unesco.org/en/digital-education/artificial-intelligence/consultation)). The merit is the bundle. A school can have a sensible age threshold and still sign a poor data contract; it can clear a privacy review and still underestimate tokens, integration work, monitoring and exit costs. These are commissioned policy papers, not evidence that a particular governance model improves learning, and the consultation means their recommendations remain open to challenge. But they make a clean local discipline available: every proposed AI use needs an educational purpose, an age case, a safety and data case, a cost-and-exit case, and a person accountable for each.

- **New York City has chosen an age-band moratorium, not an all-school retreat.** The city announced on 2 September that public-school students through eighth grade will face a one-year ban on student-facing generative-AI software while officials assess its effects. It will pair that with twice-yearly AI-literacy classes for all high-school students and supervised pilots for some high-school career and professional-learning programmes; companion chatbots are barred at every grade. Teachers may still use AI for planning and operations ([Associated Press, 2 September 2026](https://apnews.com/article/zohran-mamdani-ai-ban-nyc-schools-647f6a968eea0399521b7934418b1aff)). The policy’s useful distinction is between adult workflow, adolescent learning under supervision, and younger pupils—not a fiction that all uses carry the same risk. It is also a political and operational choice, not experimental proof that a one-year prohibition will improve attainment. International schools should resist importing the headline as a rule. Its sharper question is whether an age boundary is tied to developmental rationale, supported alternatives and a plan to evaluate what happens, rather than being a press-release-shaped substitute for all three.

- **Google’s refreshed educator series has practical grain, and the usual vendor-shaped definition of success.** Google added new material to its no-cost AI Educator Series on 3 September, saying the bite-sized sessions take under 15 minutes to earn a badge. The new examples include automating recurring simple tasks, Gemini’s Guided Learning feature, Deep Research for higher-education inquiry, and an “AI Quest” classroom activity; a free virtual Badge-a-thon is scheduled for 19 September ([Google, 3 September 2026](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/new-ai-educator-trainings-september-2026/)). Google says it heard two familiar needs from educators: practical application and a community in which to test it. That is more useful than another generic literacy module, especially for schools using Google Workspace: staff can rehearse a bounded routine rather than admire a capability slide. But badges measure completion, not whether a teacher chose an appropriate task, protected student data, caught an error or improved learning. Treat the series as vendor-primary professional-learning material—good for a small, voluntary try-out with a before-and-after task—not as independent evidence that the tools save time or personalise learning safely.

- **OpenAI is funding access for journalism students, which makes workflow literacy part of the curriculum and vendor influence part of the picture.** On 8 September, OpenAI said it would provide more than 400 ChatGPT Edu subscriptions to interested graduate students and faculty at CUNY’s Newmark J-School and Northwestern’s Medill School through partnerships with the Tow-Knight Center and Knight Lab. The company frames the programme around public-record analysis, archives, translation, new formats and responsible experimentation; it says Edu workspaces have privacy, permissions and administrative controls, and that their data are not used to train its models ([OpenAI, 8 September 2026](https://openai.com/index/supporting-journalism-from-classrooms-to-newsrooms/)). There is a sensible curricular proposition here: students entering a profession being reshaped by AI should learn verification, disclosure and judgement with the tools present. The limitation is equally visible. This is OpenAI describing a partnership that gives its product to future professionals, not an independent study of learning or newsroom quality. Any school borrowing the model should make tool fluency subordinate to the discipline’s own standards—show sources, retain human editorial responsibility, disclose material assistance—and keep an exit route if the product, price or terms change.

## On the radar

- **UNESCO’s ministerial statement is the document to inspect, not the conference branding.** Digital Learning Week runs from 8 to 11 September and UNESCO says more than 25 education ministers will adopt a joint statement on sustaining education as a common good in the age of AI. The programme also promises a policy-brief compilation spanning safety, sovereignty, procurement, costs and age-appropriateness ([UNESCO, 25 August; updated 2 September 2026](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/education-age-ai-ministerial-statement-be-adopted-unescos-digital-learning-week)). Until the final text is public, this is an intention rather than a policy settlement. Watch whether it names enforceable public-interest tests, financing and accountability, specifies who must meet them, or settles for the familiar human-centred vocabulary.

- **Google’s 19 September Badge-a-thon is worth watching for the curriculum, not the prize counter.** The company describes a ten-hour virtual event of 30-minute blocks: teacher lightning talks, module walkthroughs, live badging and prizes, built around its AI Educator Series ([Google, 3 September 2026](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/new-ai-educator-trainings-september-2026/)). It is a current, vendor-run professional-learning event rather than third-party evaluation. Watch which examples survive the live format: data boundaries, error checking, assessment design and consent are more revealing than another parent-letter prompt. The actual materials and facilitator guidance matter more than the event’s claimed alignment.

- **Samsung and the Mark Cuban Foundation have announced a new AI curriculum, but the evidence claim has not travelled with it.** Their 8 September release says the programme will bring curriculum to classrooms and cites a figure that 97% of teachers need more support or resources for emerging technology. The release does not state the survey’s sample, method or question wording ([Samsung, 8 September 2026](https://news.samsung.com/us/samsung-mark-cuban-foundation-sending-classrooms-back-school-new-ai-curriculum/)). Watch for the actual curriculum, age range, teacher preparation and independent evaluation; “teachers need support” is plausible, but a press-release percentage is not yet a procurement basis. Claimed demand is also not evidence that the curriculum changes teaching or learning.

## Sceptic's corner

The headline PISA invites is already doing the rounds: students who use AI do worse, so schools should ban it. The OECD has given that argument a real observational pattern. For specific schoolwork tasks, students who do not use chatbots generally score higher; frequent users often score below moderate users. But it has not given anyone a causal ban result. The data come from students’ reports of their own use in 2025, alongside a single assessment; they cannot tell whether a chatbot weakened learning, whether struggling students sought more help, or whether particular teachers set the sort of tasks that make AI a shortcut.

Weekly users who said they used AI “to help me learn” performed at a similar level to non-users after socio-economic adjustment, while the OECD found differences in access, curiosity and help-seeking between use profiles ([OECD, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)). New York’s moratorium may be a defensible precaution for younger pupils, but it is a governance choice made under uncertainty, not the policy conclusion hidden in an international correlation.

The wrong response is to use PISA as cover for either extreme: unrestricted chatbot access because some moderate users do fine, or a prohibition because some frequent users do badly. Make the local question observable. What does the learner do before asking, what must they explain afterwards, which tasks allow which help, and what happens when the tool is wrong? The staffroom line is: PISA has measured a relationship; it has not done our thinking for us.
