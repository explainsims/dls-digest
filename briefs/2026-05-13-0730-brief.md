# Wednesday, 13 May 2026

## Top story

**Instructure paid the ransom. ShinyHunters sent shred logs. Congress opened an investigation the same day. The leak deadline didn't pass quietly — it was bought.**

Instructure confirmed Monday, 11 May 2026 — one day ahead of its own publicly known deadline — that it had reached an agreement with ShinyHunters, paid an undisclosed sum, and "received digital confirmation of data destruction (shred logs)." The company added that "no Instructure customers will be extorted as a result of this incident, publicly or otherwise" and that the agreement "covers all impacted Instructure customers." The Hacker News, Inside Higher Ed and The Register all confirmed the settlement Monday and Tuesday; the company's apology for "lack of transparency" was bundled into the same statement. ([Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/05/instructure-reaches-ransom-agreement.html), [Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/tech-innovation/administrative-tech/2026/05/11/instructure-pays-ransom-canvas-hackers))

Late Monday, House Homeland Security Committee Chairman Andrew Garbarino (R-NY) sent Instructure CEO Steve Daly a letter requesting a committee briefing on "the circumstances of both intrusions, the nature and volume of data accessed... and the adequacy of the company's coordination with federal law enforcement and CISA." The Register reported the letter Tuesday. No public statement yet from the company's PE owner. Meanwhile the legal track has consolidation potential: *Hinds v. KKR* in SDNY (1:26-cv-03816, filed Fri 8 May, Yagman PLLC) now sits alongside *Peterman v. Instructure Inc.* in D. Utah (2:26-cv-00374, filed Tue 5 May, Milberg PLLC). Two filings on opposite coasts inside a week mirrors the *In re PowerSchool* consolidation arc; Hinds is the load-bearing one because it names the PE owner under that precedent. ([The Register — Congress investigates](https://www.theregister.com/cyber-crime/2026/05/12/congress-investigates-canvas-breach-after-instructure-cuts-deal-with-shinyhunters/5238927), [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/kkr-instructure-sued-after-data-breach-of-canvas-edtech-tool), [ClassAction.org](https://www.classaction.org/data-breach-lawsuits/instructure-may-2026))

**Why this matters at the rubric, sharply this week.** Three new rubric axes get added: (1) does the vendor's incident-response policy *prohibit* ransom payment, or leave it discretionary; (2) what is the vendor's relationship with its capital owner on extortion-spend authorisations; (3) how does the contract document recourse if attacker assurances are later breached. The congressional letter elevates these to inquiry status the same week procurement committees are setting their rubric register, and Steinhauer's caveat — "data destruction claims cannot be independently verified" — is the polite version of the structural problem. The Friday Toddle "PYP Refresh" webinar lands inside this window: the question to bring is no longer "how is Toddle's architecture different from Canvas" but "what is Toddle's documented policy on ransom payment, and which capital owner authorises it."

## Sceptic's corner

**"Shred logs" is a category that does not exist in computer-security forensics.** The phrase appears nowhere in NIST SP 800-61, the SANS Incident Handler's Handbook, or CISA breach-response playbooks. It is a marketing term — likely the attacker's — describing a textual artefact claiming a file set was deleted. No cryptographic primitive produces "proof of deletion" the way a hash proves integrity. ShinyHunters provided a log file claiming destruction; Instructure's acceptance and onward representation that "no customers will be extorted" is *a business decision*, not a forensic finding. The harder version of Steinhauer's caveat: Instructure has paid a known extortion group on unverifiable assurances and is representing those assurances to the market as fact. That is not a security failure; it is the structure of the deal. The rubric question is whether that structure is acceptable.

**The Garbarino letter is procurement-quality evidence, not political theatre.** A House Homeland Security Committee chair's letter to a vendor CEO requesting a briefing on "the adequacy of the company's coordination with federal law enforcement and CISA" is the kind of artefact procurement committees can cite directly — proof not of wrongdoing but that the vendor's incident response is an open question of public-interest concern. The CISA-coordination axis is sharper for international schools: a vendor whose US K-12 customer base sits under a federal coordination-adequacy review is a vendor whose multi-jurisdictional compliance posture is in flux. A rubric entry citing the congressional letter is defensible-on-paper input even if weighted lightly.

**The Anthropic-Canvas embedding deserves a procurement-rubric distinction the field has not yet drawn.** "Canvas breach → consider alternatives" doesn't survive contact with the embedding question. An AI-in-LMS architecture where the model vendor sits on separate compliance posture and separate incident-response policy is *structurally different* from an AI-in-suite architecture where the AI is delivered by the LMS vendor directly. Rubric distinction: single-throat-to-choke (faster procurement, single SLA, single blast radius) versus separated-vendor (slower procurement, multiple SLAs, narrower per-vendor blast radius)? Most rubrics collapse this into one "AI capability" cell; the breach makes the case for splitting it.
