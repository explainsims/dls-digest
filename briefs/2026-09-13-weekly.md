# Week ending Sunday, 13 September 2026

## The Week That Was

If you are catching up, the week had a spine and a mood. The spine was the arrival of *PISA 2025* on 8 September, the first international snapshot of how fifteen-year-olds actually use AI for schoolwork ([OECD, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2026/09/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_5265bfb1/full-report.html)). The mood was governance running hot: ministers adopted a joint statement in Paris ([UNESCO, 9 September 2026](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/education-ministers-call-education-remain-common-good-age-ai-unescos-digital-learning-week?hub=722)), a union-and-Microsoft school-AI standard acquired contractual teeth, and Instructure floated Canvas Lite as a free, individually owned LMS. The connective tissue: "available" and "governed" kept drifting apart. On the weekend itself, Toddle's free *How Schools AI* meetup ran on Saturday 12 September, sessions recorded for later release ([Toddle](https://www.toddleapp.com/events/how-schools-ai/)) — so the artefacts, not the registration counter, are what to judge. Otherwise it was quiet.

## What Does It All Mean?

PISA handed everyone a correlation and a temptation. Across systems, students who used chatbots for schoolwork often scored below those who did not, and moderate users tended to beat frequent users ([OECD, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_73451bc5-en/full-report/student-school-life-and-beyond_861e5904.html)). The OECD was careful to call this cross-sectional self-report, not cause. So which way does the arrow run — is the chatbot degrading learning, or are struggling students simply the ones reaching for it? A population snapshot cannot say. Two bodies of evidence from outside this week's headlines can put real pressure on the question.

The sharper one landed quietly earlier this year. Analysing a ten-year panel of 3.2 million ALEKS maths interactions, Rismanchian and colleagues used a quasi-experimental design that split tasks into those a chatbot can do (text word problems) and those it cannot (interactive graph problems). Study time on the AI-susceptible problems fell after ChatGPT's release — 26.9% cumulatively for college students over eleven quarters, 31.3% for high-schoolers, 9.0% for middle-schoolers, and no detectable change for Grade 5. The tell is what proctoring did: among college students the divergence "vanishes entirely under proctoring," and proctored retention items showed a 25% decline in the odds of a correct answer while unproctored scores rose. The authors call the population-level signature "cognitive surrender" ([Rismanchian and colleagues, arXiv, July 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21629)).

That is the mechanism PISA can only gesture at. Students quietly stop doing the specific cognitive work the tool absorbs, and the deficit stays invisible until the tool is removed. The PNAS mathematics trial this digest has cited before found the same shape from the other direction: unrestricted GPT-4 lifted assisted practice 48%, then its users scored 17% below the no-AI group once it was taken away ([Bastani and colleagues, PNAS, 25 June 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)). Performance-with-the-tool and capability-without-it are different quantities, and only the second is learning.

Put together, PISA measured a shadow at population scale; the mechanism literature names the object casting it. The useful school move is therefore not the ban-or-allow argument the correlation keeps being drafted into. It is task design. If the AI-vulnerable step is the step you assess, you are measuring the tool — so build tasks where the graded thinking is the graph, the defence, the revision decision, and keep at least one AI-off checkpoint, because the gap only reappears when the tool is gone.

And the grade gradient complicates this month's prohibitions. New York drew its ban through eighth grade, on the intuition that younger pupils need the most shielding; the ALEKS panel found the study-time collapse concentrated among high-schoolers, with Grade 5 unchanged. If the offloading that erodes durable knowledge runs heaviest among the oldest students, an age line aimed downward governs the cohort least affected and leaves the exam classes — the one place a school is least willing to intervene — untouched.

## Behind You! Behind You!

**Los Angeles quietly matched New York.** On 2 September, the same day New York City announced its moratorium, Los Angeles Unified — the country's second-largest district — restricted all student access to generative AI for 2026-27, rolling back a prior policy that let students aged 13 and over use approved tools after digital-citizenship instruction. Its Generative AI Ad Hoc Committee is to bring recommendations to the board by year's end. New York's ban drew the coverage; the same-day twin in Los Angeles went comparatively unremarked ([K-12 Dive, 3 September 2026](https://www.k12dive.com/news/lausd-restricts-all-students-from-using-ai-tools/829612/)).

**Parents are bringing AI to the IEP table.** EdSurge reported on 11 September that parents of children with disabilities are feeding individualised education programs into chatbots to translate jargon, draft questions and even build home goals. "They're using it to translate the jargon, any acronyms. It's helping them to draft questions," said Olivia Coleman of the University of Central Florida. It democratises a famously opaque process — and raises FERPA and disclosure questions no policy has caught up with. One special-education administrators' body offered the opposite warning — that lengthier, AI-drafted parent submissions mean "it will slow down the process." ([EdSurge, 11 September 2026](https://www.edsurge.com/news/parents-are-bringing-ai-to-the-iep-meeting-heres-how-teachers-should-respond)).

**The detectors flag real student writing — unevenly.** A University of Notre Dame paper presented at an AI-leadership summit spanning the turn of the month found AI-writing detectors flagged genuine, recent, human-written peer-reviewed abstracts at 14.9% (Pangram) and 8.9% (GPTZero), against vendor claims of under 1%. Non-STEM writing was flagged far more than STEM (theology 26.6% versus chemistry 2.1% on one detector), and light "polish" edits — a proxy for the guideline-compliant help teachers routinely permit — were flagged at 38 to 80%, with multilingual learners harmed further still. The authors call it an "integrity catch-22" ([Karr and colleagues, arXiv, September 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11256)).

## The Riff Returns But Slower

- **19 September:** Google's AI Educator Badge-a-thon. Watch whether the modules teach account boundaries and evidence of learning, or only feature fluency ([Google, 3 September 2026](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/new-ai-educator-trainings-september-2026/)).
- **30 September:** Canvas Lite opens worldwide; the old Free-for-Teacher service reopens 30 September–6 October as a migration window. The real question is whether exports carry complete records or the 500 MB ceiling strands larger courses — an exit test wearing a launch's clothes ([Instructure Community, 9 September 2026](https://community.instructure.com/en/discussion/667092/built-for-individual-educators-built-with-security-in-mind-introducing-canvas-lite)).
- **15 October:** UNESCO's deliberative-governance consultation closes to comment; policy briefs are promised for Digital Learning Week 2027 ([UNESCO, 8 September 2026](https://www.unesco.org/en/digital-education/artificial-intelligence/consultation)). The briefs, not the ministerial statement, are the artefacts to read.
- **End of 2026-27:** Los Angeles Unified's committee reports. A year-long ban that produces only a ban will have answered the liability question and none of the learning ones.
- **Slow burn:** the cognitive-offloading literature is consolidating from anecdote into large-panel evidence. Expect "does AI help?" to keep splitting into "help with what, measured when, and with the tool present or absent?"

## Sceptic's Corner

The thing being evangelised this week was not a product. It was governance itself. Inside seven days a ministerial statement was adopted in Paris, a union-and-Microsoft "national standard" acquired contractual teeth, two of the largest school systems on earth imposed moratoria, and Google shipped context-aware access controls. Read together, the week can feel like the AI-in-schools problem is finally being brought under management. It is worth being precise about what was actually delivered.

Almost all of it governs procurement and access — not learning. A ministerial statement names public-interest tests without budgets, deadlines or signatory-by-signatory duties. The AFT–Microsoft standard binds a contract, but only for products "primarily designed and marketed" for education, expressly excluding the general-purpose assistants through which most school AI now arrives ([AFT School AI Privacy Standard, September 2026](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/2026/NAfAI-School_AI_Privacy_Standards.pdf)). Context-aware access decides who may open a tool from which device, not which task deserves it or what evidence of understanding must survive ([Google Workspace Updates, 8 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/context-aware-access-controls-are-available-for-Gemini-Enterprise-in-the-Admin-console.html)). And the moratoria remove a tool from some pupils — which is not the same as building a capability. The strongest evidence we have, PISA's correlation and the ALEKS panel's proctoring result among them, does not license a ban as a *learning* intervention; it licenses one as a *risk* decision, which is a different and more modest claim ([OECD, 8 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2026/09/pisa-2025-results-volume-i_5265bfb1/full-report.html); [Rismanchian and colleagues, July 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21629)).

None of this is a reason to wave the week away. A contract you can enforce and an age line drawn on precaution both have their place. It is a reason to keep the categories straight. The question to put to anyone brandishing this week's headline as the answer is unglamorous: what does it change on Monday morning, in one classroom, for one task? A standard that governs a data flow, a statement that governs a ministry, and a ban that governs a login have each done something real. None of them has designed the assignment where a student still has to think. That remained the job this week, and it did not get any smaller.
