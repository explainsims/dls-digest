## Top story

**Wharton has opened up the design of an AI simulation.** In a [12 November preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.16986v1), Wharton Generative AI Labs describes Primer, an open-source prototype that interviews a teacher about the learning goal, prerequisite knowledge and likely misconceptions, then drafts a simulation plan for teacher review. The student experience has three parts. A mentor prepares the learner, a practice agent plays the counterpart, and a feedback agent helps the learner reflect. The paper describes roleplay and teachback, where the learner explains an idea to a simulated character. The design gives the teacher a place to specify what the learner has to work out and when the system should help.

The researchers tested the authoring workflow with K–12 teachers between February and June 2026. Teachers made a simulation and played through it as students would. The team observed transcription mistakes, drift from the learning goal and agents stepping in with help too early. That is usability and design evidence. How much students learn from the simulation, or can do independently afterwards, remains an open question. The team also says it has not reviewed the prototype for compliance with U.S. student privacy laws or district policies. It describes recorded sessions reviewed by AI agents, with privacy and costs depending on whether the models run locally or remotely. Those are decisions a school would need to own even with access to the source code.

For Ed Tech Coaches and science teachers in international schools, a narrow rehearsal from a familiar investigation is a useful first design test. The teacher could specify that the simulated classmate insists on comparing insulation tests that started at different temperatures. The learner has to spot why the comparison fails, propose a fair test, and defend that choice under a follow-up question. Have a colleague play the evasive student and see whether the system holds back the answer. The quality of the character's acting comes a distant second to whether the student still has to do the physics.

## Other stories

- **Italy's privacy regulator puts some of the work back on the ministry.** In its [12 November newsletter](https://gpdp.it/web/guest/home/docweb/-/docweb-display/docweb/10319176), the Garante disclosed a favourable opinion, issued on 20 October, on the education ministry's draft decree providing initial guidance on school AI use. A technical annex describes organisational and security measures for pseudonymising accounts and using the systems in schools. The regulator also asked the ministry to help schools choose from the market, suggesting supplier standards, model services and a dedicated support service that could help schools deal with providers. Those suggestions concern what the ministry could put in place. The opinion is on the draft decree. The most transferable part is who does the homework. A school can explain the purpose of a tool and identify the data it needs. It is a different undertaking to verify the vendor's handling of the same data across account provisioning, support and deletion. A common supplier standard would give school teams something concrete to check, and a support route would give them somewhere to go when the contract and the settings tell different stories.
- **Clever brings student-level browser activity into the buying conversation.** In its [13 November roadmap](https://www.clever.com/blog/2026/11/product-roadmap-schools), Clever distinguishes existing application login tracking from a November launch of per-student active browser tab duration and time-of-day analytics. The company puts Safari on iOS on its back-to-school 2027 timetable. The same post describes existing field-level controls over the student information system attributes shared with each app and discusses extending identity, access and visibility controls to AI tools as work in progress. The data-minimisation feature and the activity metric belong in the same procurement conversation. A school that wants to know whether it is paying for unused software might be able to answer from aggregate use. A screen showing every student's browser minutes is a different collection decision and can skew a comparison if one group spends more time on a device or browser outside coverage. Ask the vendor to explain which devices and routes are counted and who can see the individual record. That will tell a school more about the dashboard's value than another layer of usage charts.
- **A Thai Nguyen robotics hub gives AI literacy something to measure.** The [provincial government's 14 November account](https://thainguyen.gov.vn/tin-hoat-dong-cua-don-vi/ra-mat-trung-tam-nghien-cuu-va-doi-moi-sang-tao-ai-tai-ictu-307418) confirms the opening of the AI Research and Innovation Hub at Thai Nguyen University of Information and Communication Technology and its Physical AI Convention. The province says the centre will focus on Edge AI, which runs on or near the device, and Physical AI, which acts in the world, with universities, institutes and companies supporting student projects and real-world testing. The [organiser's programme](https://pai.quanrobotics.org/) scheduled a guided small-team build for students and teachers on 15 November, starting from sensors and moving through perception, decision and action. For a school robotics programme, there is a curriculum question here. A cart that works on the taped course is only the first run. Change the lighting or the surface, ask teams to predict which readings will shift, and make them explain how the cart should behave when the input is uncertain. The technology becomes something a student can test and explain, and teachers can assess how the group makes sense of a failure.
- **Pause and replay deserve attention in accessible science video.** Mamdouh Helali and Wafaa Ragab describe eight weeks of Edpuzzle-based work with 30 learners with hearing impairments, aged 14–16, at two schools in Egypt in a [14 November article in press](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-09652-0_reference.pdf). The programme used interactive video and embedded questions. The authors report better visual literacy scores after the programme. They tested the same group before and afterwards, with no comparison group and no assessment of long-term retention. The design cannot separate effects of the platform from those of adapted materials, repeated opportunities to practise or the teaching that came with the videos. A useful question for a science department is whether the student can stop at the moment a reading changes, see the scale clearly and explain what has happened before proceeding. It is worth comparing an accessible clip with and without embedded prompts using the same evidence question. The paper supplies a reason to inspect that part of the lesson. A school can do that with a clip it already uses.
- **A tutoring review asks what the comparison group actually did.** Larisa Tararina and colleagues' [13 November systematic review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1890067/full) synthesized 89 evaluations of systems that adapt instruction using performance data. Twenty-one used large language models. Seventy-three studies compared the intervention with conventional instruction, business as usual, print materials or no additional intervention. Thirteen used an alternative structured or technological intervention, and three did not give the authors enough to classify the comparator cleanly. Only 12 included a delayed measure. The review counted the direction and statistical certainty of findings without pooling effect sizes. It also excluded comparisons of two adaptive versions of the same system from the main synthesis because those comparisons address design features. That distinction matters to a department choosing between a tutor and practice it can already provide. The comparison that helps with that choice gives both groups a serious learning task, then tests what they can do later without either set of supports. An improvement over doing nothing can be helpful evidence. It answers the first and easiest question.

## On the radar

- **20 November is the deadline for a U.S. formative assessment infrastructure call.** The K–12 AI Infrastructure Program's [7 October request for proposals](https://k12-ai-infrastructure.org/harness-rfp/) closes at 11:59 p.m. Anywhere on Earth on 20 November. It offers up to US$1 million across up to two awards, with a US$600,000 cap on an individual award, for openly licensed infrastructure from U.S.-based applicants serving U.S. students. One example is connecting evidence from an AI tutoring session to an upcoming lesson so a teacher can respond to the misconceptions that surfaced. The milestone to watch is whether the resulting tools make that evidence easy for a teacher to interpret and act on.
- **An equity check can change the prediction without changing the classroom.** An [11 November preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.16100v1) looked at what happens when a learning model is forced to remove an average difference between groups. In a held-out replay of the Eedi maths data, for pupils with recorded financial-support eligibility, one version predicted a gap close to zero. Pupils eligible for support scored about 8.5 percentage points lower on the study’s rolling measure of correct answers. The Eedi analysis used recorded learning trajectories and could only test prediction. Measuring the effect of extra help would require intervention data. Watch for replications that can connect a change to the dashboard with a change to the help learners receive.

## Sceptic's corner

Adaptive practice is often sold on the idea that a system will keep each learner at the right level of difficulty. The promise sounds sensible. It also lets the system decide how long a student should work on a problem before receiving a hint. The [Primer team](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.16986v1) saw agents help too early in testing. In a lesson, that can happen in the friendliest possible exchange. The student asks whether they should compare temperatures, the character says yes and adds which readings to use, and the rest of the conversation flows. The student may leave with a neat explanation of an analysis the character effectively supplied.

A good design review makes the point where help becomes too much embarrassingly specific. Give the simulator a confident but wrong answer, then a hesitant but sound one. See which learner gets pressed for evidence and whether the character gives away the deciding observation. Keep a record of how much help each run needed. Then change the example and ask for an explanation away from the simulator, after enough time for the wording of the conversation to have faded. The two tasks tell a department whether the learner can use the idea in a new setting and what the system contributed along the way. I would ask to see both performances before paying for the claim that the system can find the right challenge.
