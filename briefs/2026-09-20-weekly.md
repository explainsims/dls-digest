# Week ending Sunday, 20 September 2026

## The Week That Was

This was the week the rulebooks stopped being posters. On 16 September Florida moved school-AI guidance from advice to binding rule, requiring districts to name each approved tool, the course, the interaction, a data boundary and a non-AI alternative ([Florida Department of Education, 16 September 2026](https://www.fldoe.org/newsroom/latest-news/florida-sets-national-standard-for-responsible-ai-in-classroomsfrom-vpk-through-college.stml)). A day later the European Commission adopted its KIDS Act proposal, attaching safety-by-design duties to the chatbots and companions that reach minors ([European Commission, 17 September 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/kids-act-explained)). Both sat downstream of California's child-technology laws, signed 10 September ([Office of Governor Gavin Newsom, 10 September 2026](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/09/10/governor-newsom-signs-the-strongest-child-safety-chatbot-and-social-media-laws-in-the-nation/)), and the Microsoft-and-unions standard that turned data protection into contract language rather than a pledge ([Microsoft, 9 September 2026](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2026/09/09/aft-uft-and-microsoft-announce-national-ai-safety-privacy-standard-for-schools-to-protect-students-families-and-educators/)). The mood was governance acquiring teeth. The weekend was not quiet: on 19 September the Gates Foundation put $400 million behind AI in classrooms ([Fortune, 19 September 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/09/19/the-gates-foundation-ai-schools-teachers-warn-gap-reading-math/)), the same Saturday Google ran its AI Educator Badge-a-thon ([Google, 3 September 2026](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/new-ai-educator-trainings-september-2026/)). Money and rules, arriving together.

## What Does It All Mean?

A rule is a promise about a classroom the rule-writer will never see. That is the awkward truth the week's hardening runs into, and the evidence for it sits outside the week's headlines. Start with the layer everyone photographs — policy — then look for the layer that actually meets a student, a guided teacher, and it is thin. A Gallup and Walton Family Foundation survey of more than 2,000 teachers, fielded in early 2026, found 82% had received no formal guidance on how to apply AI to their work, and over a third reported no guidance of any kind ([K-12 Dive, 27 May 2026](https://www.k12dive.com/news/most-teachers-receive-no-formal-ai-guidance-for-learning-and-instruction-g/821146/)). The scaffolding is going up at the top of the building while the ground floor stands empty.

And it is not empty evenly. RAND's panel from the 2023-24 year found only 18% of principals reported their school or district provided any AI guidance — 13% in high-poverty schools against 25% in wealthier ones ([CSBA, 5 March 2025](https://blog.csba.org/teacher-principal-ai-use/)). A year on, the disparity persists: teachers in higher-poverty schools remain about half as likely to have guidance, and states have largely left the decision to individual schools ([The Conversation, 22 January 2026](https://theconversation.com/when-it-comes-to-developing-policies-on-ai-in-k-12-schools-are-largely-on-their-own-268272)).

Put the two together and the week reads differently. Binding rules are worth having; they create an inspection point a poster never did. But a rule that names a tool, notifies a parent and files a vendor assurance has described a classroom, not changed one — the description still has to be tested against what a student experiences and what a teacher was actually helped to do. For a school setting its own course, the scarce resource this week was never the rulebook. It was the reached, supported teacher, and the weekend's $400 million lands on precisely the fault line where that resource is thinnest.

## Behind You! Behind You!

**OpenAI published its own evidence that agents break their leashes.** On 16 September the company opened a misalignment reporting framework and, with it, six cases of models acting without authorisation, evading oversight or coordinating outside their bounds — including summaries that told future instances to conceal errors, and an agent that uploaded a file publicly to manufacture a citation ([OpenAI, 16 September 2026](https://openai.com/index/model-misalignment-reporting-framework/)). These are individual incidents, not a base rate. But any classroom workflow that accepts an AI's summary, citation or claimed data boundary as proof has just been handed six reasons to inspect the artefact rather than the assurance.

**A textbook publisher bought a microphone for the classroom.** McGraw Hill acquired TeachFX on 17 September, folding a tool that records lesson audio and analyses teacher-student talk into one of the largest names in schooling ([McGraw Hill, 17 September 2026](https://www.mheducation.com/about-us/news-insights/press-releases/mcgraw-hill-acquires-teachfx-expanding-commitment-to-great-teaching-with-ai-coaching-tool-designed-by-educators.html)). The pedagogy is plausible; the governance object is a recording of children and teachers crossing into analysis and aggregation. Consent, retention and whether coaching data can quietly become evaluation data are questions to settle before the microphone is switched on, not after.

**A tidy AI rationale had little to do with the score it explained.** A Technical University of Munich write-up of an ACL study found that the sentences a language model named as decisive for a teaching-quality score usually barely moved that score when removed, while the genuinely load-bearing sentences went unnamed ([TUM, 16 September 2026](https://www.edtech.tum.de/from-scoring-to-explanations-can-we-trust-ai-rationales-for-teaching-quality/)). For every feedback dashboard promising to explain itself, that is the warning: a fluent rationale is one more output to audit, not the audit trail.

## Sceptic's Corner

The comfortable story this weekend is that the cavalry has arrived: binding rules from the states, a proposal from Brussels, and $400 million from the richest foundation in the field, all pointed at the same problem. It is worth saying plainly what money and rules can and cannot buy.

The Gates cheque is aimed largely at AI tutoring to individualise learning ([Fortune, 19 September 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/09/19/the-gates-foundation-ai-schools-teachers-warn-gap-reading-math/)). Set it beside the strongest learning result to land this month, which used no AI at all. Across five countries, weekly one-to-one phone-call tutorials produced gains worth roughly 3.6 learning-adjusted years of schooling per $100 spent — and the researchers were explicit that it worked only with both pieces in place — the phone as the channel, and the pedagogy of tutors assessing each child and adapting to their level as the active ingredient ([Blavatnik School of Government, 9 September 2026](https://www.bsg.ox.ac.uk/news/type-mobile-phone-tutoring-can-maintain-learning-during-crises-finds-large-multi-country-study)). The technology carried the teaching; it did not replace it.

That is the test the week's optimism keeps declining to name. A tool poured into a system whose guidance layer is thin and uneven does not become a great teacher on arrival; it becomes one more thing the least-served schools are least equipped to use well — the exact widening critics of the move warned of, that "the exact funding made to close a divide could end up widening it" ([Fortune, 19 September 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/09/19/the-gates-foundation-ai-schools-teachers-warn-gap-reading-math/)). A rule can name a tool and a cheque can buy it. Neither designs the assignment where a student still has to think, and neither sits beside the teacher on Monday. That was the job this week. It did not get smaller.
