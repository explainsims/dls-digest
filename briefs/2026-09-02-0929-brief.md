# Wednesday, 2 September 2026

## Top story

**South Korea is staffing the human layer of AI education, not merely buying the software.** On 1 September, its Ministry of Education opened recruitment for 1,700 digital tutors across six regional centres: 1,200 new trainees and 500 current or former tutors returning for retraining. The role is deliberately practical. Tutors support teachers during AI and digital lessons, manage classroom devices and software, and help close gaps between students who arrive with very different levels of digital fluency. Training is free, runs in October and November, and now combines remote core and elective work with in-person, region-specific practice ([Korean Ministry of Education release republished by KDI, 1 September 2026](https://eiec.kdi.re.kr/policy/materialView.do?code=J0700&depth1=J0000&depth2=&device=mob&num=286098&pg=&pp=&search_txt=&topic=&type=J)).

That follows a second, complementary move. On 27 August the ministry said frontline teachers would lead the writing of textbooks and supporting materials for 16 AI subjects — six elementary, two middle-school and eight high-school courses — with the science-and-technology ministry supplying technical reviewers and ten provincial education offices coordinating the work. The books are due for classroom pilots in the first semester of 2027, provincial approval later that year and national introduction in March 2028 ([Korean Ministry of Education release republished by KDI, 27 August 2026](https://eiec.kdi.re.kr/policy/materialView.do?depth1=J0000&depth2=J0700&device=pc&num=285876&pg=&pp=&search_txt=&topic=&type=J)).

The useful pattern for international schools is the pairing: teachers shape the curriculum; another trained adult absorbs some of the operational friction. Most school AI plans fund licences and then quietly charge implementation to teacher goodwill. Korea has at least named the missing job and linked training records to a portal schools can consult when recruiting. It has also separated durable concepts for the textbooks from fast-changing technical examples for supplementary materials, reducing the risk that a printed AI curriculum is obsolete on arrival. That distinction gives technical updates a route that does not require rewriting the whole course. The test will be whether tutors become enduring instructional capacity or temporary device troubleshooters, but the architecture is sounder than another portal and a webinar.

## Other stories

- **OpenAI turned district adoption into a multi-state procurement template.** On 26 August it added 55 school systems across 20 US states to ChatGPT for Teachers, reaching more than 100,000 additional staff. OpenAI now says it works with more than 100 K–12 organisations across 30 states and provides access and training to over 300,000 educators. More consequential than the scale claim is a Student Data Privacy Consortium agreement covering 16 states: general offers are signed in 15, with California under a separate agreement. The managed workspace has role-based controls, does not use workspace data to train models by default and remains free for verified US K–12 educators through June 2028 ([OpenAI, 26 August 2026](https://openai.com/index/bringing-chatgpt-for-teachers-to-more-us-school-districts/)). This is US-specific and vendor-authored, but it shows where procurement is heading: common privacy terms, managed identity and training bundled with access. For international schools, that package is the informative comparator for an education AI offer, not a consumer account with a reassuring FAQ.

- **Gemini Notebook now meters the work, not just the number of prompts.** Google announced on 28 August that consumer limits will refresh every five hours rather than daily, while the allowance itself will depend on prompt complexity, chat length, number of sources and output type. When a notebook lacks enough compute for a Video Overview or Slide Deck, the user can defer the job and receive a notification when it finishes; the product will also suggest cheaper alternatives ([Google, 28 August 2026](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/gemini-notebook/new-flexible-usage-limits/)). The consumer rollout starts today on web and mobile; Education-domain availability is not specified. Still, the mechanism matters: two apparently similar educator workflows can consume different amounts of an invisible budget depending on source volume and output format. That turns repeatability into a design concern. A resilient school-authored routine includes a low-compute fallback when a lesson depends on a generated artefact being ready at a fixed time.

- **England made generative-AI review part of the filtering-and-monitoring job.** Updated Department for Education standards now tell schools to map where web and in-app generative AI is used, assess whether controls can handle real-time, dynamic and personalised output, and revisit the review whenever a new AI tool or major software update arrives. Senior leaders are expected to document what is blocked or allowed and why; the designated safeguarding lead and IT support share the operational work. The standard rejects one blanket profile for everyone, requires at least separate student and staff access, covers school-managed and bring-your-own devices, and calls for weekly monitoring reports plus immediate reporting of high-risk incidents ([UK Department for Education, updated 25 August 2026](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/meeting-digital-and-technology-standards-in-schools-and-colleges/filtering-and-monitoring-core-standard)). The jurisdiction is English, but the checklist travels. An AI product review that stops at privacy terms misses what the product can generate, where monitoring fails inside apps and who reads the alerts.

- **Europe’s new teacher course treats AI literacy as something schools must assess.** The European School Education Platform updated its self-paced course on 26 August. Across an estimated 15–20 hours, teachers compare literacy frameworks, map AI competences into subjects and curriculum, examine ethical, social and emotional effects, and design ways to assess what students have achieved. It covers primary through upper secondary and awards a digital certificate ([European School Education Platform, 26 August 2026](https://school-education.ec.europa.eu/en/learn/courses/developing-students-ai-literacy)). That assessment emphasis is more useful than another tool tour: if AI literacy is a learning intention, it needs observable evidence. The compromise is in the delivery. This version preserves the materials from a facilitated course but removes deadlines, live meetings, moderated discussion and peer review, leaving participants to assess their own work. It is therefore a credible content spine for professional learning, not evidence that self-paced completion alone changes classroom practice.

- **Personalised AI feedback lost to ordinary feedback in a clinical-reasoning trial.** In the September issue of the *Journal of Surgical Education*, University of Bonn researchers report a randomised crossover study in which fourth-year medical students received either AI-generated personalised or general feedback on daily key-feature questions aligned to lecture topics. Exit-exam scores did not differ between feedback conditions (p = 0.454), and simply using the app was not significantly associated with a higher score than non-use (p = 0.067). More intensive use was associated with stronger gains over time, including a group effect at p = 0.003 and a time-by-use interaction below p = 0.001 ([PubMed record, September 2026 issue; first published online 7 July](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42413233/)). Medical students are not school pupils, and intensity was exploratory rather than randomly assigned. Even so, the result punctures an easy product claim: the active ingredient may be repeated retrieval practice, not AI personalisation.

## On the radar

- **UNESCO’s ministerial statement lands next week.** Digital Learning Week runs in Paris from 8–11 September with more than 25 education ministers, over 1,000 in-person participants and a joint statement on sustaining education as a common good in the age of AI. UNESCO also plans policy briefs on safety, sovereignty, procurement, cost models and age-appropriateness ([UNESCO, updated 26 August 2026](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/education-age-ai-ministerial-statement-be-adopted-unescos-digital-learning-week?hub=80094)). The programme puts teacher agency, human learning and deliberative safety governance on separate plenary stages. The procurement and cost-model papers will reveal whether that framing produces operational tests; declarations about human-centred AI are already plentiful.

- **Two role-specific OpenAI Academy courses are scheduled for 9 September.** The company’s 1 September education newsletter says new courses for K–12 and higher-education audiences will launch next Wednesday, alongside its back-to-school promotion of interactive quizzes and teacher workspaces ([OpenAI Education newsletter, 1 September 2026](https://edunewsletter.openai.com/p/the-edu-prompt-issue-5)). Course content and assessment detail are still thin in the announcement. The revealing details will be whether completion requires a worked classroom artefact, whether privacy choices are assessed and whether examples distinguish teacher data from student data. Without those, role-specific may mean little more than role-themed prompts.

- **Gemini Notebook’s new compute model begins with consumers today.** Google says the flexible limits start rolling out on web and mobile on 2 September, but the announcement does not name Workspace for Education accounts ([Google, 28 August 2026](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/gemini-notebook/new-flexible-usage-limits/)). The five-hour refresh sounds more generous than a daily ceiling, while variable charging makes capacity harder to predict. Education admin documentation will need to settle whether limits are pooled or individual, whether administrators can see exhaustion patterns, and what happens to a queued output when account access changes.

## Sceptic's corner

*Teacher-led* is becoming the safest phrase in AI education — and one of the least specific. Korea says teachers will write the curriculum. OpenAI says teachers should lead adoption. UNESCO says AI should preserve professional judgement. All three positions are preferable to treating teachers as end users who receive a login and a launch video. None, by itself, establishes control.

Control sits in less photogenic places: who chooses the model; who can change a default; who approves a connector; who sees usage and incident logs; who owns materials after a teacher leaves; and who can stop a system when the evidence is weak. Korea’s design gets closer because it assigns teachers authorship and funds a separate implementation role. Even there, technical currency comes from ministry-nominated reviewers, approval remains provincial and the durability of the tutor workforce is unproven. OpenAI’s district package offers stronger managed controls, but the platform, training and evidence claims still come from the same supplier ([Korean Ministry of Education via KDI, 27 August](https://eiec.kdi.re.kr/policy/materialView.do?depth1=J0000&depth2=J0700&device=pc&num=285876&pg=&pp=&search_txt=&topic=&type=J); [OpenAI, 26 August](https://openai.com/index/bringing-chatgpt-for-teachers-to-more-us-school-districts/); [UNESCO, 26 August](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/education-age-ai-ministerial-statement-be-adopted-unescos-digital-learning-week?hub=80094)).

The harder test is not whether teachers are consulted. It is whether they hold a decision right after the pilot starts: the right to narrow, pause or reject a use when classroom evidence, workload or student safety points the other way. Without that, *teacher-led* describes the launch team, not the governance.
