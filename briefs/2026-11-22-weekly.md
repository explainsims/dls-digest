## What Does It All Mean?

On Thursday South Korea ran what may be the most closely guarded exam in the world. For the College Scholastic Ability Test, 497,526 candidates sat at 1,312 sites with about 83,400 invigilators. Glasses were inspected, Wi-Fi within 100 metres was switched off, and the government sent three national emergency alerts during the day ([Kyunghyang Shinmun, 19 November](https://www.khan.co.kr/article/202611191936001/)). Ninety-eight candidates were caught: 49 through the alerts, 44 for carrying a device, five for using one, including smart glasses and a small camera with wireless earphones.

The week's most notorious case was not caught in an exam room at all. On 15 November, the top-ranked candidate in this year's tax accountant exam admitted to cheating with electronic devices and generative AI ([News1 via Financial News, 17 November](https://www.fnnews.com/news/202611170601573066)). Speculation about his near-perfect results came first; the confession followed ([Al Jazeera, 17 November](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/17/south-korea-cheating-scandal-are-ai-smart-glasses-a-threat-to-exams)).

So the Sunday question: if guarding the room has limits, what should a school change instead?

Read the Korean numbers coldly. Most of what was caught was a device that went off or turned up. Detection counts the careless; whatever the careful managed is, by definition, missing from the count. England's chief regulator has reached the same place. In an 18 November letter, Sir Ian Bauckham said Ofqual would soon propose changes to coursework in A level history and the three English A levels, and told exam boards he expects exam-centre staff, invigilators included, to be trained to spot wearable misuse in time for the summer 2027 series. "But we cannot rely on invigilators alone," he wrote ([Ofqual, 18 November](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/letter-to-awarding-organisations-about-misuse-of-ai-and-wearable-technology/letter-to-awarding-organisations-about-artificial-intelligence-in-non-exam-assessments-and-misuse-of-wearable-technology)).

Research has words for this. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu separate "discursive" changes, which tell students what is allowed, from "structural" ones, which change the task itself so it works whether or not students follow the instructions ([Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2025.2503964)). Traffic-light rules for AI use are their example of the first kind. They borrow the look of a system with real enforcement and leave the enforcement behind. Korea chose the opposite extreme: maximum structure, applied to a single day.

One AP course already shows a middle path. In AP Computer Science Principles, students build a program of their own, and the supervised May exam asks written questions about that code ([College Board](https://apstudents.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-computer-science-principles/assessment)). The project can be made anywhere; the explanation happens where nobody can prompt it.

Korea is edging the same way. Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin told the National Assembly the case was a chance to rethink the purpose and format of exams, and essay-type questions are under discussion, undecided ([News1 via Financial News, 17 November](https://www.fnnews.com/news/202611170601573066)).

For an international school, an AI-use scale is a discursive instrument, and a useful one: it tells students what each task allows. It cannot make that true. Its structural partner can be cheap. For any task set at the low-AI end of the scale, name the moment, in class and in front of a teacher, where the student explains, defends or extends what they handed in. The College Board does it once a year. A department can do it every unit.

## Behind You! Behind You!

**Three of America's four largest districts are pushing back on Google.** New York City has switched off Gemini and AI Mode on student devices and is talking to Google; Los Angeles has asked for a way to turn the AI summaries off; Chicago says it will look at other search tools if it cannot suppress them ([Fortune, 21 November](https://fortune.com/2026/11/21/google-search-ai-overviews-schools-chicago-nyc-los-angeles-miami/)). New Jersey's governor's office called the policy unfair to schools and students ([NJ.com via Yahoo, 18 November](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/google-just-told-schools-ai-112820359.html)). Miami-Dade, which has offered Gemini to about 100,000 high schoolers, did not answer questions on how it will adapt. Pushback so far, not departure.

**Gemini skills can now be shared, and sharing starts switched on.** In a gradual rollout from 12 November, on the web app only for now, Gemini users can share skills with View or Edit access and attach files from Drive, Gemini Notebook and Google Photos ([Google, 16 November](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/11/share-skills-add-files-from-Drive-Notebook-Photos-to-skills-Gemini-app.html)). Sharing is on by default and admins can turn it off. Skills follow the domain's Drive sharing settings, so external sharing applies to them too, and there is no admin control over attaching files. A well-built rubric skill is worth passing round; check the external setting first.

**Gemini can now label sensitive Drive files, for schools on the right licence.** On 17 November Google made Gemini-powered classification in Drive generally available. Admins describe a label in plain language, test it, then let Gemini apply it across Drive so labels can drive data-loss and retention rules; it will not overwrite labels people have set ([Google, 17 November](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/11/gemini-powered-data-classification-in-Google-Drive-is-now-generally-available.html)). In education it needs Google AI Pro for Education. For a school that must show where student records sit, losing the manual training step matters. A rule written in a sentence still needs testing on files whose contents you already know.

## Sceptic's Corner

The centrepiece's remedy, change the task rather than guard the room, has a strong case against it. Korea is making it in public.

Redesign does not remove advantage; it moves it. When the National Education Commission's essay-type plans surfaced in August, one parent told Yonhap the family would simply add another academy, and a cram-school official expected demand for test technique to grow in the early years ([Yonhap via Daum, 16 August](https://v.daum.net/v/133sKN1yY4)). Grading fairness was the largest single concern in the commission's public comments: 754 of 3,246 ([News1 via Financial News, 17 November](https://www.fnnews.com/news/202611170601573066)). The ministry's plan for marking essays at scale is an AI that provisionally scores answers, trained on 1.1 million of them, in every regional education office by 2029 ([Money Today, 5 August](https://www.mt.co.kr/policy/2026/08/05/2026080416122870996)). The machine leaves the exam hall and returns as the marker.

Conversation-based checks have costs of their own. They reward the fluent and the confident, and a timed in-class write is harder on slow writers and anxious students.

Nor has the motive changed much. Stanford's Challenge Success surveys put the share of high schoolers reporting at least one cheating behaviour in the previous month at 60–70% for years, and the 2023 surveys, which added questions on tools like ChatGPT, found it about the same or slightly lower ([Stanford GSE, 31 October 2023](https://ed.stanford.edu/news/what-do-ai-chatbots-really-mean-students-and-cheating)). The tool changes; the stakes drive it.

The line for the staffroom: redesigning the task is right, but it is neither free nor neutral. Before calling the new version fairer, check whom it favours.
