# Wednesday, 27 May 2026

## Top story

**AI literacy is moving out of the slogan phase. The useful question now is who turns it into routines.**

No new education bombshell landed overnight. The signal in today's sweep is more prosaic and more useful: AI literacy work is hardening into frameworks, roadmaps, and teacher-facing implementation patterns. Google's recent AI Policy & Guidance Labs took school and higher-ed leaders in Brazil, India, Malaysia, Mexico, Spain, and Sweden through formal position statements and 12-month implementation roadmaps, explicitly product-agnostic rather than Gemini sales theatre. ([Google for Education](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-policy-guidance-labs/)) The AILit Framework, the European Commission / OECD primary-secondary effort feeding PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy, is still in finalisation after nearly 1,000 consultation responses and promises classroom exemplars with the final 2026 release. ([AILit Framework](https://ailiteracyframework.org/))

That matters for international schools because next-year planning should not start with "AI literacy" as a banner. It should start with the operational translation: which competencies, which year levels, which subject examples, which staff routines, which account boundaries, which review habits. Education Week's current AI cluster points the same way from the field: districts are launching summer AI PD, training quality is under scrutiny, student well-being is now part of the AI conversation, and local "vibe-coded" tools are becoming real enough to save money and create governance risk at the same time. ([Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/technology/artificial-intelligence))

For Ed Tech Coaches, the useful move is to build an AI-literacy starter map that is boring on purpose: one page of competencies, one page of classroom routines, one page of staff guardrails, and a small exemplar bank. Let frameworks supply language, but make teachers' first experience concrete. Otherwise "AI literacy" becomes another laminated abstraction, and schools have a shed full of those already.

## Other stories

- **OpenAI's Edu release notes now read like an admin-governance changelog, not a chatbot changelog.** The 21 May update adds Codex goal mode, Appshots for ChatGPT Edu accounts, browser annotations, locked remote computer use with an admin-disable setting, Codex analytics in the global admin console, and plugin sharing enabled by default for Edu. ([OpenAI Help Center](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/10128477-chatgpt-enterprise-edu-release-notes)) The useful school read is that coding agents are becoming managed workspace infrastructure: visibility, policies, sharing defaults, and audit trails matter before anyone gets excited about app generation.

- **Google AI Studio is now explicitly crossing from prototype toy to deployable agent surface.** Google's I/O developer highlights say Managed Agents can run tools and code inside isolated Linux environments, AI Studio agents can call relevant Workspace APIs, projects can export to Antigravity, and native Android support can publish to a Google Play test track. ([Google Developers Blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/google-io-2026-developer-highlights/)) That is useful for school prototyping, but it also raises the line: a teacher-built agent that can touch Workspace APIs is no longer just a clever demo.

- **Google is pitching Gemini for Education at higher-ed scale, and the account-boundary details are the interesting part.** Google says more than 1,000 U.S. higher-ed institutions now use Gemini for Education, reaching more than 10 million students, and it highlights school-logo and "EDU" labelling in the Gemini app so users can tell when they are working in a school-issued account. ([Google for Education](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/gemini-higher-education-benefits/)) The scale number is marketing; the account-signalling is governance. Students and staff need to know which identity is carrying which protections.

- **Google's provenance push is worth bringing into student media literacy, not just IT security.** Google says SynthID has watermarked more than 100 billion images and videos and 60,000 years of audio, that C2PA Content Credentials are being expanded, and that SynthID verification is moving from the Gemini app into Search and Chrome. ([Google Trust & Safety](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/identifying-ai-generated-media-online/)) This is a decent classroom hinge: verification tools help, but provenance literacy still has to include screenshots, re-uploads, crops, platform loss, and the old problem of people believing what they already wanted to believe.

- **Microsoft's Agent 365 framing is a useful outside-Google mirror.** Microsoft says its Agent 365 control plane gives IT and security leaders a place to observe, govern, manage, and secure organizational agents; it also says tens of millions of agents appeared in the registry during preview and that Microsoft itself has visibility into more than 500,000 internal agents. ([Microsoft](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/03/09/introducing-the-first-frontier-suite-built-on-intelligence-trust/)) Not a Google-school stack story, but it confirms the market direction: agent sprawl is now a management category.

## Sceptic's corner

The phrase to squeeze today is **"AI literacy."**

It is becoming the blessed phrase in the room, which means it is also becoming dangerously comfortable. Everyone can agree that students need AI literacy. Nobody has to admit, in that sentence, whether they mean prompt tricks, model mechanics, academic integrity, media verification, data privacy, bias, workplace readiness, epistemic humility, or simply "please stop pasting homework into ChatGPT at midnight."

Frameworks help because they give schools a shared vocabulary. They also let schools feel finished too early. A policy document can say "students evaluate AI outputs critically" and sound adult. The classroom version is messier: a Grade 9 student needs to compare two plausible wrong answers; a Grade 12 student needs to decide when AI help has crossed the line into outsourcing; a teacher needs to model source-bound use without making the tool look like magic; a department needs to decide which tasks are now measuring thinking and which are measuring AI supervision.

The test is not whether the school can name AI literacy. The test is whether a teacher can point to next Tuesday's lesson and say: here is where students practise it, here is what I will look for, here is what the tool is allowed to do, and here is what still belongs to the learner.

If the phrase cannot survive contact with a lesson plan, it is not literacy yet. It is branding with a nicer lanyard.
