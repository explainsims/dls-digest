# Monday, 1 June 2026

## Top story

**The school AI problem has moved from access to guidance.** The most useful signal this week is not another product launch. It is the gap between everyday teacher AI use and formal school guidance. Gallup and the Walton Family Foundation report that only 18% of U.S. K-12 teachers say they have formal administrative guidance on using AI at work; across common teaching tasks, many receive either no guidance or only informal norms ([Gallup, 27 May](https://news.gallup.com/poll/710534/teachers-receive-no-formal-guidance.aspx)).

For Ed Tech Coaches and international-school leaders, the practical lesson is clear: AI support cannot stop at tool access or prompt-sharing. Schools need concise, teacher-usable guidance across planning, feedback, grading, tutoring, assessment design, disclosure, and student data.

## Other stories

- **AFT pushes back on screens and student-facing AI.** The American Federation of Teachers has called for strong limits on early-childhood screen use, an end to student-facing AI in elementary schools, tighter privacy expectations, independent research, and a "tech tax" on Big Tech ([AFT](https://www.aft.org/press-release/devices-down-eyes-hands-weingarten-calls-screen-bans-ai-limits-active-learning-major)). Even outside the U.S., this marks a broader turn: AI-in-schools arguments are becoming developmental, political, and labour-facing, not only technical.
- **Google's AI Educator Series is live.** Google says the first 20+ sessions in its AI Educator Series are now available, created with ISTE+ASCD and designed as short, modular teacher training ([Google](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-educator-series/)). The model is worth studying even where the U.S. access assumptions do not apply.
- **Microsoft's Study and Learn agent reframes student AI as managed infrastructure.** Microsoft's Study and Learn agent is now generally available inside Microsoft 365 Copilot for education customers, with IT-admin controls and a learning-science frame around guided questioning, productive struggle, and active practice ([Microsoft Education](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/education/blog/2026/05/study-and-learn-ai-built-for-your-student/)). This is the direction of travel: student AI packaged inside managed school accounts.
- **NCTE works on subject-specific AI guidance.** The National Council of Teachers of English has released a working framework for responsible AI use in English language arts and is collecting educator feedback through 2026. The framework centres student voice, critical AI literacy, and academic integrity ([NCTE](https://ncte.org/blog/2026/05/working-ela-ai-framework/)). Subject-specific guidance is likely to be more useful than generic AI policy.
- **OECD keeps the learning question in view.** OECD's Digital Education Outlook 2026 argues that generative AI can support learning when guided by clear teaching principles, but unsupported use may improve short-term performance without real learning gains ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html)). That is a useful guardrail for any school AI rollout.

## Sceptic's corner

A training course is not a school position. A managed chatbot is not a pedagogy. A framework is not classroom uptake. The useful next step for schools is to turn the emerging evidence into local guidance teachers can actually act on.
