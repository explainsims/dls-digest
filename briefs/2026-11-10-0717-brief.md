## Top story

**Smart glasses have reached the exam room.** South Korea's education ministry [reported on 9 November](https://www.moe.go.kr/boardCnts/viewRenew.do?boardID=294&boardSeq=107600&lev=0&m=020402&opType=N&page=1&s=moe&searchType=null&statusYN=W) that a nationwide survey of middle and high schools had confirmed four cases of cheating involving AI-enabled glasses in internal assessments this academic year. One was in middle school and three were in high school. Two had already been reported in the press. All four students received zero for the affected subject exam and the three high schools imposed separate disciplinary measures. The ministry plans to circulate prevention guidance and student materials in the third week of November, before second-semester finals.

A separate [9 November notice for the 19 November national university entrance exam](https://m.korea.kr/news/policyNewsView.do?newsId=148975290&pWiseMinistry=ministryNews&repCodeType=%EC%A0%95%EB%B6%80%EB%B6%80%EC%B2%98&repCode_P=A00002) names smart glasses alongside phones, smartwatches and Bluetooth earphones. Wearing AI glasses during the exam carries disqualification extending to the next year's sitting. The same notice allows devices needed for medical or physical reasons, such as hearing aids and continuous glucose monitors, after an invigilator checks them before each session. The survey and the notice answer different questions. One counts the incidents schools could confirm. The other tells candidates and invigilators what to do before a high-stakes sitting.

For a secondary school, the immediate question is whether the instructions at the door match the decision a proctor is expected to make. Four confirmed cases cannot tell us how common the practice is. They are enough to test whether a rule written around phones reaches a device worn on the face. A useful instruction covers devices that can record, receive or display assistance, gives students a confidential route to arrange an approved accommodation, and says who handles an uncertain case. A student should be able to find out in advance what they can wear, and the proctor should have someone to call without pausing the exam for the entire room.

## Other stories

- **A study will test what AI can infer from a teacher's account of student thinking.** In [9 November reporting by EdSurge](https://www.edsurge.com/news/can-ai-tell-how-well-teachers-notice-researchers-are-testing-it), Rowan University's Molly Fisher and University of Kentucky colleagues describe plans to recruit about 100 practising and 50 prospective elementary maths teachers. Expert raters will score how teachers attend to, interpret and respond to students' thinking. Claude, ChatGPT and a third tool still to be selected will score the same de-identified written responses. Teachers will watch classroom video for two of the three assessments. No student footage goes to the AI tools. Teacher data collection is planned for spring 2027. For secondary instructional coaching, the privacy choice has a side benefit. A colleague can discuss a short, de-identified account of a wrong answer without posting footage of the child who gave it. Ask the teacher what the student did, what else might explain it, and what question would distinguish the two explanations. The study is still at the design stage, and that conversation is already available.

- **The Philippines began handing out teacher laptops, with support in the purchase.** The [Philippine News Agency reported on 9 November](https://www.pna.gov.ph/article/marcos-leads-nationwide-laptop-distribution-to-public-school-teachers) that the first handover in Dagupan City delivered 209 MacBook Neo units to 10 public schools. The national allocation for teachers is 231,725 units, comprising 169,008 MacBook Neos and 62,717 Windows laptops. A separate 3,646 MacBook Neos are for administrative staff. The package includes productivity software, delivery, training and a five-year warranty. Suppliers have until March 2027 to complete deliveries, and the department says it will prioritise schools and teachers who have yet to receive government-issued laptops, particularly those leading ed-tech work. For school technology teams, the informative line item is support. A warranty can cover a repair, while a usable rollout still needs an answer for the teacher who has a lesson in ten minutes and a machine that will not start. Spare devices, repair turnaround and the staff time needed to learn the new workflows belong beside the quoted unit price when a school compares bids.

- **Google put a one-click confirmation on automatically labelled files.** Its [9 November Workspace update](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/11/) adds separate *Accept* and *Edit* buttons to the banner in Drive, Docs, Sheets and Slides when one classification label was applied automatically and the user has permission to change it. When several labels are applied, the banner still sends people to review them. The rollout began on 9 November, with up to 15 days for visibility. There is no new admin control for the confirmation feature. Education Standard and Plus are listed for default classification, while Google's AI classification has separate eligibility, including the Google AI Pro for Education add-on. The school test is a borderline file with familiar-looking contents. Give a reviewer a synthetic timetable, then the same timetable with a column of invented student support notes. If the reviewer accepts the suggested label on both because the banner looks authoritative, a faster click has sped up the wrong part of the workflow. Measure whether staff can recognise when to edit before celebrating how often they accept.

- **Google Vids can now make a longer synthetic shot inside the editor.** In its [5 November Workspace notice](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/11/gemini-omni-12-flash-now-in-vids-with-15second-generations-and-improved-quality.html?hl=eng), Google says Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash offers generated clips from three to 15 seconds, better consistency across reference images and more realistic voices. Its managed education availability list includes Education Plus and the Teaching and Learning and Google AI Pro for Education add-ons. Google lists no admin control specific to this feature. A media assignment can use the extra seconds to make a process visible, but the smoothness of the shot creates its own assessment problem. A generated clip of an experiment can show a plausible apparatus and an entirely invented result in one convincing take. In a student research course, a pupil who uses a generated shot to illustrate a proposed setup could put the source of the design in the caption, mark the scene as synthetic and show any real observation separately. Those three details tell a viewer more than a general AI disclosure at the end.

- **Microsoft put a date on where Claude in Copilot will process data.** In its [5 November announcement](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/blog/2026/11/05/bringing-claude-models-in-microsoft-copilot-into-the-eu-data-boundary/), Microsoft says eligible organisations in the EU and European Free Trade Association will get processing within its EU Data Boundary for Claude models across Copilot experiences, including Copilot Cowork, by the end of 2026. Existing users of the integration will move automatically once it is available. The option is off by default in the EU and EFTA, and administrators can enable it by user or group. Microsoft already says customer data in the integration is excluded from foundation-model training. The new promise concerns where processing occurs. For schools comparing assistants, ask suppliers to fill in the same small table for each model inside the product. Record where data is stored, where inference happens and whether a different organisation receives or retains any of it. The school signs a product agreement, but staff make decisions using the menu they actually see.

- **Science education researchers have put judgment on the agenda.** The [NARST programme](https://narst.org/virtual-events-list) lists a 12 November AI4STEM Education Center panel with Kent Crippen, Xiaoming Zhai and Okhee Lee. A worked classroom example showing that division of labour would travel further than another list of approved tools.

## Sceptic's corner

If the model agrees with the expert, can it coach the teacher? The [EdSurge account of the planned study](https://www.edsurge.com/news/can-ai-tell-how-well-teachers-notice-researchers-are-testing-it) points to a sensible first test. Give expert raters and the model the same written responses, then see where their scores line up. Faster feedback could be valuable if a teacher gets to use it while there is still time to change the next lesson. A model could also pick up recurring omissions across more practice examples than a coach can read.

There is a second question hiding in the score. Suppose a pupil writes that two fractions with different denominators cannot be equal. One teacher describes the misconception neatly. Another gives a messier account but proposes a number-line task that would distinguish confusion about the notation from confusion about the quantity. The two teachers may earn similar marks overall while needing very different feedback. A fluent summary can make either score sound convincing. The useful test is whether the feedback points back to a specific piece of evidence and gives the teacher a better question to ask.

That is a fair standard for a coaching trial. A personnel decision would require evidence about how consistently the system handles different writing styles, subjects and classroom contexts, and a way for a teacher to contest a reading. A rubric can bring rigour to a practice conversation. Putting its output into a staff file raises the evidentiary bar considerably.
