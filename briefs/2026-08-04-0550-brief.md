# Tuesday, 4 August 2026

## Top story

**Mexico's largest university threw out its first-ever online entrance exam and is ordering roughly 58,000 applicants back into a hall to sit it again in person.** The National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM) moved its undergraduate admissions exam online this year for the first time — some 150,000 candidates across three weekends in May and June — a change it framed as widening access for applicants outside Mexico City. The results broke the pattern so sharply they became their own evidence: 16.3% of candidates scored 100 or more points, against a 3.5% average between 2021 and 2025 ([Times Higher Education, 3 August 2026](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/mexican-university-suspends-admissions-after-cheating-scandal)). UNAM annulled about 3,000 exams — roughly 2% — for suspected cheating, filed a formal complaint, suspended new undergraduate enrolment on 25 July, and has now ordered a fresh, invigilated in-person exam for everyone already told they were admitted, plus rejected applicants whose scores matched or beat the lowest passing mark of the past five years. The semester starts 10 August; as of 3 August no date or venue for the re-test had been set.

Rector Leonardo Lomelí Vanegas pitched the stakes above the institution: "This doesn't just affect the university; it affects society as a whole. We cannot normalise these kinds of situations." He apologised to the students being recalled through no fault of their own — "I apologise to the applicants who, having been accepted fairly, will be called to take the entrance exam, but it is necessary to ensure fairness and equity in access."

The reason this matters to international schools isn't that anything here runs near that scale; it's that this is the largest concrete instance yet of a tension the field has mostly argued about in the abstract. The convenience of assessing through a screen and the integrity of the result are now in open conflict, and when the stakes were high enough, a serious institution chose integrity and ate the cost — an in-person re-run for 58,000 people, mid-admissions-cycle. "Return to in-person exams" stopped being a think-piece and became a logistics operation. It doesn't say every screen-based task is compromised — low-stakes formative work sits on a different risk calculus entirely — but it says any high-stakes result certified through a remote channel now carries a demonstrated failure mode whose fallback is expensive and disruptive. The usable lesson isn't "ban online" or "trust the detectors"; it's to decide, before the stakes climb, which assessments can survive being run at a distance and which have to be watched — because retrofitting that decision after 150,000 people have already sat looks exactly like this.

## Other stories

- **Big Tech's AI bill hit the balance sheet, and for the first time investors flinched.** In nine days of earnings — Alphabet on 22 July, Microsoft and Meta on 29 July, Amazon and Apple on 30 July — the four heaviest spenders guided 2026 capital expenditure toward roughly $725–760 billion, up from about $413 billion in 2025: Microsoft near $175B, Alphabet $195–205B, Meta $130–145B, and Amazon carrying a trailing-twelve-month $173B ([digitalapplied, 31 July 2026](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/ai-capex-scorecard-earnings-week-july-2026) · [Tom's Hardware, July 2026](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/big-tech/big-techs-ai-spending-plans-reach-725-billion)). The split in the market's reaction is the actual story: Microsoft and Amazon rose 8–9% because they could point the spend at a metered, external, growing line — Azure up 43%, AWS up 37%, which CEO Andy Jassy called "our fastest growth in 18 quarters" — while Meta fell 9–10%, Apple 4–8%, and Alphabet about 5% after posting its first negative free cash flow (−$5.9B) since its 2004 IPO. The thread to a school runs under every "free for educators" banner: those tiers are marketing spend, not a metered product, and the investors underwriting them have just started pricing the difference between AI outlay that returns cash and AI outlay that only promises to.

- **A Chinese model undercut OpenAI's freshly-cut prices — and shipped drop-in compatibility with OpenAI's own agent tooling.** DeepSeek released V4-Flash-0731 on 31 July at $0.14 per million input tokens and $0.28 per million output ([explainx.ai, 31 July 2026](https://www.explainx.ai/blog/deepseek-v4-flash-0731-codex-responses-api-july-2026)) — below even the Luna tier OpenAI had discounted to $0.20/$1.20 the day before, and roughly a quarter of Luna's output price. The number that matters more than the price is the format: V4-Flash ships "native support for OpenAI's Responses API format" and "full Codex adaptation," which the write-up frames as letting it be "a lower-cost backend swapped into existing Codex-based agent workflows." No school is routing student data to a Chinese provider, and data governance is exactly why. But the interoperability move is the one to clock: if the cheap models learn to speak the expensive models' tooling, the switching cost that vendor lock-in depends on begins to erode — quietly good news for anyone who built a small classroom tool on one provider and would rather not be married to it. (Keep the caveat the same coverage flags: Chinese-provider pricing "has shifted mid-quarter before.")

- **The largest study yet of a real AI tutor measured how much students use it — not whether it taught them anything.** Researchers at Germany's IU International University analysed anonymised usage logs — not surveys — from about 77,000 distance-learning students using Syntea, the university's own AI learning companion, and found 58% used it during the observation window, rising to 64% among Gen Z against 38% of Baby Boomers ([research-in-germany.org, 31 July 2026](https://www.research-in-germany.org/idw-news/en_US/2026/7/2026-07-31_World_s_largest_AI-in-Higher-Education_Study__AI-supported_learning_is_now_part_of_everyday_student_life.html)). Lead author Prof. Kristina Schaaff: "Our study is the first to demonstrate, how students actually use an AI learning companion such as Syntea, based on real usage data." Co-author Quintus Stierstorfer: "The findings clearly demonstrate that AI-supported learning has become part of everyday student life at IU." It is the biggest real-behaviour dataset anyone has put on this question and worth reading for the usage patterns alone — when students reach for the tool, which cohorts lean on it hardest. But note precisely what it does and doesn't show: it is a map of engagement, not of learning, and that distinction matters more here than usual — enough that it is the hinge of the Sceptic's corner below.

- **Meta handed out nearly $2 million to put AI glasses on the faces of learners and disabled users.** On 27 July Meta named 30 recipients of its AI Glasses Impact Grants — 25 Accelerator awards of $25,000–$50,000 and five $200,000 Catalyst grants, across 18 states, chosen from nearly 500 applicants ([Meta, 27 July 2026](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/07/ai-glasses-helping-people-work-learn-live-independently/) · [EdTech Innovation Hub, 30 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/meta-launches-ai-glasses-impact-grants-to-back-accessibility-and-social-use-cases)). The education-facing awardee is Immerse Inc., using the glasses for "real-time, hands-free language tutoring" that Meta says draws on 48 academic studies and serves more than 200,000 learners; others coach construction trainees hands-free and help people with dementia navigate daily routines. Meta's framing is the tell — "When your work requires both hands, full attention, and real-time information...glasses are easier and safer to use" — and the Catalyst apps reach Ray-Ban Meta glasses later this year. It is early and it is Meta's platform, but hands-free, always-available AI is the form factor that would genuinely change what a language or lab classroom feels like — and, like every wearable, it walks the same camera-and-microphone data questions straight into the room.

## On the radar

- **The industry's biggest room opens today, with its own founders openly disagreeing about being there.** Ai4 2026 runs 4–6 August at The Venetian in Las Vegas — more than 12,000 attendees and nearly 400 exhibitors — and Wednesday's headline session, "The Architects of Intelligence," puts Geoffrey Hinton, Fei-Fei Li and Andrew Ng on one stage despite Hinton and Ng holding openly opposed views on whether AI is an existential risk ([TechTimes, 2 August 2026](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322674/20260802/ai4-2026-opens-tuesday-hinton-ng-face-off-ais-existential-stakes.htm)). Agentic AI dominates the agenda. The signal isn't a product; it's that the field's founders can't agree on the risk framing while enterprises book agents at scale — the same gap a school sits in when it's asked to adopt quickly and govern slowly.

- **A second university answered the assessment question the opposite way to UNAM.** Stellenbosch, one of South Africa's leading universities, has explicitly rejected a single institution-wide AI rule, pushing the decision down to the individual module and assessment: depending on the intended learning outcomes, lecturers may "prohibit, restrict, allow, encourage or require" AI ([EdTech Innovation Hub, 29 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/stellenbosch-rejects-blanket-rules-for-ai-use-in-university-assessments)). It treats AI as a teaching-and-learning question rather than a compliance one. Set against UNAM's centralise-and-go-in-person response, the two map the poles the sector is settling between — lock it down from the top, or devolve the judgement to whatever each task is actually trying to evidence. The second is harder to administer and closer to how good assessment already works.

- **Gates put a number, and an AI bet, on the "is a credential worth it" question.** The Gates Foundation announced on 29 July a goal of 10 million more US learners aged 18–34 earning "credentials of value" by 2045 — double the current growth rate — naming AI integration into instruction and college advising as central, with roughly $200 billion to spend before the foundation closes for good on 31 December 2045 ([Inside Higher Ed, 30 July 2026](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/07/30/gates-foundation-new-goal-focuses-credentials-value)). "Credentials of value" is the operative phrase, arriving the same season as survey data showing nearly half of adults expect degrees to matter less in the AI era. Worth watching whether "of value" gets defined by labour-market return — and whether the AI-advising money produces guidance students actually act on.

## Sceptic's corner

The headline the IU study will travel under is its co-author's line: AI-supported learning has "become part of everyday student life." It is the most quotable sentence in the release, and read quickly it sounds like a verdict on whether these tools work. It isn't.

Look at what the study actually measured: anonymised usage logs from 77,000 students — who opened Syntea, when, and how often. That is a rigorous, large, genuinely novel adoption dataset, and it earns its "largest yet" billing. But usage is not learning. The logs can establish that 58% of students used the companion; they cannot tell you whether those students understood more, retained longer, or — the failure mode the evidence keeps surfacing — quietly offloaded the thinking. The recent evidence base is the counterweight: a Sungkyunkwan study in which the heaviest AI users reported the highest "perceived learning" and posted the lowest test scores, and a Wharton randomised trial in which a teacher AI assistant left students less motivated and lowered exam results for the weaker classes. Engagement rose in those studies too. It was not the good news it looked like.

So the usable judgement, and it is one that Ed Tech Coaches will reach for on repeat this year: engagement is the metric a vendor can always show you — daily actives, adoption curves, "the students love it." Learning is the metric they usually can't, because it is slower and costlier to measure and sometimes comes back inconvenient. When the dashboard is all usage, the polite question is the sharp one — show me the outcome data, not the login data. "Everyone is using it" and "it is working" are different claims, and this study, for all its scale, only closes the first.
