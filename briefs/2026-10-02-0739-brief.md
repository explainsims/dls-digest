## Top story

**The children did the writing; the adults checked the AI.** A new Joan Ganz Cooney Center report, published on 1 October, follows the co-design of The GIANT Remix, a literacy and creativity platform developed with teachers and second- and third-grade pupils in two New York City Title I schools. Across three semesters in 2024–25, the project involved four second-grade and two third-grade classrooms, 120 students and 10 teachers. Teachers chose curriculum goals; children drew, wrote, critiqued generated avatars and revised their own texts before publishing books and trading cards ([dated release](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/publication/remixing-the-future/); [report, pp. 6–7](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/jgcc_remixingthefuture.pdf)). The interesting product feature is the sequence: a machine-assisted publication process built around work the child has already done.

The safety mechanism is unusually tangible. An adult reviews every generated avatar before children see it, and every AI feedback comment before it reaches the writer. Trained educators were present during the pilots. Paper-and-pencil work and peer collaboration were deliberately retained, rather than treated as inconvenient remnants of the old classroom. Students were also taught to spot mistakes and give the system feedback. That makes critical judgement part of the activity, not a warning slide delivered before the fun starts ([report, pp. 14–16, 20–21](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/jgcc_remixingthefuture.pdf)).

Keep the evidence label attached. This is a co-design case study, co-authored by The GIANT Room's founder and CEO, not an independent efficacy trial. It describes an AI-literacy comparison and positive observations, but does not supply the assignment and effect-size detail needed for a causal learning claim. Nor do six primary classrooms establish what works in high school. For international schools, the transferable idea is a design question for student authorship: which decisions stay with the learner, which outputs need adult review, and what does the finished work reveal about the learning? A polished book is gratifying. The student's explanation of the revisions is the more demanding exhibit.

## Other stories

- **A new maths research roundup separates useful routines from impressive software.** Digital Promise's 1 October account of its first AIMS EduData cohort describes research using data from established maths platforms. In a Vanderbilt-led spring pilot, translating annual i-Ready targets into adaptive weekly goals raised weekly goal attainment from 26% to 40%; a controlled trial is planned for the autumn. That is a promising change in practice, not yet a causal result. At two KIPP middle schools in Camden, an AI-driven Khan Academy programme had low engagement and a largely unused Khanmigo tutor. Moving towards lesson-aligned content midyear did not rescue engagement; across the year, learning was about the same as with existing software ([Digital Promise, 1 October](https://digitalpromise.org/2026/10/01/what-aims-edudata-cohort-1-learned-about-teachers-and-students/)). The researchers interpret the problem as fit with curriculum and routine, and the next rollout will have clearer ownership. That interpretation deserves testing too: it cannot make a disappointing outcome disappear. For school pilots, record when students use a tool, what lesson it supports and who owns the implementation, alongside the attainment measure. Otherwise a flat result produces competing stories about the product and the rollout, with no way to distinguish them. The weekly-goal finding is also a useful reminder that a smaller, intelligible target may matter more than another dashboard.

- **Google's AI training festival produced thousands of badges, with learning still to demonstrate.** Its 1 October post says more than 1,400 educators attended a ten-hour live drop-in Badge-a-thon and earned over 4,000 digital badges. Twenty sessions are now available on demand, covering workflows including rubrics and reading comprehension; a toolkit supports local events, and Google points to routes from badges to continuing-education or graduate credit ([Google, 1 October](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-educator-series-badge-a-thon/)). Those counts establish reach and participation. The post supplies no comparison group, assessment of teacher judgement or follow-up evidence about pupil learning. The resources can still be useful: a teacher does not need a randomised trial before watching a practical demonstration. At a Google-first school, however, vendor training needs translation into the school's actual instructional and data expectations. A rubric session is worth assessing by the quality of the resulting rubric, including whether it rewards the intended thinking and handles language differences fairly. A comprehension session needs a check that the AI has not done the interpreting the pupil was meant to practise. The badge is a record of activity; a reviewed classroom artifact and a later example of student work would tell a more interesting story.

- **AI writing feedback helped with sentence-level accuracy while broader structure stayed stable.** An article published on 1 October in the *Turkish Online Journal of Distance Education* examines an eight-week exploratory action-research cycle combining automated writing evaluation with teacher feedback in English-as-a-foreign-language instruction. The authors' abstract reports gains in micro-level linguistic accuracy alongside stable rhetorical structure. Teachers managed the volume of feedback, clarified its meaning and helped students regulate reliance on it. Students valued speed and coverage, but usability problems, digital-fluency differences, motivation and concerns about voice constrained engagement ([Suzan and Esfer, 1 October](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/tojde/article/1890182)). This account rests on the published abstract; it does not justify an effect-size claim or a claim that AI caused the improvement. Its practical distinction is still valuable in a multilingual school: cleaner sentences and a stronger argument are separate outcomes. If a student accepts twenty corrections without understanding three of them, the revised document may improve while the writer stands still. Teacher mediation should therefore mean more than checking whether the machine's advice is technically correct. It includes deciding which suggestions are worth the student's attention, asking for reasons to accept or reject them, and protecting the student's meaning. The feedback bottleneck may be interpretation, not generation.

- **Google.org is funding implementation work, with the results mostly ahead of it.** At EDUCAUSE, its 30 September announcement outlined support for adult-learner development, career-connected community learning and community-college redesign. The Urban Adult Learner Institute will integrate AI-related tasks into practitioner certification and test approaches across 30 universities. A separate project will examine ten community-based learning models over 15 months and publish case studies. Education Design Lab's next phase is a national competition in early 2027 for a state-level college system to undertake a two-year redesign initiative ([Google.org, 30 September](https://blog.google/company-news/outreach-and-initiatives/google-org/educause-2026/)). These are programme commitments, not demonstrated improvements in completion, employability or learning. They also concern US higher education, so the institutional model does not transfer wholesale to an international high school. What travels is the choice to fund the connecting tissue: adviser practice, authentic work and a longer institutional redesign cycle. In project-based learning and independent research, the analogous question is whether AI helps a student connect inquiry to a real audience or merely makes the deliverable look more professional. Watch for the promised cases to explain what students actually did, how supervisors judged it and which constraints remained. Access to a tool is the beginning of that account, not its conclusion.

## On the radar

- **A university grant pairs short AI courses with an assessment boundary.** South Dakota State University announced a three-year, $750,000 US Department of Education grant on 30 September. Plans include a 30-day faculty programme with daily ten- to twenty-minute lessons, a 15-day student programme, and a tool for AI-assisted oral exams or quizzes in which the professor retains grading responsibility ([SDSU, 30 September](https://www.sdstate.edu/news/2026/09/education-grant-supports-ai-literacy-sdsu)). This is funded programming rather than an evaluated result. For schools watching oral assessment evolve, the consequential detail is where the human judgement remains. An automated questioner still needs scrutiny for question quality, accessibility and whether it elicits the student's understanding rather than rehearsed fluency.

- **Barclays' AI scale numbers describe operations, not learning.** Anthropic's 1 October partnership announcement says more than 16,000 Barclays colleagues use a knowledge assistant that has handled over a million searches; another workflow processes about 120,000 emails daily. The bank expects Claude Code adoption to reach half its developers by year-end ([Anthropic, 1 October](https://www.anthropic.com/news/barclays-scales-claude)). These are vendor-reported deployment counts and a future adoption target, not an independent productivity estimate. The school relevance is architectural: the knowledge assistant retrieves institutional information, while email classification routes work. Such bounded jobs may offer clearer verification than an all-purpose assistant. A school would still need its own error, permission and service-quality measures before borrowing the success claim.

- **A student engineering case gives the design process a real recipient.** In a 30 September Digital Promise account, a Dove Virtual Academy educator describes students adapting a battery-powered ride-on car for a child who could not use its foot pedal. They researched accessibility, rewired controls, tested prototypes and delivered the result to the family. The same page invites Ciena Solutions Challenge submissions through 8 March 2027 and applications for a $3,000 Sustainability Award ([Digital Promise, 30 September](https://digitalpromise.org/2026/09/30/what-happens-when-student-innovation-is-rooted-in-empathy/)). This is a teacher's project account, not a comparative study. Its useful constraint for school technology projects is that an actual person's needs set the brief, and a working result faces a test beyond the presentation slides.

## Sceptic's corner

The convenient explanation for reluctant teachers is that they need more confidence. Sometimes they do. Sometimes they have noticed that the proposed workflow removes a piece of thinking they were trying to teach. Those are different diagnoses, and a training attendance sheet cannot separate them.

Mark McCormack's 1 October EDUCAUSE research note argues that resistance can be a healthy demand for evidence of institutional value. He cites a survey in which 94% of higher-education professionals reported AI use in the previous six months, while concerns about misinformation and data used without consent remained widespread ([EDUCAUSE, 1 October](https://er.educause.edu/articles/2026/10/resistance-to-ai-isnt-futile-its-healthy)). That statistic comes from a January report cited by the new essay; it is not a fresh October adoption survey, and the respondents are not schoolteachers.

The counterweight is that scepticism can become a comfortable veto, too. A teacher who rejects every tool without testing a bounded use is no more evidence-led than a vendor who treats every badge as progress. Today's co-design report offers a better standard: identify the learning goal, preserve the learner's consequential decisions, specify the human review, and inspect the work that results. Then compare the effort and outcome with a credible existing approach. Adult review has a cost; a less conspicuous tool may accomplish the same thing. The case for adoption gets stronger when it survives those questions. Training should make teachers better at asking them, including when the answer is to leave the tool out.
