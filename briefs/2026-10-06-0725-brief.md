## Top story

**The next AI teaching tool may watch what the teacher notices.** Rowan University’s 5 October announcement describes a National Science Foundation-backed project led by Molly Fisher with colleagues at the University of Kentucky. They are exploring whether AI can help mathematics teachers assess their professional noticing: attending to student behaviour, interpreting the thinking behind it and deciding how to respond. The team plans to train models on data from previous studies to recognise examples of effective noticing ([Rowan University, 5 October](https://today.rowan.edu/news/2026/10/researcher-explores-how-ai-could-help-educators-understand-student-thinking.html)). This is research in progress, with an envisaged feedback tool. The announcement reports no evaluated product or demonstrated improvement in teaching or learning.

That distinction protects a promising idea from its own headline. A learner who quietly counts, hesitates or changes strategy can be telling the teacher something a correct answer conceals. The interesting use of AI here would be to help a teacher revisit an interpretation, rather than produce another worksheet. But Fisher identifies a difficulty that comes before model training: the field has struggled to measure what good noticing actually is. Training on examples does not remove that judgement. It places the judgement inside the training set.

For international schools, the useful professional-learning question is what makes an observation worth acting on. A pause could indicate uncertainty, language processing or careful thought; an assistant that confidently labels it may narrow the teacher’s view instead of extending it. Any eventual trial would need to show the observation behind the suggestion, allow the teacher to disagree and distinguish developmental feedback from staff evaluation. That last boundary deserves plain language before a camera or transcript enters the room. A tool that supports reflection and a tool that scores teachers can share the same interface while creating very different incentives. The project is worth following because it starts with teacher expertise. Whether it preserves that expertise will depend on how disagreement is handled.

## Other stories

- **Google’s new agent-security report asks whether a permitted action is appropriate in its context.** Google Research’s 5 October publication introduces a workshop report involving more than 50 academic and industry participants, following a workshop in late 2025. It applies contextual integrity—the appropriateness of information flowing between particular people for particular purposes—to agents that also take actions. Its proposed architecture includes a contextual policy engine in a supervisory layer, alongside work on sandboxing, model reasoning, user controls and governance ([Google Research, 5 October](https://www.research.google/blog/open-and-emergent-problems-in-agentic-privacy-and-security-a-contextual-angle/)). These are research directions, not a newly deployed protection in Workspace. The school example is straightforward: permission to read a document does not settle whether an assistant may quote it in a parent message, share it with another service or use it for a different task. A file permission describes access; the purpose describes the acceptable use. For Ed Tech Coaches reviewing an agent demonstration, the revealing moment is a change of audience halfway through the job. Can the system recognise that the information flow has changed, explain the consequence and stop before disclosure? The report’s value is making that problem explicit. The diagram alone does not establish that a product solves it.

- **Writing feedback improved local accuracy while leaving the larger argument much as it was.** Dila Suzan and Sezin Esfer’s study, published on 1 October, follows an eight-week exploratory action-research cycle using ProWritingAid with 30 B1 English learners aged 17–18. It is a single-class study, with qualitative observations and supporting indicators, rather than a controlled comparison. Linguistic accuracy improved across the cycle; rhetorical structure did not show a statistically significant change ([Turkish Online Journal of Distance Education, 1 October](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/tojde/article/1890182)). Teacher mediation included prioritising two or three feedback points, explaining technical suggestions and having learners justify revisions. The paper also records usability, digital-fluency and voice concerns. For multilingual secondary classrooms, the distinction is useful: fewer sentence-level errors and a stronger argument are different achievements. The teacher still has to decide which feedback serves the lesson. A paragraph can become grammatically smoother while keeping the same unsupported claim. Asking students to explain one accepted correction and one rejected suggestion makes that decision visible. It also gives them a defensible reason to preserve an unusual phrase that carries their meaning. Treat the study as an account of a classroom routine worth examining, with the teacher’s adapting practice part of the intervention—not as proof that automated feedback independently produced the gains.

- **A large arithmetic study offers modest support for adaptive practice, using data from before the chatbot era.** Radboud University’s 2 October research report describes 7,885 Dutch primary pupils followed over three years, using data from 2014–2017. Classes using adaptive arithmetic exercises showed more positive growth on average than classes without them. The researchers describe small effects, more visible in larger schools and schools serving disadvantaged populations. The cited paper is a longitudinal observational study, listed for the February 2027 issue of *Learning and Instruction* ([Radboud University, 2 October](https://www.ru.nl/onderzoek/onderzoeksnieuws/leren-rekenen-gaat-iets-beter-met-ai-maar-leraar-speelt-sleutelrol)). The fresh event is the university’s report; the classroom data are older, and the technology adjusts exercise difficulty rather than generating conversational answers. That separation matters when somebody uses evidence for adaptive practice to sell a general chatbot. The results suggest a bounded use can be helpful without making the teacher dispensable or every digital method equivalent. For a school considering practice software, the questions are which skill it diagnoses, how it chooses the next exercise and what the teacher sees when a student gets stuck. The system’s record is narrower than the teacher’s knowledge of that learner. A small sustained difference can still be worthwhile; it needs a case about the particular task and pupils, not a borrowed halo around the word AI.

- **Cambodia’s teacher-development announcement puts AI inside a career-long support system.** In its 5 October World Teachers’ Day release, UNESCO says Cambodia is developing its first national digital and AI competency framework for teachers. It places that work within the $27.02 million STEPCam teacher-education programme, financed by the Global Partnership for Education. The release reports 84 accredited continuing-professional-development courses across 11 providers, alongside changes to initial teacher preparation and entry pathways ([UNESCO, 5 October](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/press-release-world-teachers-day-cambodia-ministry-education-and-unesco-affirm-essential-role)). The framework is under development; this is an official account of reform, not an independent evaluation of its effects. The regional signal is the architecture: digital competence has somewhere to live beyond a one-off workshop. For international schools, that raises a practical question about progression. A beginning teacher learning to interpret AI output, an experienced colleague redesigning assessment and a leader choosing a vendor need different evidence of competence. Attendance at the same introductory session cannot certify all three. A useful development pathway would connect a small classroom problem, supported practice, feedback and a later review of what changed. It would also make time for that work. An AI capability framework can name the destination quite elegantly; the continuing support determines whether anyone can actually travel there.

- **TED and Google are asking educators for ideas, with a route to the stage and a sponsor in the room.** Their 5 October announcement opens a global search to primary, secondary and higher-education educators actively using AI in teaching or curriculum design. The entry point is a free, asynchronous one-hour Idea Workshop. Five educators will be selected to develop talks for TED2027 in San Diego, 1–4 March; submissions close on 15 November 2026 at 11:59 p.m. Eastern Time ([TED, 5 October](https://blog.ted.com/ai-is-changing-how-we-teach-and-learn-educators-should-shape-what-comes-next/)). Workshop completers receive public-speaking and community resources, including materials from Google’s AI Educator Series. This is an opportunity to articulate practice, not evidence that any featured approach improves learning. The partnership should remain visible when the resulting talks arrive: selection for a compelling idea is different from selection through an effectiveness review. Still, requiring teachers to make an argument has more promise than another catalogue of prompts. The useful account would name the original problem, the learner’s actual work, an instance where the approach failed and the evidence that justified keeping it. A persuasive story can bring a good classroom practice to wider attention. It becomes much more transferable when it includes the conditions under which another teacher should decline to copy it.

## Sceptic's corner

Today’s reassuring phrase is that the teacher remains in control. It appears in discussions of adaptive practice, feedback tools and professional learning, and it is a sensible ambition. It is also easy to say while changing the conditions under which the teacher makes a decision.

Consider the noticing project. A suggestion arriving instantly, decorated with a confident label, may be harder to set aside than a colleague’s tentative observation. If disagreeing requires extra explanation while accepting takes one click, the interface has already expressed a preference. A nominal veto is a thin form of professional agency.

The writing study offers a more concrete counterexample. The teacher triaged dense feedback, interpreted it and asked learners to justify their choices. Those are observable acts of judgement, rather than a reassurance in the product description. Its single-class design limits the causal claim, but the account makes the human work visible enough to discuss.

Nor is teacher control a reason to discount the arithmetic result. Small gains in a bounded task may be useful precisely because the technology leaves other decisions with the teacher. The challenge is to preserve that division when the system becomes more fluent and ambitious.

When evaluating an assistant, watch what happens after a person disagrees. Can they inspect the grounds, reverse the suggestion and continue without penalty or repeated pressure? If the person can only approve a conclusion they cannot examine, control is mostly ceremonial. The teacher’s chair should come with more than a large accept button.
