## Top story

**University students managed their learning better when AI came with structured guidance.** Xing Liu and Yumeng Yang's 26 November meta-analysis pulled together 76 effect sizes from 26 studies on self-regulated learning. That's the ability to set a goal, notice whether a strategy is working and change course when it isn't. In the ten studies that directly compared generative AI with and without an added instructional intervention, the pooled difference was moderate, at Hedges' g = 0.528, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.359 to 0.697. All ten direct comparisons were in higher education. The figure gives us a reason to take the design of classroom guidance seriously and leaves open how much it helps younger students. [Liu and Yang, 26 November](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1968882/full).

There's a second comparison in the paper worth keeping separate. The researchers looked at studies that compared AI-supported learning with learning without AI. They estimated a positive effect for AI alone and a larger positive effect for AI combined with instruction. The difference between those two estimates fell short of statistical significance (p = 0.089). The direct comparison above asks a cleaner question for a school that has already made a tool available. What happens when students get help deciding what to do with it? The paper also notes that it could not account for correlations among outcomes measured on the same students, which could make the pooled estimates look more precise than they are. [Liu and Yang, methods and results](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1968882/full).

For international schools, there's a small enough classroom question here to try with the tools they already have. In a physics lesson, a student predicts which variable matters, asks an approved assistant for one challenge to that prediction and decides whether to revise it. On a second problem, the student has to spot the same trap unaided. The first answer gives the teacher something to discuss. The second makes it possible to see whether the student learned a way to check their thinking.

## Other stories

- **Tokyo puts teacher workload on the agenda for its school AI platform.** On 26 November, the Tokyo Metropolitan Board of Education opened comments on its draft school work reform plan through 25 December. The board's materials say the existing Toritsu AI environment was built for 256 metropolitan schools and 160,000 people. Its chart classifies 14.7% of configured AI menus as school administration, with the rest for learning. Those are categories of available menus. How often staff used them and how much time they saved are different measurements. The draft proposes practical staff training that includes checking AI answers and examples built around everyday work, such as drafting school documents, organising minutes and preparing explanations. A school could test the same proposition on one repetitive job, with fictional details where necessary. Time the draft, the check against the source and the corrections someone makes before submission. The document the recipient can actually use is where the stopwatch should stop. [Tokyo Metropolitan Board of Education consultation, 26 November](https://www.kyoiku.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/ja/information/press/2026/11/2026112601). [Board's draft, pp. 38–39](https://www.kyoiku.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/documents/d/kyoiku/2026-11-26-103535-876).

- **Australia passes a duty of care that reaches AI chatbots.** Communications Minister Anika Wells announced parliamentary passage on 25 November. The legislation requires covered services such as social media platforms, games, apps and AI chatbots to protect Australian users from serious harms, with specific protections for under-18s. The department says the duty takes effect 12 months after Royal Assent. The minister puts potential penalties at up to 5% of global turnover or A$109.2 million, whichever is greater. A reporting button is useful after something has gone wrong. The duty asks providers to take responsibility earlier, including for how features behave. That suggests a question for school procurement even outside Australia. What changes when a user is identified as a minor, and where can a school see that change? A supplier can show what a student account encounters in a scripted test and who at the supplier is responsible for fixing a failure. This is a decision about Australian services, with any effect on other markets still to be seen. [Communications minister, 25 November](https://minister.infrastructure.gov.au/wells/media-release/australias-digital-duty-care-becomes-law). [Department summary, 27 November](https://www.infrastructure.gov.au/department/media/news/digital-duty-care-law-passed).

- **Goa opens an AI study lab with its disability commissioner.** The nonprofit I-Stem launched Nclude Labs at Santa Cruz Higher Secondary School on 26 November and exchanged a memorandum of understanding with the state disability commissioner's office for a wider rollout. The programme uses iTutor, a voice-based study tool within I-Stem's wider Nclude platform. A learner can ask a question aloud and work at their own pace. The organiser lists labs already operating in four other Indian states. That's evidence of places where the programme operates. Whether students can use their own textbooks and class materials independently, and whether the independence lasts beyond the lab session, are results to look for next. Voice can give a learner a way to ask for help when printed text is a barrier. It also raises practical questions about recognition across accents, what happens with a formula and how a student can find and revisit an answer. Try a real lesson's materials in the format the learner will use. An impressive conversation in a quiet room sets a low bar. [Nclude Labs release, 26 November](https://www.csrbox.org/India_CSR_news_AI-study-tool-that-talks-back--I-Stem%27s-Nclude-Labs%2C-now-in-five-states_3514).

- **The UAE gives families a guide to the AI curriculum.** The Ministry of Education and the office of the Minister of State for Artificial Intelligence launched a parent guide on 26 November for the curriculum introduced in public schools in 2025–26. It outlines seven areas from basic concepts and data to fairness, project design and responsibility. Families get advice on protecting private information, checking claims and setting age-appropriate boundaries. ## Sceptic's corner

The appealing sales claim is that an AI study assistant makes students independent because it can plan a session, spot a gap and cue the next step. That describes a plausible way to get a student through a session. Independence is a claim about what the student can do when the assistant stops cueing. Liu and Yang's direct comparison favours adding instructional support to AI use in higher education. Their K–12 evidence comes from comparisons with no-AI conditions. A school buying a planning or reflection feature would still have to find out whether younger students can keep the habit when the on-screen structure disappears. [Liu and Yang, discussion and limitations](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1968882/full).

Consider two students who get through the same revision set. One takes the assistant's judgements and does the next exercise it serves. The other predicts which item will be hard, asks for a single hint and keeps a record of why one answer changed. Both dashboards log a completed session. Give each student a new problem and five minutes to make a plan, then ask what would cause them to change it. The teacher can see who picked up a strategy that travels. It is a small test, and it can fit inside a lesson. If a product claims to teach independence, ask where it gives the learner a chance to practise doing without it.
