## Top story

**The awkward minutes of a lesson deserve a closer look.** On 9 November, [Southern Cross University announced two Lismore seminars for 11 November](https://www.scu.edu.au/news/2026/do-classrooms-need-a-digital-detox/), one for families and one for teachers, to share evidence and a [practical toolkit](https://screensineducation.org/toolkit/) on classroom screen use. The Macquarie University-led project collected data between August and November 2025 and released its [survey](https://screensineducation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/SurveyReport.pdf) and [classroom observations](https://screensineducation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/ClassroomObservationReport.pdf) on 5 October 2026. This week's development is the work of putting those findings in the hands of teachers and parents. The research behind it spans 1,057 surveyed students, 914 current teachers and 1,686 parents or carers, alongside 57 hours of observation across 68 classrooms.

The survey estimates about 68 minutes of learning-related screen use at school in Years 5–6 and 131 minutes in Years 7–8, using the midpoints of the time ranges students selected for their most recent school day. The observers saw disengagement cluster around conditions such as waiting, changes of activity and unclear tasks. Most observed off-task periods were brief. Schools nominated lessons in which digital tools would be used, so the observations describe those lessons. The survey helps locate a step change in reported exposure. The observations offer clues about where adults can make a difference inside a lesson. Estimating the effect of a daily screen limit on attainment would require a different design.

For international schools, the primary-to-secondary handover is a useful place to look. Imagine a class opening shared documents to compare evidence. A student who gets in immediately can start sorting examples. The student waiting for access has an idle device and no next move. The teacher can have both students mark up a paper extract or discuss a sample while the documents load, then bring the screens back for the comparison. That keeps the reason for using the device clear and gives teachers something observable to change when a lesson regularly loses attention at the same point.

## Other stories

- **A Sheets canvas gets a second audience.** [Google's 11 November Workspace notice](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/11/publish-sheets-canvas-as-standalone-link.html) lets a canvas editor publish a standalone, read-only link to an interactive calendar, board or dashboard without granting access to the full spreadsheet. The link has its own audience setting. A publisher can choose a static snapshot or automatic republication as the source changes, then change the audience or stop publishing from the web interface. Rapid Release started on 11 November and Scheduled Release starts on 2 December, with up to 15 days for visibility in either case. The listed education entitlement is the Google AI Pro for Education add-on. Google applies the existing Drive setting for publishing files to the web. The school-sized use is a filterable activity calendar that can go in whichever learning platform families already visit, with the planning file still limited to its editors. That division works best when the calendar draws from a separate spreadsheet containing only data intended for its audience.

- **Richmond's incident notice reaches beyond current enrolment.** In a [12 November notice](https://rvaschools.net/notice-of-cyber-incident), Richmond Public Schools in Virginia said it detected system disruptions on 21 October and brought in outside specialists. The district said personal information may have been exposed and attributed claims of access or acquisition involving current and former students, staff and contractors to the people behind the incident. Its list of categories in affected systems includes contact and identity details and limited medical information. Exposure of particular records remains uncertain. The notice offers free Experian monitoring with separate enrolment instructions for minors and adults. A school can keep a family reachable for everyday announcements through its current portal and still have no workable route to a graduate from five years ago. A records map needs to show how long those files are retained and who is responsible for reaching people whose school accounts have expired. Otherwise the people holding the records and the people managing the response may be working from different lists.

- **A geometry prototype makes the dependencies visible.** In a [preprint submitted on 9 November](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13368v1), the authors report a small randomised, think-aloud comparison with 12 US secondary students. Ender links steps in a two-column proof to the diagram and shows how claims depend on earlier ones. The comparison group used a digital version of the conventional two-column display with a static diagram. Students averaged 80.5% accuracy with Ender and 58.2% with the comparison display, with the clearest advantage on questions about spotting and fixing mistakes in the proofs. The numbers describe performance with the display in one session, with six students in each group. A delayed proof written independently would answer a different question. One lesson idea survives even if the prototype never leaves the lab. Present a plausible but faulty proof, mark the first place a claim depends on something unproved, and have students defend that choice. The diagram can carry some of the work of finding the step while students still have to supply the mathematical reason.

- **An LMS reading tool puts the layout in the learner's hands.** In its [11 November Ally 2.21 release notes](https://community.blackboard.com/public/blogs/ally-221-release-to-production-november-11-2026-2026-11-11), Blackboard says Enhanced Reader is available automatically in existing Blackboard, Canvas, Brightspace and Moodle integrations. Students can select the new alternative format for a course file, open it in a browser, adjust typeface, size and spacing, use a reading ruler, and take the reading offline with their settings intact. The same release upgrades the AI Alt Text Assistant and adds administrator switches for PDF fixes for missing titles, missing language metadata and scanned documents. Reading preferences travel with the downloaded version. The original file still travels through other routes, including email and shared folders. A representative assignment can show whether a student can find and use the alternative format from the course page. Opening the original through the other routes shows whether its headings, tables and diagrams are still accessible. A usable handout needs to survive the way students actually receive it.

- **A French expert panel gives AI literacy a place in primary school.** The Élysée [received its report on 10 November](https://www.elysee.fr/emmanuel-macron/2026/11/10/remise-du-rapport-de-la-commission-dexperts-ia-generative-et-vulnerabilites). The [commission's recommendations 12 and 13](https://www.conseil-ia-numerique.fr/files/uploads/2026/Rapport_Commission_IA_generative_vulnerabilites_2026.pdf) call for a dedicated programme from primary school with minimum teaching hours and competencies, and for classroom models suited to age and development. The panel favours tools that guide by questioning and proposes that a scientific education council evaluate classroom labelling. These are proposals from experts. Their pairing clarifies an issue that trips up school conversations about AI literacy. A Year 5 class can compare a machine-produced claim with a reliable source, discuss who takes responsibility for a mistake, and practise explaining the check without signing pupils up for a chatbot. A separate decision applies when children use a model themselves. At that point, a teacher needs to see the tool work on a realistic task, including how it responds when a child asks for the answer and keeps asking.

## On the radar

- **Apple puts sign-language interpretation on the player menu.** [Apple announced on 10 November](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/11/apple-tv-introduces-new-sign-language-interpretation-feature/) that supported Apple TV titles would get the option starting on 13 November with *Being Heumann*, its film about disability-rights activist Judy Heumann. Apple also names American and British Sign Language for *F1*. Its Apple-device requirements list version 27.2 or later across the supported operating systems. The interpretation was designed and tested with Deaf and native signers. The development worth watching is whether services make it easy for a teacher or student to discover the right language and version before choosing a film. Availability on a given title and device matters at the point of playback.

- **Seoul's standards programme put the curriculum itself on the table.** [1EdTech Korea's 11–12 November programme](https://1edtechkorea.org/en/events/learning-impact-korea-2026) listed a demonstration of Korea's national curriculum and job competency standards as machine-readable data using CASE, alongside a bilingual dictionary spanning 12 standards and a comparison with US and EU skills frameworks. The second day was scheduled as a hands-on session on standards and certification. The useful follow-through is what happens when one system imports a curriculum standard, another attaches it to an assignment, and a later revision has to be traced across both. A shared identifier is the beginning of that test. It matters whether the tools preserve it through a real export and reimport.

- **A group-chat simulator will need a test outside the simulation.** In a [10 November preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14260v1), Lilo puts children into a staged peer dispute and uses a mentor character to prompt them to pause and consider their responses. Researchers recruited 28 children aged 9–11, with 26 included in the survey analysis. Reported confidence about coping or intervening rose after the session. The study used one session and had no control group or follow-up measure of real group-chat behaviour. The development to watch is whether a controlled comparison can show transfer, including for students who learn to spot when a situation calls for an adult rather than another reply.

## Sceptic's corner

The tempting claim about [Google's standalone canvas link](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/11/publish-sheets-canvas-as-standalone-link.html) is that keeping a source spreadsheet restricted makes its read-only dashboard a safe object to circulate. The release gives the canvas its own audience and optional automatic republication. [Google's help page](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/17035851?hl=en) adds a consequential detail. The underlying data goes straight to the viewer's browser and may be accessible there even when the spreadsheet itself is restricted.

Take an activities schedule. At launch it shows the day, room and supervising adult, all intended for the audience. Later, a coordinator adds a pupil roster to the working file. If that working table feeds the canvas, the calendar's visible columns are a poor guide to what its audience could receive. A later filter or detail view may surface a field that was previously out of sight. The people with the published link can still be the same people who bookmarked it when it was simply an activities schedule.

A static version freezes the data at publication and can go stale. A live schedule can draw from a separate spreadsheet with only publishable fields or with aggregate totals where those are sufficient. That keeps the sensitive data out of the published canvas's input. A viewer account can then check how the calendar behaves after a representative edit. Give the canvas only the data its audience is entitled to receive.
