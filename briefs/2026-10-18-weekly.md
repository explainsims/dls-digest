# Week ending Sunday, 18 October 2026

## The Week That Was

Over the past ten days, three things moved together: the frontier went behind paywalls, free tiers moved down, and Australia's national evidence body told schools to slow down.

**Background, Wednesday 7 to Friday 9 October:** OpenAI put GPT-6 with "Intelligent UI", answers built as interactive diagrams, calculators and forms, into paid ChatGPT tiers on 7 October, with Free and Go users following the next day on the smaller GPT-6 Luna ([OpenAI, 7 October](https://openai.com/index/gpt-6-for-everyone/)). On 9 October Google went the other way. Personal Gemini accounts without a paid plan lost Flash and Pro as choices; Auto now sends most of their prompts to Flash-Lite ([9to5Google, 3 October](https://9to5google.com/2026/10/03/gemini-model-limits-oct-26/); [Google's help page](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/17004136)). Google scopes the change to personal accounts. For schools, a student's personal Gemini just got weaker, which changes how it compares with a school account.

**Tuesday 13 October:** Gemini 4 Argon, gated since 30 September, reached the public, or the paying part of it: Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers in the Gemini app, and the paid API tier at an introductory $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens until 30 November ([Google, 13 October](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-argon/)). Gemini for business followed on 14 October ([Google Cloud, 14 October](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/gemini-4-argon-available-on-google-cloud)). None of the announcements mentions Education editions.

**Same day:** Google delayed Workspace skills "until further notice", while Gemini-app skills began rolling out to users of all ages, finishing by mid-November. On the second point Google is plain: there are no admin controls for skills in the Gemini app ([Google, 13 October](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/an-update-on-launch-of-skills-in-gemini-app-and-workspace.html)). Whatever students build there, nobody can switch off centrally.

**Wednesday 14 October:** The Australian Education Research Organisation published two explainers on AI in schools. Its five considerations are evidence-based teaching, monitoring risks to learning, clear expectations, assessment, and a culturally responsive lens. Its candid note on the evidence: mostly recent, short-term, exploratory and conducted outside Australia ([AERO, 14 October](https://www.edresearch.edu.au/summaries-explainers/explainers/ai-schools-considerations-school-leaders-and-teachers)). Chief executive Jenny Donovan went further with ABC News: use AI sparingly, avoid it in the first three years of primary school, and introduce it in high school ([ABC News, 14 October](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-10-14/ai-in-schools-warning-affecting-long-term-learning/107263444)). The written guidance asks only for greater caution before secondary. The bright line came in the interview.

**Also Wednesday:** the attorneys general of Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho, Montana, Iowa, Kansas, West Virginia and Nebraska filed separate lawsuits against OpenAI, alleging inadequate safeguards for minors and other vulnerable users and citing its rogue agents. They want profits made while minors were harmed handed back, but named no damages figure ([Bloomberg Law, 14 October](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/antitrust/half-dozen-states-hit-openai-with-kids-online-safety-lawsuits); [AP via Fortune, 17 October](https://fortune.com/2026/10/17/states-lawsuit-openai-cyberhacking-agents-chatgpt-harm-children-adults/)). OpenAI says it has built age-specific protections and wants industry-wide rules. These are allegations, not findings, but discovery is where vendor safety claims finally get tested.

## What Does It All Mean?

AERO's headline word was "sparingly". Is quantity the right dial for a school to turn?

AERO's own document suggests not. Its stated concern is whether AI use supports or displaces the thinking learning requires, not whether it improves immediate performance ([AERO](https://www.edresearch.edu.au/sites/default/files/2026-10/aero-ai-in-schools-considerations-for-school-leaders-and-teachers-aa.pdf)). That is a question about manner, and the best large dataset agrees.

David Strömberg, Victor Lei and Yanhui Wu followed 26,811 Chinese secondary students in grades 7–12 over 30 months ([CEPR discussion paper, 2026](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/21577.html)). Adopting generative AI raised homework scores by 18% and cut completion time by 30%. Monthly exam scores then fell by 20% within six months. The losses were concentrated in roughly 80% of users whose pattern looked like outsourcing: very short completion times with high scores. Users who kept their homework time close to non-users' saw small losses. Same tool, same access, different outcome.

Dose matters less cleanly than the word implies. A meta-analysis in *Educational Psychology Review*, published 3 October, pooled 47 randomised trials ([Ren and colleagues](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-026-10217-5)). Effects on academic outcomes were large (Hedges' g = 0.68). Effects on cognitive outcomes were small and not significant (g = 0.17). Academic gains peaked at about 22.79 hours, then levelled off with a slight decline. These were designed interventions, not open chatbot use, and the gap between those two numbers is the real warning: scores can move while thinking doesn't.

Then there is how little anyone knows. A Stanford SCALE chapter by Cristina Barnard, Lily Fesler and Susanna Loeb reports that a review of more than 800 papers found only 20 high-quality causal studies. None were US K–12 studies of student-facing tools, and many measured performance while students still had the AI or shortly after ([SCALE](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/artificial-intelligence-in-k%E2%80%9312-schools.pdf)). AERO's evidence note cites the same Fesler review. The age line, then, is caution reasonably applied to an evidence gap, not a finding.

For a high school, AERO's advice points towards introducing AI deliberately rather than rationing minutes. The cheapest monitor is the one Strömberg's data supplies: high-quality work arriving in implausibly little time. Teachers already notice this. The practical move is to treat it as a learning signal rather than an integrity charge, and to follow a tool-assisted task with a short unaided check.

## Behind You! Behind You!

**Anthropic opened a Singapore office and signed an MOU with the Ministry of Digital Development and Information.** The [14 October announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/singapore-office-opening) covers safety evaluation, research on AI adoption and responsible use across the public and private sectors. Education is not mentioned. Singapore ranks second of 121 countries for Claude.ai usage relative to population, and more than 1,500 GovTech staff use Claude Code. Governments are now signing with labs directly. Whether school-sector deals follow is the thing to watch.

**Anthropic gave six METR evaluators near-employee access.** Under the [15 October arrangement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/an-update-on-independent-evaluators), METR receives company hardware, office space and internal tools, and Anthropic cannot veto its publications, only require redaction of predefined categories such as customer data. It describes this as the highest of three access tiers. For schools taking vendors' safety claims on trust, "who checked, with what access, and can they publish?" is now a reasonable question to ask of every supplier.

**OpenAI marked Brazil's Teachers' Day with Study Mode and a Portuguese educator course.** The [15 October post](https://openai.com/index/teaching-students-to-think-and-learn-through-questions/) launches AI for Educators in Brazilian Portuguese on OpenAI Academy. Its own model lesson is telling: after using Study Mode on evaporation, each student writes an explanation and a discussion question without referring back to the conversation. Even the vendor's exemplar ends with the tool switched off.

**Gmail's AI Inbox reached paid Workspace users with no admin switch.** The [15 October update](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/gmails-ai-inbox-is-rolling-out-to-paid-Workspace-users.html) lists AI Pro for Education among eligible editions, but only for US users with EN-US as their language. Rapid Release began 12 October; Scheduled Release starts 26 October. It is the week's second Google feature arriving without a control, after Gemini-app skills.

## Sceptic's Corner

Two announcements this window used the same word. OpenAI's headline was "GPT-6 and Intelligent UI for everyone". Google's page title was "Gemini 4 Argon is now available for everyone". Both are true for a definition of everyone.

Argon in the Gemini app means AI Pro or Ultra; AI Pro costs $19.99 a month ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2026/10/03/gemini-model-limits-oct-26/)). Free personal accounts moved down to Flash-Lite the same week. ChatGPT's free tier gets GPT-6, but on Luna rather than Sol ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/gpt-6-for-everyone/)). The gap between a student who pays and one who doesn't widened while the headlines said the opposite.

Now the strongest case against the obvious alarm. If Strömberg's data is right, the paying student has mostly bought a faster way to skip the thinking. Frontier capability is not the scarce input in a Grade 10 essay. Effort is.

So the equity problem is real, but it isn't really about models. It is about which students have an adult-designed reason to use any model well. A school account is not an equaliser just because it exists. It is one only when the tasks around it require the student to think.
