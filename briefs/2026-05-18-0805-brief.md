# Monday, 18 May 2026

## Top story

**Google's 15 May AI Policy & Guidance Labs note is the first useful Monday signal: the company is moving from tool announcements to implementation-roadmap language.**

Google says its recent AI Policy & Guidance Labs ran in six countries: Brazil, India, Malaysia, Mexico, Spain and Sweden. The useful detail is not the country list. It is the output shape: participants left with formal Position Statements and 12-month implementation roadmaps, and Google says the experience was built to be product-agnostic rather than tied to Gemini or any one platform. ([Google for Education](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-policy-guidance-labs/)).

For international schools, the phrase to keep is **shared language**. Google's own lessons from the labs are exactly where Ed Tech Coaches are often pulled: frameworks are only the beginning; teams need a common vocabulary that bridges technical terminology and pedagogical practice; teachers need to lead AI use rather than merely supervise it. A good artefact is not enough. What matters is whether the artefact becomes a shared operating language, whether teachers can use it under time pressure, and whether leaders repeat it often enough that it stops being wallpaper.

The strongest school-facing question is: **what would a 12-month AI implementation roadmap look like if it had to be useful to teachers by October, not impressive in a folder by August?**

## Other stories

- **OpenAI's 14 May Enterprise/Edu release notes make Codex remote control and access tokens an admin-governance story, not just a coding convenience.** Codex can now be monitored and redirected from the ChatGPT mobile app, while Enterprise workspaces can issue access tokens for trusted non-interactive workflows. The education relevance is indirect but real: long-running AI work is becoming manageable under workspace identity, audit surfaces and admin controls rather than personal-machine bricolage. ([OpenAI release notes](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/10128477-chatgpt-enterprise-edu-release-notes)).

- **OpenAI workspace agents now support eligible Enterprise workspaces with EKM, and the admin surfaces are getting more legible.** The 7 May release note says workspace agents can run repeatable workflows across connected apps, be scheduled, use custom MCP servers, and remain off by default behind admin controls. For schools, the procurement question is no longer "can teachers make clever bots?" but "can the institution govern who can publish agents, what apps they touch, and what evidence admins can inspect afterward?" ([OpenAI release notes](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/10128477-chatgpt-enterprise-edu-release-notes)).

- **Google's April education update is now more relevant after the 15 May policy note: Gemini is becoming an LMS-adjacent layer, not only a chat product.** Google says Gemini is an official AI provider for Moodle's AI placements and that Gemini LTI support for Moodle starts in May, letting educators assign Gems or notebooks directly inside Moodle. Even for schools that do not use Moodle, the pattern matters: model vendors are moving into LMS workflow slots through standards and placements. ([Google education update](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-tools-programs-educators/)).

- **Instructure's current incident page still makes Free-for-Teacher the quiet platform-design lesson.** Instructure says both the 29 April access and the 7 May defacement used one of its Free-for-Teacher accounts; it disabled the second attack after about 10 minutes and says no additional data was exfiltrated in that second attack. It also says Free-for-Teacher is temporarily shut down while it works out a safer return path. ([Instructure incident page](https://www.instructure.com/incident_update)).

- **US House Homeland Security's 11 May letter to Instructure is worth keeping as a governance artefact.** The letter asks for a committee briefing on the breach, including the company's coordination with federal law enforcement and CISA. Even outside the US jurisdiction, the questions are reusable: what did the vendor know, when did it know it, who did it coordinate with, and what should downstream institutions have been told? ([House Homeland Security letter](https://homeland.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/2026.05.11-Homeland-to-Instructure-Holdings-Inc.pdf)).

## Sceptic's Corner

**Policy-to-practice labs are useful; vendor-hosted policy labs still deserve a raised eyebrow.** Google's note is more mature than a product launch because it talks about roadmaps, shared language, teacher leadership and product-agnostic planning. Good. But the sponsor is still a vendor with a product ecosystem to normalize. The school move is not to reject the material. It is to strip it for structure: what facilitation moves helped leaders turn ideals into a 12-month roadmap? what vocabulary actually bridged IT and teaching? what evidence showed teachers, not tools, were leading the change?

**The platform layer is converging from three directions.** Google is entering through classroom workflow and LMS-adjacent placements. OpenAI is entering through governed workspace agents and admin analytics. Toddle is entering through curriculum-planning intimacy. Canvas is the warning case for what happens when a platform is already critical infrastructure and a free/low-friction surface sits too close to institutional trust. The model-comparison question is too small. The better question is: which surfaces become operational infrastructure before schools have named the risk class?

**The implementation lesson keeps echoing.** A framework can be well-written and still die. A vendor can publish responsible language and still leave implementation thin. A school can have strong AI ambitions and still lack a shared operating vocabulary. Monday's useful question is not "which framework should we adopt?" It is: **what repeatable practice makes the framework show up in October decisions?**
