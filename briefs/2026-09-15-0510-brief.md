## Top story

**Google’s education AI menu acquired hard edges today.** A Google Workspace administrator page updated on 10 September says promotional access to presentation generation for Education Fundamentals and Standard begins having limits enforced on 15 September. The same matrix separates entitlements across Fundamentals or Standard, Education Plus or the Teaching and Learning add-on, and the paid Google AI Pro for Education add-on—and explicitly warns that limits inside Workspace apps are separate from limits in the Gemini app ([Google Workspace Admin Help, updated 10 September 2026](https://knowledge.workspace.google.com/admin/generative-ai/workspace-with-gemini/ai-pro-for-education)). That is less glamorous than a model release and more useful to Google schools deciding what staff can actually rely on.

The paid tier is now unusually legible. Google lists AI Pro for Education at US$24 per user monthly on a flexible plan or US$20 on an annual or fixed-term plan. Its stated ceilings include 25 style-matching uses in Docs per month, 40 PDF audio overviews a day and 150 Drive-organising actions a month, 25,000 AI-function uses and 500 build-or-edit actions in Sheets per month, 500 generated slides a month, and a separate set of Vids allowances. Workspace Studio remains promotional until its own limit enforcement begins on 1 October. These are entitlement claims, not service-level guarantees; the page gives no evidence about classroom quality, latency under load or whether a quota resets gracefully in the middle of a task.

For international schools, the immediate work is not to chase the largest number. It is to distinguish three things that staff will otherwise hear as one: a feature appearing in the interface, a feature being licensed for their edition, and a feature carrying enough quota for a repeatable workflow. A pilot should record edition, admin state and actual consumption alongside the instructional result. Otherwise a promising teacher prototype can become either an accidental upsell or a workflow that fails differently across colleagues’ accounts. Today’s deadline makes the matrix worth testing against each school’s tenancy; it does not, by itself, make the paid add-on worth buying.

## Other stories

- **UNESCO has turned AI governance into six separable school decisions.** On 8 September UNESCO opened a global consultation, running through 15 October, around a discussion paper and six commissioned background papers: digital sovereignty, age appropriateness, learner safety, procurement, total cost of ownership and explainability. The framing asks what an education system needs in order to retain agency as AI diffuses, with consultation responses feeding policy briefs planned for Digital Learning Week 2027 ([UNESCO, updated 8 September 2026](https://www.unesco.org/en/digital-education/artificial-intelligence/consultation)). This is a normative package rather than an impact evaluation, and the long policy horizon means schools cannot wait for a finished global answer. Its value for international schools is architectural: the six papers prevent one privacy checklist from masquerading as complete governance. A product may minimize data but still be age-inappropriate; be explainable but unaffordable at scale; or look cheap while shifting review, training and exit costs onto staff. Those are different gates and should remain different rows in any local tool review.

- **Microsoft and two teacher unions have made AI safeguards contract language, not a pledge.** On 9 September Microsoft, the American Federation of Teachers and the United Federation of Teachers announced a National AI Safety & Privacy Standard that US districts can insert into Microsoft customer agreements. The stated protections bar use of student and educator data for model training, sale or repurposing; keep schools in control of data use, retention and deletion; require human oversight; and require clear communication about product changes, risks and controls. The unions say districts can end agreements and seek damages when providers breach the terms ([Microsoft, 9 September 2026](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2026/09/09/aft-uft-and-microsoft-announce-national-ai-safety-privacy-standard-for-schools-to-protect-students-families-and-educators/)). The release is jointly authored by parties promoting their agreement, and it does not show enforcement in practice. Even so, it supplies procurement language stronger than a generic responsible-AI page. For an international school, the transferable question is whether equivalent promises sit inside the signed contract, flow to subprocessors and survive a product rename—not whether the vendor has published reassuring principles.

- **The emerging AI divide may be institutional capacity, not chatbot access.** Chicago Booth Review reported on 11 September that Christopher Campos and John Singleton surveyed roughly 1,200 US principals from December 2025 to February 2026, after invitations went to more than 70,000 public, charter and private schools. Responses were linked to school and neighbourhood data and reweighted nationally; varying the guaranteed participation payment helped the researchers test response-selection effects. Teacher use had risen from about 20% of schools in 2023 to 90% two years later, yet 58% of principals reported no written AI policy and 76% no contract for AI tutoring. Schools serving more disadvantaged communities, along with charter and private schools, scored lower on a seven-measure integration index ([Chicago Booth Review, 11 September 2026](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/ai-inequity-is-developing-schools)). Principal reports are not classroom observations, and US structures do not map cleanly onto international schools. The finding still travels: equal logins do not create equal implementation. Shared routines, protected learning time, policy ownership and technical support may now be the scarce layer.

- **Parents are bringing AI-generated material into special-education meetings.** An EdSurge report on 11 September describes families using chatbots to translate IEP jargon, draft questions, propose home goals and interpret possible accommodations. Educators interviewed saw a real access benefit, especially for parents unfamiliar with specialist language, but also reported submissions stretching to 20 or 30 pages and warned that general chatbots can be inaccurate. The article advises against entering diagnoses or legal documents containing identifiable data into consumer tools and argues for disclosure, joint checking and a collaborative response rather than treating the parent as an adversary ([EdSurge, 11 September 2026](https://www.edsurge.com/news/parents-are-bringing-ai-to-the-iep-meeting-heres-how-teachers-should-respond)). This is practitioner reporting, not a prevalence study, and the legal discussion is US-specific. The operational pattern matters in an international school: AI can lower the language barrier while raising the verification and privacy burden. A humane protocol should accept AI-assisted questions, ask families not to upload identifiable records, and bring every recommendation back to the actual student evidence.

- **OpenAI has packaged the agent harness itself as a managed service.** On 10 September OpenAI released the Agents API in public beta, offering durable sessions, context compaction, tool search, programmatic tool calling, MCP connections and optional parallel subagents. Builders can use an OpenAI-hosted sandbox, their own infrastructure or a partner environment; there is no separate API fee beyond the models and tools consumed. OpenAI also says the underlying Codex harness is open source, while the managed service will keep evolving with model releases ([OpenAI, 10 September 2026](https://openai.com/index/introducing-the-agents-api/)). The performance figures on the page are customer testimonials, not controlled benchmarks, and public beta is not a reliability commitment. For school prototyping, the important change is that a small app can now inherit long-running agency and filesystem or connector access with very little code. That reduces engineering friction and increases the need to inventory tools, permissions, sandbox boundaries, logs and human approval points before an attractive demo is allowed near school data.

## On the radar

- **Connecticut has adopted an AI governance cadence rather than a frozen rulebook.** A resolution adopted on 14 September sets enduring principles for oversight, educational purpose, AI literacy, workforce support, access, privacy and vendor accountability, then adds an annual state report, a rapid-advisory working group and a disruption scenario exercise at least every 18 months ([Connecticut Commission for Educational Technology, 14 September 2026](https://portal.ct.gov/das/commission-for-educational-technology/2026/september-14-2026)). Watch whether the advisory machinery actually reacts faster than ordinary policy cycles. The design is useful precisely because platform changes and security events do not respect annual handbook revisions.

- **The University of Sydney is reportedly moving from local AI tools to campus-wide ChatGPT Edu.** Student newspaper Honi Soit reported on 11 September that a multi-year agreement would give staff access from October, pilot student access in October and expand in Semester 1 of 2027, alongside a $1 million AI-sustainability fund. The university confirmed an enterprise-AI partnership was coming but had not publicly named the vendor at publication time ([Honi Soit, 11 September 2026](https://honisoit.com/2026/09/breaking-usyd-signs-multi-year-partnership-with-openai/)). Watch the formal announcement, data terms, evaluation design and how staff concerns are handled; reported scale is not yet evidence of educational value.

- **Google’s educator training push has a live test on 19 September.** Google announced on 3 September that its free AI Educator Series would add monthly modules and run a ten-hour virtual Badge-a-thon on Saturday, 19 September, with short sessions on administrative automation, Guided Learning and AI-assisted classroom creation ([Google, 3 September 2026; event 19 September](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/new-ai-educator-trainings-september-2026/)). The publication sits outside the normal seven-day daily window; the imminent event is the current development. Watch whether the sessions teach teachers to test outputs and preserve cognitive work, or mainly accelerate feature adoption and badge collection.

## Sceptic's corner

The easy conclusion from the Microsoft-union agreement is that the school-AI safety problem finally has a contract-shaped solution. It is materially better to have enforceable controls over training, retention, deletion, human oversight and product-change notice than to rely on a vendor’s general promises. Procurement teams should borrow the language.

But privacy compliance is not evidence that a product is developmentally appropriate, instructionally useful or worth the workload it creates. Fairplay’s response on 9 September makes that distinction directly: it welcomes data minimisation and the training and advertising bans, while warning that the agreement does not answer whether student-facing generative AI should be used at all ([Fairplay, 9 September 2026](https://fairplayforkids.org/statement-on-aft-uft-deal-with-microsoft/)). UNESCO’s six-paper package arrives at the same problem from another direction by separating learner safety, age, procurement, cost and explainability.

That is the guardrail for international schools. A strong data clause can move a tool from disqualified to eligible for evaluation; it cannot move it from eligible to educationally sound. The next gate still needs a named learning purpose, evidence that the task preserves the thinking students are meant to practise, a workable teacher-review burden, an exit route and a measure that can fail. The staffroom sentence is worth keeping plain: a privacy-respecting tool may still be the wrong tool.
