## Top story

**Gemini's new visual lessons arrive without a separate school switch.** Google's 28 October release introduces immersive explorations: scenes with interactive nodes and guided explanations, covering subjects including the Silk Road, jet engines and lightning. The library is available now on web and mobile; access from inside a Gemini conversation and audio narration are still forthcoming. Google lists Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus for the student hub, and all Workspace customers and personal accounts for the direct Gemini library. Crucially, it says there is no feature-specific admin control or end-user setting; administrators are referred to the broader Gemini app control ([Google Workspace Updates, 28 October](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/immersive-explorations-now-available-in-the-Gemini-app.html)).

That is a useful teaching development and a slightly awkward governance one. A visual explanation can make scale, structure and relationships easier to discuss than another paragraph of chatbot prose. A physics teacher could ask pupils to predict the relevant mechanism before opening a layer, then explain which detail changed their model. A humanities teacher could interrogate what a trade-route scene foregrounds and what it leaves out. The intellectual work comes from those questions; tapping all the nodes is an interface achievement.

The control boundary matters because school approval of an application now includes changes to the learning experiences inside it. There need not be a new app to install or a new procurement conversation before something materially different reaches a class. Ed Tech Coaches therefore need a reasonably current account of what an approved environment can do, alongside the familiar account of who may use it.

Language is another classroom detail hiding in the release: six languages are supported, including English, Indonesian and simplified Chinese; other languages currently receive English. Vietnamese is absent from that supported list. In international schools, a visually accessible resource can still impose a reading-language barrier. The interesting question is whether pupils can explain a relationship afterwards, including without the scene in front of them. Attractive navigation has never been an assessment criterion worth much.

## Other stories

- **Frontline's breach has moved from containment to individual notification.** The school administration supplier began notifying affected people on or about 26 October about an August incident involving Frontline Central and School Health Management. Its notice says a third-party software vulnerability allowed unauthorized access beginning 14 August, with the last activity on 15 August. Depending on the individual, affected records included Social Security numbers, birth dates, driving-licence and passport numbers, other government IDs, Medicaid IDs and student IDs. Frontline says the incident is remediated and offers eligible US adults two years of credit monitoring, with cyber monitoring for minors ([Frontline Education, 26 October](https://www.frontlineeducation.com/news/frontline-education-notice-of-data-breach/)). The new event is the notification, rather than a new attack. The notice does not establish the total number affected or identify the vulnerable product. For schools elsewhere, the transferable issue is what happens after the technical repair: a person's identity data remains exposed even when a server is secure again. Procurement discussions tend to dwell on prevention and uptime; this is the case for giving notification ownership, usable contact records and post-incident support equal space. A vendor can finish its remediation while a family's consequences are only beginning. Those are different clocks, and a reassuring status update usually shows only one.

- **Minnesota supplies a more useful unit of AI policy: the learning process.** In a dated 22 October university Q&A, learning scientist Panayiota Kendeou explains a new expert-consensus report's distinction between initial thinking and subsequent practice: learners should first do the thinking themselves, then use AI for feedback, examples and checking understanding. Younger children require particular care because foundational reading, writing and self-management still need practice ([University of Minnesota, 22 October](https://twin-cities.umn.edu/news-events/talking-ai-and-education-university-minnesota)). The underlying October report maps 65 processes, reaching consensus on 42, and explicitly warns that the empirical literature lacks adequate rigor ([expert-consensus report, October](https://www.cehd.umn.edu/AI-expert-consensus-report-2026)). This is expert judgment organized transparently, rather than a trial demonstrating one intervention's effect. Its practical contribution is a better question than whether AI is allowed for an assignment: which part of the thinking is this particular use replacing? Generating another practice example after an independent attempt and generating the first explanation of an unfamiliar problem have different instructional purposes, even inside the same approved tool. A whole-school policy can acknowledge that difference without creating an encyclopedia of applications. The report earns attention for its granularity; treating its judgments as settled causal evidence would undo that advantage.

- **Anthropic's drug-development results are an evidence lesson before they are a careers slogan.** Its 27 October account describes partnerships with six organizations and work across molecule design, manufacturing documentation and clinical-trial planning. At BigHat Biosciences, scientists and an Anthropic researcher used Claude while the laboratory made and tested about 1,010 antibody-fragment variants over roughly ten weeks. Partway through, concern about the existing stability and clumping measures prompted reanalysis and a revised protocol, which scientists refined and ran. Anthropic emphasizes that the wider work is mostly preclinical ([Anthropic, 27 October](https://www.anthropic.com/research/early-progress-drug-development)). That combination is more educationally useful than a headline about AI discovering cures: a large search space, a physical test, a measurement problem and a human decision about whether the test was meaningful. Biology and chemistry classes can follow the boundary between a plausible design, a laboratory result and an effective treatment in people. Those are three different claims requiring different evidence. There is also a sound careers angle: scientific work includes coordinating experiments and understanding data systems, as well as disciplinary knowledge. This is the vendor's account of collaborative projects, with an obvious interest in demonstrating progress. It is credible material for examining a research workflow; it is not independent proof that its model reliably delivers medicines.

- **Google's latest AI-literacy quest puts the unglamorous work on screen.** Polar Peaks, launched on 26 October with the Stanford Accelerator for Learning, is the third AI Quest for ages 11–14. Pupils investigate a character's memory loss by scanning and mapping neurons, including data-cleaning and model-testing tasks. Google reports more than 400,000 quest completions across the series and localization into ten languages; those are completions, not a demonstrated count of learners or learning gains. Teacher modules for the first two quests are available, while the third quest's training module is due next month ([Google, 26 October](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-quests-polar-peaks/)). The worthwhile distinction is between learning to prompt a chatbot and learning why an AI system can go wrong. Cleaning inputs and testing a model make the second kind of literacy concrete. For middle-school science or digital-literacy teaching, the neuroscience setting may provide a reason to care about the data rather than treating responsible use as a detached list of rules. The debrief is where the lesson earns its place: can a pupil identify a bad input, explain a mistaken output and say what a human should check? A polished quest provides the occasion for that conversation. Completion totals do not answer those questions on its behalf.

## On the radar

- **An AI crawler's name is becoming a disguise.** Cequence's 28 October threat report tracks one actor impersonating about 32 bots associated with providers including OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity, probing roughly 90 paths for configuration files, keys and other secrets. Nearly all requests were blocked or failed in its observed environment ([Cequence, 28 October](https://www.cequence.ai/blog/ai-agent-impersonation)). This is vendor telemetry, not a school breach census. The portable lesson for school web services is nevertheless plain: a request claiming to be a familiar assistant has not thereby proved its identity. The interesting governance boundary is verified identity and permitted behavior, rather than whether an agent's brand sounds respectable.

- **ServiceNow's AI announcement spans today and next year.** The 28 October OpenAI partnership expansion says its MCP plugin is available now, with enhanced capabilities expected by year-end. Voice is expected in the first quarter of 2027, security early access in January and general availability in the second quarter; the Codex workflow integration is expected in 2027 ([OpenAI, 28 October](https://openai.com/index/openai-servicenow-partnership-expansion/)). The design pattern is relevant beyond large enterprises: conversation becomes a place to update operational records, with existing business rules and permissions applied underneath. For schools evaluating workflow assistants, a permission model and a release calendar belong beside the demonstration. Announced integrations have several different tenses.

## Sceptic's corner

The tempting inference from today's Google release is that a visual, interactive AI experience must be more educational than a chatbot answer. That is a hypothesis about learning dressed as a description of an interface.

Google's announcement establishes what users can open, how the scenes unfold and where the controls sit. It supplies no classroom comparison or retention result ([Google Workspace Updates, 28 October](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/immersive-explorations-now-available-in-the-Gemini-app.html)). That omission does not make the feature useless. It means the announcement answers a product question, while the teacher still has a learning question to answer.

Consider two uses of the same lightning scene. In one, pupils browse until the picture feels familiar. In the other, they predict what would change under a different condition, inspect the explanation, and then draw and defend their own account with the screen closed. The second use creates something observable to assess. Its advantage is an instructional argument, not a result demonstrated by this release.

The counterweight to overclaiming should not become an impossible demand that every diagram arrive with its own randomized trial. Teachers reasonably use resources whose value depends on how they are taught. The usable judgment is narrower: give a new resource a defined job, preserve an independent student response, and compare what that response shows against an existing way of teaching the idea. A scene that provokes better explanations has earned attention. A scene that merely provokes longer browsing has earned a usage statistic.
