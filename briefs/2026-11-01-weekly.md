## What Does It All Mean?

Every story above assumes a lever somewhere. So the question worth a Sunday: when a school cannot switch a feature off, what is its AI rule actually made of?

Start with the lever schools think they have. Among American teens who use AI for schoolwork, Common Sense Media found in August that 44% had had an AI tool blocked on a school network or device, and 59% of those simply moved to a personal device, where there is even less support ([Common Sense Media, 18 August](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/press-releases/teens-who-use-ai-for-schoolwork-are-handing-off-critical-thinking-common-sense-media-research-finds)). A network block is a detour, not a wall.

Assessment researchers have been making this case for a while. Thomas Corbin, Phillip Dawson and Danny Liu split institutional responses into discursive changes, which tell students what is allowed, and structural ones, which change how the task itself works. Traffic-light permission schemes, they argue, borrow the look of a real enforcement system without its power, and leaning on rules like these creates what they call an "enforcement illusion" ([Corbin, Dawson and Liu, 2025](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/talk-is-cheap-why-structural-assessment-changes-are-needed-for-a-/)).

The University of Sydney, where Liu works, had already written the idea into policy: from 2025, AI allowed by default except in exams and in-semester tests, then two lanes ([ACS Information Age, December 2024](https://ia.acs.org.au/article/2024/university-of-sydney-to-allow-ai-in-some-assessments.html)). Secured, supervised tasks leave AI to the examiner; everything else is open, with students guided in its use. Its FAQ is blunt that AI use cannot be restricted in assessments not secured face to face, nor reliably detected ([Teaching@Sydney, updated April 2026](https://educational-innovation.sydney.edu.au/teaching@sydney/frequently-asked-questions-about-the-two-lane-approach-to-assessment-in-the-age-of-ai/)).

Even the AI Assessment Scale's own authors agree. Leon Furze, one of them, writes that assessment security is not what the scale aims at; it is a framework to support assessment design, and a colourful handout plus "a hopeful expectation" secures nothing ([Furze, September 2025](https://leonfurze.com/2025/09/23/how-not-to-use-the-aias/)).

That would be bleak if rules kept students honest. They never did. A follow-up study published in February, surveying 4,354 students at six US high schools about eighteen months after ChatGPT's release, found an overall cheating rate of 72.06%, which its authors call consistent with historical baselines; AI availability does not appear to have changed overall prevalence ([Chen et al., February 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-026-10587-1)). The tool changed the method, not the rate. Whatever sets the rate, whether engagement, pressure or a task that feels worth doing, sits upstream of any switch.

So the working answer: an AI rule rests on three layers, in falling order of reliability. First, what is structurally true of the task: done in the room, staged, defended aloud. Second, what the school can actually switch, which is coarse and covers products more than features. Third, what it tells students, which is invaluable for conversation and useless as security.

For a school running an AI use scale, the practical move is small. The scale stays as the shared language. But every task pitched at the no-AI or low-AI end needs a one-line answer to "what makes this true in the room?" If the honest answer is that the student promised, the task is open and should be marked as one.

## Behind You! Behind You!

**New York will treat unverified chatbot users as children.** Governor Hochul signed S.9051B/A.10379C on 28 October. In the coming months, companies may not offer under-18s, or any user not confirmed as an adult, features such as posing as human, claiming a relationship, asking unprompted emotional questions or using a child's private details from earlier chats. The attorney general can seek up to $25,000 per violation ([New York State, 28 October](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-signs-nation-leading-legislation-protect-kids-ai-chatbots-and-ensure-baby)). The state's Business Council had argued that educational tools were swept in ([TechTimes, 29 October](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/329665/20261029/hochul-signs-chatbot-safety-law-that-reaches-chatgpt-claude-not-just-companion-apps.htm)). Watch whether vendors build that default once and ship it everywhere.

**States want proof and can't get it.** SETDA's 2026 State EdTech Trends report, published 29 October from 73 survey responses, found 79% of state leaders naming AI a top technology priority. Yet 52% said their state collects no data on the use or effectiveness of ed-tech products, and 68% said independently verifying vendor claims about AI products is challenging ([GovTech, 29 October](https://www.govtech.com/education/k-12/setda-states-shift-from-ed-tech-adoption-to-accountability)). If American states with procurement offices struggle, an international school is checking alone.

**Half of the US schools and districts surveyed had a cyber incident last year.** The Center for Internet Security's 2026 K-12 report puts it at 51% in the past 12 months, and 66% of districts leave cybersecurity to one or two people ([CIS, 27 October](https://www.cisecurity.org/insights/white-papers/state-k-12-cybersecurity-report-2026)). Phishing or email compromise ranked among the top incidents for 87% of leaders, and the report warns of more convincing AI-enabled phishing ([K-12 Dive, 27 October](https://www.k12dive.com/news/cybersecurity-incidents-hit-over-50-of-schools-and-districts-in-past-year/872783/)). The weak point is staff inboxes, not servers.

**The ed-tech backlash is on the ballot, and it isn't partisan.** Ahead of Tuesday's US midterms, Chalkbeat found a Republican candidate for California governor promising to ban AI in schools on day one. In Texas, the Republican governor pledges less classroom screen time and his Democratic challenger opposes AI for elementary students. Seven of eight San Francisco school board candidates back limits on classroom technology ([Chalkbeat, 26 October](https://www.chalkbeat.org/2026/10/26/backlash-to-ai-screen-time-ed-tech-factors-into-midterm-elections/)). International schools enrolling families from those systems should expect the mood to travel with them.

## Sceptic's Corner

Next time someone says the research shows AI improves learning, ask which research.

Jill Barshay reports 39 meta-analyses on AI in education, every one finding substantial benefits. Patrick O'Neill, a community college professor, audited 14 of them and found serious conceptual and statistical flaws, from hallucinated references to underlying studies citing generative-AI papers published before the technology was public. One, published in a Nature journal in May 2025, has since been retracted ([Hechinger Report, 26 October](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-ai-education-meta-analysis/)).

When O'Neill pooled 44 well-designed experiments, outcomes ranged from very helpful to very harmful, and his preliminary calculations put the average at roughly zero. Susanna Loeb's team gathered almost 900 studies and judged only 20 good enough for its evidence summary. O'Neill's audit is itself a preprint and his numbers are preliminary, so hold him to the same standard.

Two things follow. A literature that is uniformly positive is a warning, not a comfort; real interventions produce null results. An average of roughly zero is not a verdict of harmlessness. It is what you get when good designs and bad ones cancel out, which is why the question is always which use, for whom, checked how.

The line for a staff meeting: there is no effect size for "AI", only for particular uses of it. Anyone quoting one number is quoting a brochure.
