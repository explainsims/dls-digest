## Top story

**The notebook has become a pipeline.** Google’s 7 August Workspace update adds an action that can [automatically feed sources into Gemini Notebooks through Workspace Studio](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/automatically-add-sources-to-your-Gemini-Notebooks-in-Workspace-Studio.html). A scheduled or event-driven workflow can now add text, Drive files, and ordinary web or YouTube links. What was a manually curated research space can therefore become a living collection: a department might add each approved unit guide as it lands in a Drive folder, or keep a policy notebook supplied with the latest published documents.

That sounds like a small automation feature. It is really a change in the object’s nature. A static notebook has a knowable evidence boundary; an automatically replenished one can change underneath the same link. The attraction for international schools is obvious. Curriculum teams, Ed Tech Coaches and operational groups all have collections that are useful precisely because their source material keeps moving. Removing the repetitive upload step could make those collections genuinely maintainable rather than impressive for a fortnight.

The governance catches arrive in the same parcel. Google says the step is available by default wherever administrators already allow Gemini for Workspace steps, and lists Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus among the eligible editions. Its announcement explains how material enters a notebook, but not how a school should approve, deduplicate, retire or audit it. A permissive workflow could steadily accumulate superseded guidance, duplicate files or weak web sources while continuing to present itself as source-grounded.

The rollout began on 6 August and may take up to 15 business days. That creates a useful window for schools to inspect the administrative default, then test one staff-only collection whose source set and owner are explicit. The clever part is not making the pipe run. It is making sure everyone knows what is allowed through it.

## Other stories

- **New Zealand is putting applied AI beside construction and tourism.** The government has [announced nine new industry-led subjects](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/education/902259/nine-new-secondary-school-subjects-announced-by-government) for Years 12 and 13, including *Applied intelligent systems*, *Next-gen manufacturing*, engineering technology, energy and infrastructure, food and fibre systems, health services and care, hospitality, construction, and tourism. They are intended to be available from 2029 within the qualification replacing NCEA. The Ministry’s earlier [industry-led subjects outline](https://tahurangi.education.govt.nz/new-industry-led-subjects) says Industry Skills Boards and the Ministry will develop the material through 2026 and 2027. The important move is not merely adding an AI course. It places intelligent systems inside a family of applied pathways shaped around sectors and occupations, rather than treating AI literacy as a generic digital-citizenship add-on. For schools, the useful question is curricular: where should AI be learned as a tool, where as a system to be understood, and where as part of disciplinary practice? New Zealand’s descriptors and assessment model will matter more than the title. Until those appear, this is a direction of travel, not yet a curriculum to copy.

- **Back-to-school AI development is becoming a marketplace.** Three programmes in one week show how different the offers now are. OpenAI’s [AI Skills Jam for K–12 educators](https://openai.com/index/k-12-educators-practical-skills/) ran its Utah stop on 7 August after workshops focused on planning, communication and administration. Teachers College, Columbia University ran a [seven-hour builder day](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/events/info/the-ai-frontier-day-2-the-next-frontier-making-ai-work-for-your-teaching-practice-11470344/) spanning Google Gems, custom GPTs, Claude Projects and multi-step workflows. California’s Association of Technology and Digital Educators used a two-day [AI Design Sprint](https://catdc.org/inspire_events/ai-design-sprint/) to move faculty teams from mission and values to an implementation plan. Free vendor-sponsored access, university continuing education and paid school-team design are solving different problems, but they increasingly compete under the same professional-learning label. Schools should judge them by transfer: whether a teacher leaves able to make a defensible classroom decision, not merely operate the sponsoring product or collect a certificate. Procurement, pedagogy and professional development are starting to blur; the evaluation rubric should not.

- **The International Olympiad in AI has finished its third edition before most schools have settled an AI curriculum.** The [International Olympiad in Artificial Intelligence](https://ioai-official.org/) ran in Astana from 2 to 8 August. Its existence is the noteworthy item, even before the organisers publish a complete, auditable results set. AI now has an international student-competition infrastructure alongside older mathematics, physics and informatics traditions, with regional selection systems growing around it. That creates a useful tension for schools. Olympiads can expose ambitious students to serious modelling, coding and problem solving; they can also narrow a new subject prematurely around what is easy to rank under competition conditions. The right response is neither to dismiss the contest nor to mistake it for the curriculum. Released tasks will show whether the competition rewards data reasoning, model evaluation and explanation, or mainly specialist technical performance. Those tasks will be a sharper signal than promotional participation figures. A healthy school pathway should leave room for both technically advanced competitors and students learning the civic, creative and disciplinary uses of AI.

- **San Diego is reducing school-issued screen time as well as policing phones.** Changes taking effect today in San Diego Unified prohibit general YouTube access on one-to-one devices unless an educator enables it for a specific learning outcome, prohibit non-instructional gaming, and remove computer carts from transitional-kindergarten classrooms while preserving access required by individual plans, accommodations or language needs. The district’s [screen-time announcement](https://www.sandiegounified.org/about/newscenter/all_news/action_to_reduce_screen_time) is useful because it does not frame the problem solely as student distraction. It also asks what schools themselves require through platforms and device routines. A phone restriction can leave the instructional screen burden untouched if every lesson still assumes a laptop, multiple tabs and constant digital submission. Equally, an analogue quota can become theatre if it ignores accessibility and the occasions when a device genuinely improves feedback, creation or collaboration. The stronger audit asks what the technology is doing in each learning sequence and whether a lower-friction medium would do it better. San Diego’s year-long effort will be worth watching for teacher workload, annual software reviews, accessibility practice and evidence beyond parent approval.

## On the radar

- The [TCU International e-Learning Conference](https://iec.thaicyberu.go.th/) closed in Bangkok on 7 August under the theme *Resilient Minds: AI and Online Learning for Uncertain Times*. The programme language is broad; the useful test is whether proceedings or presentation materials turn that theme into implementation evidence. Work on learner resilience should measure more than platform access or satisfaction and distinguish emergency continuity from good everyday learning design. Regional evidence with actual institutional constraints would be considerably more useful to international schools than another global AI principles deck.

- The [International Olympiad in Informatics 2026](https://ioi2026.uz/) began in Tashkent on 9 August and runs to 16 August. Its problems and results will provide a clean view of what the world’s strongest school-age programmers are being asked to do at the same moment generative systems can produce plausible code on demand. The educational value lies in the task design and verification practices, not in another humans-versus-models headline. Watch how explanation, testing and originality are made visible, and whether the public task archive offers useful extension material without importing the competition’s selection culture wholesale.

- Hong Kong’s Education Bureau begins a new round of [AI Leadership strategic-planning sessions](https://applications.edb.gov.hk/circular/upload/EDBCM/EDBCM26107E.pdf) for primary leaders on 24 August, followed by secondary sessions and September reruns. The circular explicitly connects AI literacy to the school planning–implementation–evaluation cycle, rather than offering isolated tool training. If examples or planning artefacts are released, they could give international schools a practical regional comparator for moving AI work from enthusiastic pilots into an annual school-improvement process, especially if the materials show what leaders are expected to measure after the initial training phase.

## Sceptic's corner

Source-grounded AI acquires a halo it has not earned. Attach a notebook to a Drive folder, schedule fresh inputs, and it is tempting to call the result current, controlled and therefore trustworthy. Only the first adjective follows automatically.

Google’s new Studio action makes ingestion easier; its announcement does not promise judgement. A workflow cannot tell that a policy has been superseded unless someone designs a retirement rule. It cannot know that two near-identical files encode materially different approvals. A web or YouTube link can be relevant without being authoritative. Even a perfectly permissioned Drive source may be inappropriate for the audience now querying the notebook. Automation moves these problems from an occasional upload decision to a recurring background process, which makes quiet failure more likely, not less.

There is also a user-interface problem. A stable notebook link suggests a stable object. If the evidence underneath changes overnight, two colleagues can ask the same question on different days and receive different grounded answers without realising the source set moved. That is not necessarily bad; it is unmanaged versioning.

The sensible school pattern is to automate intake, not canon. Every living notebook needs a named collection owner, an approved class of sources, a visible review date, a retirement rule and a small set of known-good and known-bad questions after each refresh. Until those controls exist, the manual upload is not pointless friction. It is the moment someone is forced to decide what counts.
