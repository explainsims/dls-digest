## Top story

**A better-looking answer is not necessarily evidence of learning.** The American Psychological Association published a ten-recommendation report yesterday that puts a useful distinction at the centre of education-technology decisions: attention inside a product is not the same as learning, and improved performance while a tool is present is not the same as a capability a student retains. Covering learners aged 5–18 and technologies from games and tutoring systems to generative AI, the report argues that transfer beyond the app or screen is the stronger test. It places responsibility on both developers and adopting institutions to produce credible, preferably independent evidence rather than treating clicks, time-on-task, rewards or a polished final product as proof ([American Psychological Association, 3 September 2026](https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/children-adolescent-learning-educational-technology)).

The AI recommendation is neither prohibition nor cheerful adoption. Students should learn to interrogate a chatbot’s assumptions, find gaps in its reasoning, compare its response with other sources and test how the answer changes under different conditions. Adult involvement remains part of implementation. That is more demanding than adding an AI-literacy slide before allowing a tool: it makes the act of challenging the system part of the learning design.

For international schools, the report supplies a procurement and classroom test that can travel across products. Ask a vendor what students can do unaided after the intervention, then ask to see the design, comparison and follow-up interval behind the answer. In a classroom trial, capture an AI-assisted performance and a later AI-free transfer task; do not infer the second from the first. A near-transfer task can test whether a method travels to a new example; a delayed task can test whether it lasts. Neither needs to become an exam or surveillance exercise. The same logic applies to teacher-facing tools: minutes saved and resources produced matter operationally, but they do not establish better instruction. The report’s sharpest contribution is not another list of risks. It is a change of outcome variable—from activity and artefact quality to capability that survives when the software leaves the room.

## Other stories

- **Gemini Notebook has gained audit logs, but its content still has no regional home.** From 3 September, eligible Workspace administrators can inspect Notebook events in the audit and investigation tools, including visibility, user identity, IP address and resource context; logs can also be exported to BigQuery once that export is enabled. Google says log storage follows normal Workspace regional routing, while notebooks, sources and chat histories are stored globally and do not currently support data regionalisation ([Google Workspace Updates, 3 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/introducing-comprehensive-audit-logs-for-Gemini-Notebook-in-the-Workspace-Admin-console.html)). This closes part of the observability gap, not the governance question. Before wider use, schools should confirm which licences expose the events, run a benign notebook through create/share/access/delete scenarios, record what the logs actually show, and separate audit-location assurances from content-location assurances. Retention and deletion visibility also need testing; the announcement names event categories, not a complete evidentiary guarantee. A log can help reconstruct access; it cannot retroactively make a prohibited source appropriate to upload.

- **Google’s newest teacher training teaches automation before it teaches schools how to govern the automation.** The free Google AI Educator Series now promises new modules on the first Wednesday of each month, with individual badge sessions taking less than 15 minutes. Its latest examples include automating missed-assignment emails to parents, using Guided Learning for Socratic problem solving, Deep Research for inquiry and an “AI Quest” for game-based learning. A ten-hour virtual badge event is scheduled for 19 September, with 30-minute blocks and ISTE-aligned badges ([Google, 3 September 2026](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/new-ai-educator-trainings-september-2026/)). This is useful, teacher-sized PD and also a vendor adoption funnel. Ed Tech Coaches adapting a module should add a local second half: approved data, human review, failure rehearsal, evidence of student learning and a shutdown path. A badge shows that someone completed Google’s sequence; it does not show that the resulting classroom use fits school policy or survives an AI-free transfer test.

- **A new meta-analysis reports large effects, with heterogeneity doing most of the honest talking.** Researchers synthesised 215 independent effect sizes—172 for achievement and 43 for motivation—and report a random-effects estimate of *d* = 0.80 for achievement (95% CI 0.68–0.91) and *d* = 0.64 for motivation (95% CI 0.46–0.82). Effects varied by subject, intervention duration, educational level and AI-tool type, and the authors conclude that pedagogical design and implementation conditions matter ([Studies in Educational Evaluation, September 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.stueduc.2026.101651)). Those pooled numbers are striking, but “AI applications” is too broad to be an intervention a school can buy or reproduce. Without the full-text coding, study quality, publication-bias tests and outcome timing in hand, this is a map to interrogate rather than a verdict. The practical use is to locate the subgroups closest to an intended trial, then read those underlying studies and look specifically for delayed, unaided measures.

- **A US research grant has written the evaluation plan before claiming the classroom result.** The Institute of Education Sciences lists a $3,638,543, three-year award to MIT to develop Collaborative Artificial Intelligence for Learning, a tool intended to support project-based learning through driving questions, assessment and collaborative-work management. The programme starts with prototype interaction data, proceeds through preliminary implementation, then plans an efficacy comparison across six varied high schools. Measures include classroom observation, course grades, collaboration and critical-thinking rubrics; RAND will evaluate the work, and feasibility, cost and cost-effectiveness are included throughout ([Institute of Education Sciences, 2026 award; period begins 1 September](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/ai-supported-project-based-learning)). There are no outcomes yet. That is precisely why it belongs here: the public record names constructs, comparison, evaluator and costs before the product story hardens. Schools can borrow that order on a much smaller scale—define observable collaboration and thinking before switching on a tool.

- **Pearson has bundled four assessment functions into one platform and bundled the evidence claims with the sales copy.** Navvy+, launched yesterday for US PreK–12 schools, combines formative assessment, universal screening, progress monitoring and instructional resources behind one sign-on. Pearson cites a commissioned survey in which 65% of educators called pinpointing student needs very important while 38% strongly agreed their current tools do so ([Pearson, 3 September 2026](https://plc.pearson.com/en-GB/news-and-insights/news/pearson-launches-navvy-give-prek-12-educators-clearer-more-complete-picture)). The release also links a 17-page impact evaluation: an opt-in Arizona implementation used propensity-score matching, and its focal threshold comparison matched 193 students who used Navvy across at least two-thirds of available standards with 193 non-users, reporting Hedges’ *g* = 0.245 and an improvement index of 9.7 ([Pearson impact evaluation, April 2026](https://www.pearsonassessments.com/content/dam/school/global/clinical/us/assets/navvy/az-navvy-report-2024-2025.pdf)). That is bounded vendor-authored quasi-experimental evidence, not an independent causal verdict; participation and treatment intensity were not random. The architecture is still worth noticing: consolidation can reduce duplicate rostering and fragmented teacher views, but it can also create a single high-stakes profile spanning screening, intervention and daily instruction. Any international-school review would need independent replication plus data-flow, interoperability, export and deletion evidence alongside psychometrics.

## On the radar

- **UNESCO’s Digital Learning Week begins on 8 September with “Facts | Frictions | Frontiers” as its organising frame.** The programme runs to 11 September and names synthetic knowledge, “ghost learners”, agentic systems, public-interest AI, digital sovereignty and analogue approaches among its themes ([UNESCO, accessed 4 September 2026](https://www.unesco.org/en/weeks/digital-learning)). Watch the promised ministerial statement and practical resources, especially whether “human agency” becomes an auditable design requirement or stays conference language. For Asia-facing schools, any treatment of sovereignty should be tested against cross-border cloud storage rather than read as an abstract national-policy debate.

- **UNICEF has held a “From Promise to Proof” education session; the recording is now the useful artefact.** Yesterday’s Executive Board programme put UNICEF, Estonia, Egypt, Education International, Google, Stanford and youth advocates on one panel, and links the video beside its agenda and Digital Education Strategy ([UNICEF, 3 September 2026](https://www.unicef.org/executiveboard/special-focus-session-innovation-education-2026)). The mix makes disagreement more valuable than consensus: look for explicit definitions of proof, who bears the evaluation cost and what youth participants say should count as harm. A panel title is not an evidence framework; the test is whether the promised proof becomes measures, thresholds and public follow-up.

- **Kahoot has put both less screen and more AI into the same back-to-school release.** New modes let a class answer through one shared display, while a ChatGPT app turns PDFs, notes and articles into editable Kahoot sessions. The company also says administrators can control AI features and that it is testing the UK Department for Education’s AI Education Content Store ([Kahoot, 3 September 2026](https://kahoot.com/press/2026/09/03/kahoot-launches-new-experiences-to-boost-engagement-and-help-improve-learning-outcomes-this-back-to-school-season/)). Watch whether generated-question provenance and teacher review are visible in the finished activity; “device-free” changes the interface, not the evidence standard.

## Sceptic's corner

The tempting synthesis this morning is that the APA has finally supplied the anti-hype test: ignore engagement, require AI-free transfer, and the procurement problem is solved. That would turn a valuable distinction into another blunt metric.

Engagement is not sufficient for learning, but it is often necessary for practice to happen at all. Transfer tasks are stronger than clicks, yet a badly chosen transfer task can miss motivation, collaboration, accessibility or the slow formation of disciplinary habits. Even the new meta-analysis reports that effects vary with subject, duration, level and tool type ([Studies in Educational Evaluation, September 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.stueduc.2026.101651)). The IES project correspondingly plans classroom observation, grades, collaboration, critical thinking, feasibility and cost rather than betting everything on one score ([Institute of Education Sciences, 2026](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/ai-supported-project-based-learning)).

So do not replace the vendor’s engagement dashboard with a favourite transfer quiz and call the work scientific. Start with the capability the lesson is meant to build, choose an unaided measure that genuinely samples it, then add the human and operational outcomes that could reveal benefit or harm. Record who benefits, who opts out, whether students can explain their approach, and what teachers must do to make the intervention work; averages can conceal all four. The practical line is: polished work is not proof of learning—but neither is one delayed test the whole of education.
