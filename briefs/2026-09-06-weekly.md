# Week ending Sunday, 6 September 2026

## The Week That Was

September opened with a small institutional argument about who gets to press the button. New York City’s 2026–27 guidance restricts student-facing generative AI through Grade 8 and confines high-school use to approved purposes, while preserving necessary accessibility support ([NYC Public Schools, current guidance](https://www.schools.nyc.gov/about-us/policies/guidance-on-artificial-intelligence)). Google announced automation steps that will move and copy Drive files and reply in email and Chat, with administrator controls and approval requirements for possible external sharing ([Google Workspace Updates, 2 September](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/automate-drive-gmail-and-google-chat-actions-with-new-steps-in-Workspace-Studio.html)). These are different responses to the same awkward development: software is becoming capable of acting before institutions have settled what a meaningful human decision looks like. The interesting contest is between the speed of execution and the quality of the pause before it.

## What Does It All Mean?

A teacher is shown a generated message about a struggling student. The details look plausible, the tone is sympathetic and the system wants approval. There is a human in the loop. Is there judgement in it?

That question has an older research history than the chatbot boom. In a 2021 experiment with 199 participants, Zana Buçinca and colleagues tested designs that made people engage more deliberately with AI advice. These reduced acceptance of wrong recommendations compared with straightforward explanations. The catch was beautifully inconvenient: the designs that reduced overreliance most received the least favourable subjective ratings. Benefits also favoured participants more motivated to think effortfully ([Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos, 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09692)). A reassuring explanation and a pleasant experience were poor substitutes for checking.

There is a complementary finding. Across five studies involving 731 participants, Helena Vasconcelos and colleagues used a maze task with simulated AI to vary the costs and benefits of verification. Task difficulty, explanation difficulty and incentives affected overreliance. Their argument is that people make strategic decisions about whether checking is worth the effort; an explanation helps when it actually makes verification cheaper ([Vasconcelos and colleagues, revised January 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.06823)). Neither study establishes what teachers will do with contemporary school agents. Together, they provide a better hypothesis than telling everyone to remain vigilant.

The school implication is that review has to be designed as work. A generated parent message should arrive with the relevant source record, the intended recipient, the proposed claim and the action awaiting approval. A reviewer should be able to correct, defer or reject it without reconstructing an entire conversation. These are proposed design requirements, not demonstrated findings from those experiments. Their purpose is to spend professional attention on the decision rather than on hunting for its ingredients.

This also changes the economics. A product that saves drafting time but creates a queue of difficult approvals may have moved the workload. The pilot should count review minutes, detected errors, mistaken approvals and recovery effort alongside production speed. There is no universal acceptable number: a classroom resource draft and a message about a student’s support needs deserve different thresholds.

For international schools, the strongest small trial would test the reviewer as well as the generator. Use fictional cases, include plausible errors, and see whether staff can identify and stop them under ordinary working conditions. Make the evidence easy to reach, then test again. The ambition is not maximum friction. It is to make the consequential choice visible, affordable and genuinely optional. An approval button earns its place only when someone can explain what they were able to check before pressing it.

## Behind You! Behind You!

**The quieter school-AI framework came from Beijing.** UNESCO IITE’s 3 September report describes the Global Smart School Initiative launched at the 18–20 August Global Smart Education Conference. Its five pillars span learning experiences, teaching, teacher development, sustainable school planning and home–school–community collaboration ([UNESCO IITE, 3 September](https://iite.unesco.org/news/unesco-iite-co-organised-2026-global-smart-education-conference-in-beijing/)). The event is older; the report is this week’s pickup. The interesting choice is to make organisational development part of the frame. The account describes an initiative, not evidence that participating schools improved.

**Cross-platform calls are reaching more room hardware.** Google’s 2 September announcement extends Meet–Teams interoperability to Android/AOSP devices in Early Preview, with Google’s end-user rollout limited to Rapid Release domains and expected to finish by 7 September. The feature is on by default and can be disabled by organisational unit ([Google Workspace Updates, 2 September](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/new-built-in-interoperability-between-Google-Meet-and-Microsoft-Teams-on-Android-AOSP-devices-now-in-Early-Preview.html)). For schools hosting remote colleagues or external specialists, the useful test is whether a real guest can join with the required controls. Preview availability is not fleet-wide readiness.

## Sceptic's Corner

The fashionable reassurance is *human oversight*. It fits neatly into a product slide, a policy paragraph and a procurement response. It can also mean that a busy employee receives the machine’s decision after the machine has done everything necessary to make disagreement inconvenient.

The answer is not to require a second adult signature on every generated worksheet. That would make caution expensive enough to be quietly abandoned. The verification-cost experiments suggest a more useful direction: improve the evidence and the decision interface so that scrutiny becomes easier ([Vasconcelos and colleagues, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.06823)). Putting a warning above an opaque answer does not do that work.

Nor should schools confuse disagreement with success. A reviewer who rejects accurate suggestions indiscriminately is wasting the tool; one who approves everything is decorating it. An evaluation needs both correct and incorrect cases, and it needs to record whether the human made the combined system better. The benchmark cannot be satisfaction alone: the 2021 experiment found that effective interventions could be less liked ([Buçinca and colleagues, 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09692)). That is a reason to measure actual decisions, not to dismiss complaints about cumbersome design.

Finally, the person reviewing needs authority to stop the work. If the queue must be cleared before the end of the day, the sources are inaccessible and rejection requires a manager’s explanation, the institution has designed compliance with the machine. Calling the employee accountable does not repair that.

The staffroom line this Sunday: human oversight is a working condition, not a checkbox. Give people the evidence, time and authority to disagree—or stop claiming their presence makes the system safe.
