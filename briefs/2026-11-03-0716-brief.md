## Top story

**A teacher-feedback agent improved practice, with a boundary worth keeping.** A study published on 2 November in *Educational Technology & Society* reports a semester-long randomised trial with 30 secondary teachers and 240 classroom videos. An agent combining a language model with retrieved material supplied formative feedback on how faithfully teachers implemented their instructional designs. The abstract reports greater gains in implementation fidelity and overall teaching performance than in the comparison group. Exploratory associations were clearer for classroom management and a supportive climate than for cognitive activation: the work of getting pupils to think deeply ([Du and colleagues, 2 November](https://www.j-ets.net/collection/forthcoming-articles/30_4)).

That distinction deserves more attention than the headline improvement. Feedback about whether a lesson followed its design can be specific, frequent and useful. It can help a teacher notice a missing step or make participation more orderly. But a lesson can be beautifully executed while asking pupils to do very little intellectual work. A system that checks the script and a colleague who challenges the script are offering different kinds of help. The useful professional-development question is which kind a teacher needs at that moment, rather than whether one source of feedback can replace the other.

For international schools, the practical reading is to separate a coaching tool's claims before evaluating it. Does it recognise an observable routine accurately? Does its advice improve the next lesson? Does that lesson leave pupils better able to explain, transfer or challenge an idea? Those are successive questions, and success on the first does not answer the third. Classroom video also creates its own consent and data-handling questions; a promising abstract does not settle them.

This is a small trial, and this account is limited to the published abstract, not an independent examination of its full methods or effects. The journal assigns the article to an October 2027 issue but explicitly records publication yesterday. Its value today is a sharper boundary for an interesting result: feedback that improves enactment still needs a human conversation about what is worth enacting.

## Other stories

- **McGraw Hill is taking licensed course material into Copilot, with a limited beta still ahead.** The publisher's 29 October announcement says customers with Microsoft 365 Copilot licences will be able to summarise, translate and adapt the McGraw Hill material they license, producing study aids and lesson resources through a plugin using Model Context Protocol. A limited beta begins in early 2027; this is an announced route to access, not a classroom feature available today ([McGraw Hill, 29 October](https://investors.mheducation.com/news/news-details/2026/McGraw-Hill-Announces-Agentic-AI-Education-Plugin-for-Microsoft-Copilot/default.aspx)). The strategic move is more interesting than another assistant button: the publisher wants its paid library to travel into the interface where teachers already work, while keeping a commercial boundary around the material. For international schools, that adds a question to the textbook comparison. Which useful functions come with the content licence, and which depend on a second platform's subscription? Grounding an answer in a licensed book can make its provenance easier to inspect; it does not guarantee that the adaptation preserves the intended difficulty or assessment demand. A teacher still needs to compare the generated resource with the original and with the learner's task. The content may have a respected publisher behind it. The particular worksheet has still just been made.

- **Google's new African learning hub treats connectivity as part of the teaching design.** Launched on 2 November, Skilling Africa with Google brings together more than 15 training initiatives, with a target of reaching three million people by 2030. Google describes short lessons that can be downloaded and completed offline, a low-bandwidth mobile design, and SCOUT, an AI study companion offering answers and study guidance. The service is free; the reach figure is a target, not a completed training outcome ([Google Africa, 2 November](https://blog.google/intl/en-africa/unlocking-africas-ai-potential-introducing-skilling-africa-with-google/)). The useful design lesson travels further than the launch market. An online course that assumes continuous, comfortable access has already selected who can finish it. An international school may have excellent campus Wi-Fi while its families still face uneven home connectivity, shared devices or awkward mobile-only tasks. That makes a small downloadable activity a substantive accessibility choice. The companion is the part that will attract the demonstration; the ability to keep learning when it is unavailable may matter more. Nothing in the announcement establishes the quality of SCOUT's feedback or the effect of its streaks and rewards on learning. Those need their own evaluation. Access, sustained participation and worthwhile learning are three separate achievements, even when a product page puts them in one paragraph.

- **Microsoft is testing workplace assistants against invented organisations rather than borrowed customer records.** Its 30 October research account describes synthetic environments for pharmaceuticals, capital markets and software work, built using public signals from occupational data, job advertisements and company filings. The environments include evolving communications and projects, allowing questions about changing decisions rather than isolated documents. Microsoft reports a 39% improvement in an internal measure of synthetic-data quality compared with its initial generation approach. That is not a 39% improvement in Copilot's accuracy, and the account is a vendor description of its evaluation infrastructure ([Microsoft Research, 30 October](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/from-synthetic-enterprise-data-evaluations-to-real-microsoft-copilot-ai-confidence/)). Schools can borrow the principle without imitating the scale. A fictional pupil's record can contain an outdated accommodation, a later correction and two similarly named activities; an assistant then has to find the authoritative current answer. A clean demonstration with one immaculate document never asks that question. Synthetic data keeps real children out of an early experiment, but it is useful only if the invented case contains the awkwardness the system must handle. Otherwise the exercise measures how well an assistant navigates the world its designers wish they had. The valuable test is a realistic conflict with a known answer, followed by a clear account of what the assistant got wrong.

- **A small journalism study offers a better discussion prompt than another declaration that students value critical thinking.** Kentucky State University's 2 November account of David Shabazz's preliminary qualitative work describes 17 college students using AI for reporting tasks. Fifteen saw it as an assistant rather than an author, 14 emphasised verification, and 13 stressed human judgment and authorship. The research was presented on 22 September; yesterday's university report is the new publication, not a newly completed experiment ([Kentucky State University, 2 November](https://www.kysu.edu/news/2026/11/student-perspectives-on-ai-inform-kentucky-state-professors-journalism-research.php)). These are participants' perspectives, not measured proof that they reliably checked their sources. That limit makes the teaching opportunity clearer. Ask a learner to identify one suggestion they rejected, the evidence that changed their mind and what they did next. The answer reveals more about editorial responsibility than agreement with a principle almost nobody would oppose. Journalism also keeps the audience in view: a plausible claim can harm a reader even when the writer had no intention to mislead. That is a useful bridge from classroom citation rules to a public responsibility. Seventeen undergraduates cannot speak for school students generally, but their language gives teachers a concrete starting point for asking what authorship requires after a machine has helped with the draft.

## On the radar

- **Chatbot engagement is beginning to be described through the conversation rather than the click count.** Another ET&S article published on 2 November examines 29 graduate students using two chatbot activities, one built around open discussion and another around structured tutoring. Its exploratory profiles describe different engagement patterns, not general learner types or evidence that a chatbot caused better learning ([Che and colleagues, 2 November](https://www.j-ets.net/collection/forthcoming-articles/30_4)). The useful question for schools is whether a learner challenges, revises and explains during an exchange. A long conversation can contain very little of that. The profiles are hypotheses to investigate, not labels to attach permanently to children.

- **EDUtech Asia's immediate value is the questions its demonstrations must survive.** The long-advertised Singapore event reaches its workshop day today, 3 November, ahead of the 4–5 November conference and exhibition. The organiser distinguishes the paid conference from the free, registered expo and says sessions will not be recorded ([Terrapinn, current event details](https://www.terrapinn.com/exhibition/edutech-asia/frequently-asked-questions.stm)). Watch for evidence that travels beyond a successful demonstration: who was taught, what they could subsequently do, and what staff had to maintain. A regional showcase can expose useful approaches and common implementation problems. A crowded stand is a measure of attention; the learning claim still needs a separate denominator.

## Sceptic's corner

The attractive claim is that more feedback must mean better teaching. AI can make feedback abundant, immediate and consistently attached to a rubric. All three properties can help. None establishes that the rubric is asking the most important question.

A teacher may receive precise advice about the visibility of an objective, the sequence of activities and the distribution of participation. Those are observable features with genuine classroom value. The uncomfortable follow-up is whether a pupil encountered an idea difficult enough to change their understanding. A routine can become more fluent while the intellectual demand remains comfortably low. Rewarding what is easy to recognise can gradually narrow what teachers feel permitted to attempt.

Today's trial is interesting precisely because its abstract distinguishes aspects of practice instead of treating a single improvement score as the entire result ([Du and colleagues, 2 November](https://www.j-ets.net/collection/forthcoming-articles/30_4)). It does not justify dismissing automated feedback. It suggests giving that feedback a bounded job and keeping the wider judgment open.

For coaching, a useful division of labour is to let a tool help identify a pattern, then ask the teacher what the pattern means and what alternative might serve the class better. Sometimes a departure from the plan is the best decision of the lesson. A system trained to reward fidelity needs room for that possibility. The standard should be a more thoughtful next lesson, with pupils doing worthwhile thinking, rather than a teacher becoming easier for a machine to score.
