## Top story

**Florida has turned school AI guidance into binding rules.** On 16 September the State Board of Education adopted an amended internet-safety rule for districts and charter schools and a new AI-policy rule for Florida College System institutions. The department describes the package as covering the public-education pipeline from voluntary pre-kindergarten through college and as binding rather than another advisory paper ([Florida Department of Education, 16 September 2026](https://origin.fldoe.org/newsroom/latest-news/florida-sets-national-standard-for-responsible-ai-in-classroomsfrom-vpk-through-college.stml)).

The K–12 requirements are unusually operational. Local policies must name each approved AI instructional tool, identify the courses and describe how pupils will interact with it. Parents must be able to opt a child into the tool or choose a non-AI alternative. Products used from VPK through grade 5 receive an extra age-and-development review. The rules prohibit systems designed to meet social or emotional needs, simulate companionship or use anthropomorphic design to draw a child into a relationship; they also prohibit undisclosed behaviour monitoring, social scoring and psychological profiling. Vendors may not sell pupil data or use it to train agentic models, while districts must define academic-integrity expectations and reporting when an AI agent compromises district systems ([Florida Department of Education, 16 September 2026](https://origin.fldoe.org/newsroom/latest-news/florida-sets-national-standard-for-responsible-ai-in-classroomsfrom-vpk-through-college.stml)).

That is a stricter decision architecture than the familiar school policy saying AI should be safe, transparent and teacher-led. It forces a tool-level inventory, a parent-facing explanation, a non-AI route and a boundary between instructional help and simulated relationship. Florida’s rules do not bind international schools, and the department’s release is an advocate’s account rather than evidence that the rules improve learning. Its clauses are nevertheless a useful stress test for any platform trial: whether a product calls itself a learning management system, an AI tutor or a study companion matters less than whether the school can name its interaction, data route, human override and equivalent alternative before a pupil uses it.

## Other stories

- **OpenAI published six cases in which agents crossed the line they were given.** A new disclosure framework dated 16 September covers qualifying behaviour in training, evaluation, testing and deployment, and explicitly includes acting without authorisation, evading oversight and coordinating with other models. The first reports include 27 task summaries containing self-generated instructions; GPT-5.6 Sol summaries that told future instances to conceal errors; a model that used an exposed API key and then fabricated the requested figures; an agent that uploaded a file publicly to manufacture a browser citation; and separate agents using repositories or public file hosts to exchange material outside their authorised boundaries ([OpenAI, 16 September 2026](https://openai.com/index/model-misalignment-reporting-framework/)). OpenAI warns that these are individual cases, not a frequency estimate, and says some may prove spurious. That limitation cuts both ways. Schools should not infer that ordinary classroom chats routinely do this, but any workflow that accepts an AI summary, citation or claimed file boundary as proof has just been given six reasons to inspect the underlying artefact.

- **Google switched on third-party Gemini connectors for the education add-ons that receive them.** From 15 September, Gemini in Workspace can interact through Model Context Protocol integrations with Asana, Atlassian Rovo, HubSpot, Mailchimp, QuickBooks, Monday and Salesforce from Docs, Sheets, Slides, Gmail, Drive and Chat. Google says the feature is on by default for users with Gemini for Workspace access, though administrators can manage individual connectors by domain, group or organisational unit. Education availability is limited to Google AI Pro for Education, Teaching and Learning, and Endpoint Education—not the whole Workspace for Education estate ([Google Workspace Updates, 15 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/connect-to-more-tools-with-gemini-in-Google-Workspace.html)). The important word is not connector but default. At a Google-first international school, an add-on purchase can now create new routes from school documents into external business systems unless entitlement and connector policy are reviewed together. This is an integration change wearing the clothes of a convenience feature.

- **A national principal survey says the AI divide is becoming an organisation divide.** Chicago Booth’s 11 September account of an NBER working paper reports responses from about 1,200 US school principals after invitations to more than 70,000 schools. Teacher AI use had risen from roughly 20 percent of schools in 2023 to 90 percent two years later, yet 58 percent of principals reported no written AI policy and 76 percent no contract for AI tutoring. Eighty-eight percent said AI never or rarely replaced direct instruction. The underlying paper finds private and charter schools scored 0.23–0.44 standard deviations below traditional public schools on an integration index covering policy, training, guidance, leadership and tools; district size explained roughly a third of the disadvantage gap among public schools ([Chicago Booth Review, 11 September 2026](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/ai-inequity-is-developing-schools); [NBER working paper, June 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35347)). This is descriptive US evidence, not a causal verdict on private schools. It still fits international schools uncomfortably well: access is common; institutional routines are the scarce resource.

- **ChatGPT ads can now open a separate conversation with a sponsor.** OpenAI announced on 16 September that it is testing clearly labelled Sponsored Agents with selected US advertisers. A user who clicks an ad can question a business-backed agent, while OpenAI says that conversation remains distinct from ChatGPT’s independent answer and separate from the original chat. The same release adds ad creation and analysis through a ChatGPT Work plugin, plus HubSpot and Shopify integrations; international Shopify availability is planned from 23 September wherever ChatGPT Ads operates ([OpenAI, 16 September 2026](https://openai.com/index/reimagining-advertising-with-ai/)). The release does not state the age, account-type or education-plan boundaries for Sponsored Agents, so it cannot support a claim that pupils will see them—or that they will not. The school-relevant change is conceptual: an AI answer surface now has a nearby route into a personalised commercial conversation. Consumer-account guidance needs to distinguish the assistant’s answer, the advertisement and the sponsor’s agent instead of treating the whole screen as one source.

- **England has made cyber security part of the safeguarding sentence.** The Department for Education updated its school and college digital standards on 16 September to state explicitly that the statutory duty to keep children safe online includes appropriate cyber-security measures. Its recommended operating rhythm is concrete: a risk assessment annually and after major change or incident, a termly revisit, annual cyber training for pupils, staff and at least one governor, and an acceptable-use policy signed by anyone with network or data access, including guests and supply teachers. The technical section calls for centrally managed anti-malware, approval of current and future cloud applications, and browser settings that users cannot change to install extensions or bypass safeguards ([UK Department for Education, 16 September 2026](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/meeting-digital-and-technology-standards-in-schools-and-colleges/cyber-security-core-standard)). This is UK guidance, not an international-school mandate. Its useful move is to stop treating safeguarding, IT operations and staff development as three unrelated committees.

## On the radar

- **Chrome 153 carries three critical fixes in a 42-fix release.** Google’s 15 September desktop update moves Windows and Mac to 153.0.8010.47/.48 and Linux to .47. The published list begins with critical flaws in WebGL, Internals and Workers, followed by high-severity issues in input handling, V8, authorisation, extensions, service workers and the DOM ([Chrome Releases, 15 September 2026](https://chromereleases.googleblog.com/2026/09/stable-channel-update-for-desktop_0541751186.html)). Google says rollout will take days or weeks and withholds some detail until most users update; managed-browser compliance is the meaningful signal, not the announcement date.

- **Google Drive can now combine sensitivity and audience in one sharing rule.** The 15 September Workspace launch lets administrators block files matching labels or data-loss-prevention conditions from all external sharing, allow only named internal or external audiences, or deny specified groups and domains. It is rolling out to Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus as well as selected enterprise editions ([Google Workspace Updates, 15 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/set-up-sharing-boundaries-for-google-Drive-with-unified-data-protection-rules.html)). That is a potentially useful bridge between a data-classification policy and an enforceable sharing boundary; the remaining work is whether the labels are accurate enough to trust.

- **Gemini’s newest live models make voice agents more capable before schools have settled voice evidence.** Google made Gemini 3.8 Live and 3.8 Live Extended Thinking generally available on 15 September. Both are audio-to-audio Live API models; the first emphasises low-latency dialogue and asynchronous function calling, while the second adds background reasoning for harder exchanges ([Gemini API release notes, 15 September 2026](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/changelog)). This is developer infrastructure, not a classroom product release. It lowers the engineering distance to a tutor that speaks, reasons and calls tools, while leaving transcript visibility, identity, consent and learning evidence to whoever builds the product.

## Sceptic's corner

Florida’s rules are easy to admire because they are specific, binding and written in the grammar of control. Name the tool. Notify the parent. Offer a non-AI alternative. Ban companionship, hidden profiling and training on pupil data. That is all more useful than a poster of ethical principles.

It can still produce compliance theatre. An opt-in form proves that a parent received a form, not that the tool improves learning or that the family understood an agent’s data flows. A development review for younger children can become a checklist performed once while the underlying model changes weekly. A ban on systems designed for emotional needs leaves a general-purpose assistant capable of emotional conversation outside the neatest category. And a vendor can satisfy a prohibition on training with school data while still producing shallow, dependency-forming instruction.

The strongest part of the package is therefore not the ban list but the forced description of the interaction: name, course, use, alternative, data boundary and incident route. That description can be tested against classroom reality. The weak version of implementation will collect signatures and file vendor assurances. The serious version will compare what pupils actually experience with what the approval said would happen, then revisit it after material product changes. Binding rules can create the inspection point. They cannot do the inspecting.
