## Top story

**Europe rewrote its AI rulebook on Monday, and the one clause pointing straight at school practice got weaker rather than merely later.** Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 — the Digital Omnibus on AI, adopted 8 July and published in the Official Journal on 24 July — entered into force on 27 July, the third day after publication, having been drafted to take effect "as a matter of urgency" ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng)). It amends the 2024 AI Act on three fronts a school would feel. The timetable first: full obligations for Annex III high-risk systems now fall due on 2 December 2027, and 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products ([AI Front Page, 27 July 2026](https://aifront-page.com/eu-ai-act-amendment-takes-effect-today-2026-1744/)). Education sits squarely inside Annex III — systems that determine admission, evaluate learning outcomes in ways that steer the learning process, assess the appropriate level of education for a person, or monitor for prohibited behaviour during tests ([Annex III](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/)). Second, two new Article 5 prohibitions bite on 2 December 2026: generating or manipulating realistic intimate material without "freely-given, specific, informed, unambiguous and explicit consent", and the equivalent involving minors ([NicFab](https://www.nicfab.eu/en/posts/digital-omnibus-ai-official-journal/)). Article 5 breaches carry the top penalty band, up to €35 million or 7% of global turnover, and the transparency duty for newly placed generative systems still applies from 2 August 2026, with machine-readable marking of systems already on the market due 2 December 2026 ([Modulos](https://www.modulos.ai/blog/eu-ai-act-omnibus-now-law)).

Third, and least covered: Article 4's AI-literacy duty has been softened from ensuring staff hold sufficient AI literacy to taking measures that support it, with explicit text that the obligation "does not require... guarantee any specific level". Schools outside the EU are not deployers under this regulation, but vendors ship one compliance posture globally and the marking rules change what appears inside tools everywhere. The deadlines that slipped were the vendors'. The clause aimed at practice moved the other way — and it moved down.

## Other stories

- **NVIDIA convened 36 partners to secure AI agents, and every frontier lab stayed home.** The Open Secure AI Alliance launched on 27 July with 37 organisations — NVIDIA plus 36 — among them Microsoft, Cisco, Cloudflare, CrowdStrike, Hugging Face, IBM, Palo Alto Networks, Red Hat, Elastic and the Linux Foundation; OpenAI, Google, Meta and Anthropic do not appear on the inaugural membership list ([The Hacker News, 27 July 2026](https://thehackernews.com/2026/07/nvidia-forms-37-member-open-secure-ai.html)). NVIDIA contributed NOOA, an Apache 2.0 research framework meant to make agent behaviour testable, traceable, auditable and governable, and Jensen Huang framed the whole thing against last week's incident: "During the Hugging Face incident, closed AI blocked essential forensics. An open-weight frontier model helped contain the intrusion" ([EdTech Innovation Hub, 29 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/nvidia-and-36-partners-form-open-secure-ai-alliance-for-cyber-defense)). Exabeam CISO Kevin Kirkwood named the gap plainly: "The major frontier model developers need to be at the table, and the industry needs agreed rules for liability when an agent exceeds scope" ([Infosecurity Magazine, 28 July 2026](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/nvidia-open-security-ai-alliance/)). The practical read for a school is unglamorous: the agents arriving on education tiers this term are built by the four companies not in the room.

- **Canvas institutions start receiving their own stolen files this week, three months after the intrusion.** Instructure began sending secure ShareFile links to designated institutional security contacts from 26 July, with live webinars explaining the delivered data files running 29–31 July; institutions had until 17:00 ET on 22 July to nominate up to two named individuals — explicitly people, "not shared inboxes" — to receive them ([Instructure incident update](https://www.instructure.com/incident_update)). Delivery had been paused on 14 July "out of an abundance of caution" after the third-party platform chosen to distribute the packets was itself reported to face a security threat; Instructure's 21 July note says that threat "did not involve Canvas or any Instructure systems, and your data was never at risk." The underlying intrusion began 30 April and was confirmed on 1 May. ShinyHunters claimed 3.65 terabytes and 275 million records; Instructure has confirmed only names, email addresses, student ID numbers and user messages, with "no evidence that passwords, dates of birth, government identifiers, or financial information were involved" ([Hackread, 6 May 2026](https://hackread.com/shinyhunters-instructure-canvas-lms-vimeo-data-breach/)). Just under three months from intrusion to "here is what they took from you" is the number to carry into any LMS transition — and a school running neither Canvas nor its parent's stack should treat this as a rehearsal rather than an incident.

- **A 152,000-student analysis says the lever is clearer goals, not more feedback — and it ran on pre-AI data.** The Education University of Hong Kong published research on 23 July from a team led by Professor Zi Yan analysing 151,969 students across 19 countries and regions in PISA. Frequent feedback alone did not lift attainment; school-wide clarity of learning goals combined with systematic progress monitoring was associated with a 0.158-standard-deviation gain in reading achievement. Yan's framing is that "An assessment ecology is shaped jointly by school policies, teacher practices and teacher-student interactions", and that in an examination-oriented system "teachers may devote considerable time to feedback, yet students may not use it effectively" ([Phys.org, 23 July 2026](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-explore-classroom-applications-ai-platforms.html)). Read the framing carefully before quoting it as AI evidence. The paper is "School Assessment Policy, Teacher Assessment Practice and Training, and Reading Achievement: A Multi-Level Analysis of PISA 2018 Data", published in *Education Sciences* in April, and PISA 2018 predates generative AI entirely. The AI belongs to two platforms the group has since built, EASE and FAITH, at roughly 2,200 and 350 registered users. The assessment finding stands on its own merits; it is not a finding about AI, and the press framing quietly conflates the two.

- **Britain's new prime minister put a technical route at 14 at the centre of the schools plan.** Andy Burnham announced on 28 July that pupils in England will be able to take high-quality technical subjects — engineering, healthcare, digital technology — alongside core English, maths and science from age 14, with regional mayors, colleges and employers rather than Whitehall shaping which specialisms a locality runs: advanced manufacturing, AI, digital tech, clean energy, life sciences, construction, health and care, creative industries. "For too long in this country, students have been told that you must take the academic path" to succeed, he said, adding that "From today, Britain will value the hard hat every bit as much as the graduation cap" ([Times Higher Education, 28 July 2026](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/burnham-shares-technical-courses-plan-end-academic-obsession)). The supporting numbers are the actual argument: more than a million UK 16-to-24-year-olds are not in education, employment or training, and higher-level apprentices' median earnings five years after completion run £37,300 against £32,100 for first-degree graduates five years after graduation ([EdTech Innovation Hub, 29 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/uk-prime-minister-andy-burnham-puts-technical-education-at-center-of-england-schools-plan)). Any school whose counselling model quietly assumes university is the destination now has a national data point arguing the other way.

- **A university named an associate provost for AI and pointedly did not name a platform.** Wake Forest appointed William Fleeson, Hultquist Family Professor of Psychology, as Associate Provost for AI Initiatives on 28 July, coordinating an "AI for Human Flourishing" programme covering campus AI priorities, support for faculty and staff leaders across schools and divisions, and long-term academic strategy. Three cross-school pilots launch in 2026-27: AI Compass on ethics and societal implications, led by Ana Iltis; an AI Teaching Studio on teaching and learning; and an AI Impact Clinic running applied projects with outside partners, led by Shannon McKeen, Paul Pauca and Errin Fulp. Two details travel down to secondary regardless of the higher-education setting: the lead is a psychologist, not a technologist, and the deliverables are three named pilots with named owners rather than a licence count. That is a structure which can report progress without reporting seats filled — which is the metric most institutional AI programmes quietly default to when nobody specifies a better one.

## Sceptic's corner

The claim already circulating is that Brussels blinked — that the Digital Omnibus is a retreat and the compliance pressure is off until 2027. It is an easy story to tell, because the headline number genuinely moved: Annex III conformity work slid to 2 December 2027, and the proposed "standards-readiness trigger", which would have tied deadlines to technical standards arriving, was deleted for fixed calendar dates ([Modulos](https://www.modulos.ai/blog/eu-ai-act-omnibus-now-law)).

Now test it from the school's side of the desk. What moved is work vendors owe: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, human oversight, database registration. What did not move is 2 August 2026 for transparency on newly placed generative systems, or 2 December 2026 for the intimate-imagery prohibitions and marking ([NicFab](https://www.nicfab.eu/en/posts/digital-omnibus-ai-official-journal/)). And Article 4, the only clause in the Act that ever pointed at staff capability, did not get a later date. It got a lower bar — an obligation that explicitly "does not require... guarantee any specific level".

So the net effect on a school is worse than "two more years", not better. The deployer's duty of care is unchanged, the prohibitions that touch students soonest arrive on the original schedule, and the vendor documentation that would have given a school something concrete to read before signing is now more than a year further away — landing inside the window when most schools choose their AI tooling.

The staffroom line: December 2027 is the vendors' deadline, not the school's. The only thing that reached the school's desk this week is a literacy obligation that no longer has to work.
