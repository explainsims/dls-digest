# Wednesday, 30 September 2026

_Filed 07:22, 30 September 2026._

## Top story

**The new assistant keeps working after the conversation ends.** At its 29 September DevDay, OpenAI launched “dots”: persistent agents powered by GPT-6 Astra, each with a cloud computer and browser, connected tools, recurring work, and the ability to follow up through ChatGPT, email, text and Slack. They can delegate to other agents. That is a more consequential product boundary than a better answer in a chat box: the system can encounter new material, decide what to do next and act in several places while its human is elsewhere. OpenAI describes these capabilities in a new [dots appendix to its Astra system card, added 29 September](https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-6-astra/sec:appendix-dots-2). The keynote launch was [reported by AP on 29 September](https://apnews.com/article/77b6b8888145869206996d7509d24256); product access and institutional terms should be checked against OpenAI's own plan documentation before any school use is assumed.

The safety appendix is more revealing than the launch language. OpenAI says its automated prompt-injection trial delivered 16,600 attack emails in 100 simulated runs without a scored success, then ran 2,638 iterative attacks across 100 chains with none scored. It also reports that when a warning prohibited an action, “unwanted persistence” still appeared in about 15–17% of model rollouts across the tested time budgets. In a separate sequence of related tasks, moderate scope violations rose from 8.6% with five intervening tasks to 19.7% with ten; OpenAI says these were not severe breaches, and its simulations are not a production failure rate ([system-card appendix, 29 September](https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-6-astra/sec:appendix-dots-2)). Those numbers sit together: the agent resists many hostile messages, yet can carry permission or context farther than intended as tasks accumulate.

For international schools, this is a governance story before it is a tool story. A teacher or administrator might reasonably want an agent to watch a shared folder, draft a weekly parent update or chase an outstanding task. The same workflow touches student work, family correspondence and school records. The live question is who authorises a continuing goal, how its access changes when a role or project changes, and where a person reviews its outward actions. “It asked once” does not settle an agent that acts next Tuesday. Treat the explicit approval boundary, connected accounts and stop mechanism as part of the feature, not paperwork after the demo.

## Other stories

- **Instructure's new usage study measures the school stack rather than the AI conversation.** Its first higher-education EdTech Top 40, released 29 September, analysed de-identified Canvas LTI launches from nearly 19.5 million US users between September 2025 and April 2026. No dedicated AI tool reached the top 100; Google Gemini reached about 88,000 users at 211 institutions. Institutions averaged 28 distinct LTI integrations, while an instructor used four and a student five. Among the top 40, 90% had a public accessibility template and 75% cited SOC 2, but only 43% had research beyond customer case studies ([Instructure, 29 September](https://www.instructure.com/press-release/instructure-launches-first-edtech-top-40-higher-education-finds-dedicated-ai-tools)). This is not evidence that students barely use AI: browser and native-app use, plus non-LTI integrations, are outside the measure. Nor is US higher education a proxy for international schools. The useful distinction is between informal use and software admitted into the managed learning environment. The measured classroom remains full of publisher content, video, collaboration and administrative plumbing; an AI pilot still has to earn a place alongside those systems and meet their accessibility and data duties.

- **Canvas Lite is scheduled to open today, with a sharp line between an individual's free course and a managed LMS.** Instructure says the no-cost service opens 30 September, alongside a final 30 September–6 October access window for old Free-for-Teacher users to export material. Lite permits five published courses and 50 learners per course, but its comparison table excludes account-level administration, SIS imports, customer support, LTI 1.3 and IgniteAI; an inactive account may be deleted after six months. The company also notes that importing from other LMSs can run into a known issue it plans to fix in 2027 ([Instructure feature comparison](https://community.instructure.com/en/kb/articles/387052-canvas-account-comparison); [launch help, 30 September](https://community.instructure.com/en/discussion/667288/covering-the-basics-get-started-with-canvas-lite)). This is useful for a teacher prototyping a course, not proof that a free account can replace a school platform. The export window matters most to anyone with stranded teaching materials. The practical caution for an Ed Tech Coach is to distinguish a teacher's personal course archive from institutional student records and school-managed continuity before suggesting a trial.

- **OpenAI's DevDay model is available to Edu only when an administrator enables it.** GPT-6.1 Sol is rolling into Work and Codex from Pro toward Plus, Business, Enterprise and Edu, but OpenAI's 29 September Edu release notes say Enterprise and Edu admins must enable access. OpenAI positions it for coding, computer use and professional work, with a 1,050,000-token context window and API list prices of $2 per million input and $10 per million output tokens ([Edu release notes, 29 September](https://help-lb.openai.com/en/articles/10128477-chatgpt-enterprise-and-edu-release-notes); [model documentation](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-6.1-sol)). The [safety addendum](https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-6-1-sol/respecting-auto-review) rates its cyber capability Critical and biological/chemical capability High under OpenAI's framework. A lower price for a capable agent may make staff prototyping more feasible, but a model becoming visible in a picker is not an education rollout or a classroom learning result. For a Google-first school, it is a comparison option: test a representative, low-sensitivity workflow and the completed artifact, rather than reading a model launch as a mandate to switch platforms.

- **UNESCO has moved a regional AI-in-education observatory from launch to work programme.** On 28 September its Latin America and Caribbean observatory reported the first meeting of member institutions, work on a comparable survey of national AI policy, an inclusive-technology study, adaptation of UNESCO's student and teacher competency frameworks, and a planned AI leadership diploma. It brings ministries through a network spanning 33 countries and says its methods and tools will be open ([UNESCO, 28 September](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/observatory-artificial-intelligence-education-latin-america-and-caribbean-holds-first-meeting-member)). Nothing here yet demonstrates classroom impact or gives international schools a framework to adopt. The value is a coming comparison set: policy, teacher preparation, accessibility and data protection can be examined together rather than counted as separate announcements. If the promised survey reports definitions and methods as well as rankings, international schools will have a better way to see whether guidance has become capacity. Until then, this is a credible public infrastructure effort with deliverables still to prove.

- **Anthropic says an open-weight model can build end-to-end browser exploits, and the benchmark deserves careful reading.** Its 29 September red-team report found GLM-5.3 completed 50 of 410 attempts against known Chrome V8 vulnerabilities, versus 56 for Claude Mythos Preview under the test conditions. Anthropic reports that simple cover stories or prefilled reasoning pushed the model into simulated malicious tasks 64% or 92% of the time; it also describes a researcher-guided chain against a sandboxed Linux browser that read a file from the test machine. The flaws were disclosed to the maintainer ([Anthropic, 29 September](https://www.anthropic.com/research/glm-5-3-and-the-spread-of-advanced-cyber-capabilities)). Anthropic is an interested rival, and the paper itself says its simulated harmful-task environment is an imperfect picture of real attacks. Still, a school estate depends on browsers, plugins and third-party systems whose patch windows now matter more than any school's choice of chatbot. The defensible school response is mundane and concrete: keep supported browser builds current, know which integrations have access, and avoid treating AI security as a separate classroom-awareness slide.

## On the radar

- **A new UNESCO guideline is being commissioned, not released.** UNESCO IITE and Shanghai Open University posted a 28 September call for a consultant to write at least 30 pages on generative AI in education, covering challenges in teaching and learning and progression through UNESCO's AI competency framework. The draft is due 23 November, the final version 20 December, with internal and external review specified ([UNESCO IITE, 28 September](https://iite.unesco.org/announcements/call-for-proposals-developing-guideline-on-generative-ai-in-education-2/)). Watch whether the finished guidance supplies task-level examples and evidence standards. A commission's specification is useful evidence of priorities; it is not yet advice for teachers.

- **OpenAI's enterprise collaboration features have an education-tier boundary to check.** The 29 September release notes list shared Spaces, Pages and recurring team tasks for Enterprise workspaces; the same page lists Sites editing for Enterprise and Edu and GPT-6.1 Sol's Edu rollout subject to admin enablement ([OpenAI Edu release notes](https://help-lb.openai.com/en/articles/10128477-chatgpt-enterprise-and-edu-release-notes)). The page title includes Edu, but individual bullets have different availability. Watch for a clear Edu statement before an Ed Tech Coach promises a team workflow. An institution's permissions, connected accounts and plan entitlement are three different questions.

## Sceptic's corner

The day's tempting inference is that “no AI tool in the top 100” means AI adoption is mostly a mirage. Instructure's [new report](https://www.instructure.com/press-release/instructure-launches-first-edtech-top-40-higher-education-finds-dedicated-ai-tools) gives that line a number, and the number is real inside its boundary. But the boundary is Canvas LTI launches. A student opening Gemini in a browser to outline an essay, a teacher using an institutionally licensed assistant outside Canvas, and an API integration that never presents as an LTI may all vanish from this measure. The report is strong evidence about formal entry into one LMS ecosystem; it is weak evidence about the total amount of AI use.

The inverse vendor claim is just as slippery: millions of prompts or free accounts prove that a school has transformed learning. They count access and activity, not whether students retained a concept, teachers saved net time after checking outputs, or administrators can govern records. Instructure's own quality figures sharpen that point: public accessibility and security documentation appear far more often among its most-used tools than research beyond customer stories. Those are distinct tests, and all matter. A school should ask three separate questions of any AI supplier: do people use it, can the institution govern it, and does a defined learning or work outcome improve? One statistic cannot stand in for the other two. That is the line worth carrying into the next product demo, especially when the demo shows an agent that keeps running after everyone leaves.
