## Top story

**AI can remove the disagreement that group work was meant to teach.** The Digital Education Council and Pearson's 27 October public announcement of *Designing AI for Collaborative Learning* describes students arriving with similar AI-prepared ideas and asking AI to settle disagreements instead of working through them together. The report was already the subject of a 29 September member briefing; yesterday's announcement is the fresh development, rather than evidence that the research was conducted this week ([DEC announcement](https://www.digitaleducationcouncil.com/newsroom/ai-risks-reducing-peer-exchange-and-eroding-collaboration-new-dec-and-pearson-report-finds), [earlier briefing](https://www.digitaleducationcouncil.com/executive-briefings-event/designing-ai-for-collaborative-learning)).

The public resource page describes an analysis of more than **80 global case studies**, mapping **15 approaches** against seven mechanisms through which collaborative learning works. One example puts AI in the role of an opposing negotiator, with students developing their response together. The page also recognises the cost: students may practise answering objections while outsourcing the generation of objections. The complete report requires registration or member access; the accessible material does not establish the prevalence of these behaviours across schools, or a causal effect on learning ([report overview](https://www.digitaleducationcouncil.com/resource-library-items/designing-ai-for-collaborative-learning)).

The useful distinction for international schools is between dividing a product among several people and needing several people to think. A group can submit something polished without anyone having to change their mind. That possibility predates AI; a private adviser available to every participant makes it easier to miss. A history discussion in which students must reconcile conflicting interpretations has a different learning purpose from a team simply assembling slides. The same permission to use AI could have different consequences in each.

My read is that collaboration belongs inside the assessment description. What must students explain to one another? Where must they weigh competing evidence? Which decision must they own together? Those questions give the teacher something more revealing to listen for than equal speaking time. A smoothly completed task can be a success, while a smoothly avoided argument can be the lesson that never happened.

## Other stories

- **Gemini can now change the notebook it previously only helped you think about.** Google's **23 October** announcement makes Gemini Notebook a Connected App inside Gemini: users can create, edit or delete notebooks and sources, save notes, and query existing material through chat. Gemini can also select the connection automatically for relevant prompts. Google lists it as available now to Workspace and personal accounts. Organisational access requires both Gemini and Gemini Notebook to be enabled; the connection is on by default, while individual users in the European Economic Area start with it off ([Google Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/take-actions-on-your-notebooks-from-Gemini-Notebook-directly-in-the-Gemini-app.html)). For a teacher's research collection, the change is from retrieval to maintenance: an instruction can alter the evidence base that a later answer will use. That is useful when adding a new curriculum source, and consequential when a casual request removes an old one. An Ed Tech Coach demonstrating the feature could show the notebook before and after the conversation, including which sources actually changed. The interesting competence is understanding the resulting collection, rather than remembering the command. Availability in a vendor's release note also leaves the school's actual account settings and age restrictions to be checked; it does not establish that every student can use every connected action today.

- **Apple's creative apps are getting assistants and an external-agent doorway, with November on the calendar.** Apple's **27 October** Creator Studio announcement adds contextual assistants in Keynote, Pages and Numbers, plus Model Context Protocol support through which external AI tools can read content, edit it and perform actions. Keynote also gains an open, JSON-based interchange format, `.keyx`. Updates are due in **November**, rather than already installed. The assistants are beta features in US English on Apple Intelligence-capable devices running version 27 of the relevant operating system; MCP requires macOS 27 ([Apple Newsroom](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/10/apple-creator-studio-adds-powerful-new-ai-features-and-even-more-creative-tools/)). For an Apple school, the quieter development is that a presentation becomes something another tool can inspect and manipulate, instead of a final file carried between applications. That could help adapt a teaching resource, but the quality question remains specific: did the revised deck preserve the mathematical notation, source attribution and intended sequence? Those are sensible checks for a classroom example when the update arrives. The announcement describes capability and compatibility, without demonstrating that an assistant can make those editorial decisions reliably. A beautifully rearranged explanation can still rearrange the reasoning out of it.

- **A new study tested whether an AI-supported programme left participants able to perform without AI.** Yuda He and colleagues' *Scientific Reports* article, published in accepted form on **26 October**, compared a structured training programme with career guidance matched for topics, duration and facilitator contact. Twelve intact cohorts of education graduates in Guangdong, **186 people** in total, were assigned as whole groups. Blinded experts rated recruitment materials produced under supervision without AI. Among the **168** with post-test data, the adjusted difference favoured the AI-supported programme by **5.38 points on a 100-point scale**; the reported standardised effect was **0.60** ([accepted article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-76662-y)). There are substantial qualifications: raters had no prespecified rubric, the allocation was by intact cohort, and the design cannot separate AI's contribution from structured practice and feedback. This is graduate preparation, not secondary-school learning. Still, its outcome choice is worth carrying into teacher discussion. An assisted practice session and an unassisted performance answer different questions; a better-looking draft during the session answers neither by itself. A school trial could define the independent performance first, then judge whether the support helps learners reach it. The article is an early accepted version, subject to further editing, and offers a promising association with that testable shape rather than a general endorsement of AI training.

- **A four-part vocabulary challenges the single AI strategy box.** In **26 October** *EDUCAUSE Review*, Szymon Machajewski distinguishes AI supporting educational operations, AI used deliberately in teaching and learning, AI as part of a discipline's professional practice, and general AI literacy. His argument is that progress in one layer does not establish readiness in the others: staff might automate routine work and teach prompting while students remain poorly prepared for how AI changes their field ([original analysis](https://er.educause.edu/articles/2026/10/why-ai-in-education-is-four-things-not-one)). This is a proposed framework illustrated through higher education, rather than an evaluated intervention. It nevertheless supplies a useful question for international schools' professional learning: which capability would a session actually develop? Learning to format resources more quickly does not demonstrate that a teacher can design an effective AI-supported learning activity. Equally, an excellent lesson about hallucinations does not settle what counts as competent AI use in design, programming or journalism. Secondary schools need not turn subjects into job training to recognise that distinction. An authentic example from a field can expose what its practitioners must still know, verify and take responsibility for. The framework earns its keep if it makes those gaps visible; four renamed columns in a strategy document would be a rather efficient way to miss the point.

- **Buffalo is funding the work between broad AI principles and advice for young children's learning.** The University at Buffalo announced an additional **$500,000** from the US Institute of Education Sciences on **27 October** for its Center for Early Literacy and Responsible AI. The work will connect state and regional agencies with researchers, identify evidence gaps, and develop practical guidance for educators and families. Planned collaborators include U-GAIN Reading and the National AI Institute for Exceptional Education; a convening is planned for **2027–28** ([university announcement](https://ed.buffalo.edu/news-events/news.host.html/content/shared/ed/news/ticker/2026/10/27.detail.html)). These are funded intentions, not a finished guidance document or proof of improved literacy. The useful signal for a whole-school conversation is the explicit combination of child development, literacy, inclusive education and family concerns. A policy written around an older student's chatbot use will not automatically answer what an adult should do with an AI-generated reading text for a six-year-old. Vocabulary, cultural assumptions, decoding difficulty and the adult's mediation all matter to that example. The announcement supplies a research programme to watch, while leaving those judgements with educators today. Waiting for evidence need not mean waiting to ask precise questions about the reading experience a child is actually being offered.

## Sceptic's corner

There is a tempting overcorrection in the collaboration story: if AI removes productive disagreement, configure it to disagree, and the educational problem is solved. DEC's public example is more careful than that. Its adversarial-peer approach also flags the possibility that students stop producing their own counterarguments ([report overview](https://www.digitaleducationcouncil.com/resource-library-items/designing-ai-for-collaborative-learning)). A debate can become more demanding while leaving a different part of thinking unpractised.

That is not a reason to reject the activity. A novice sometimes needs a supplied objection before learning to spot one. The question is what happens later, when the scripted opponent is absent. Can the group notice an assumption, formulate a challenge and revise its view without a machine setting the agenda? Counting the number of rebuttals in the assisted discussion would not answer it.

The Guangdong study offers a useful contrast in measurement by assessing performance without AI after the programme, although its combined intervention and unstructured expert ratings limit what can be inferred ([accepted article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-76662-y)). Schools do not need to reproduce a research trial to preserve that distinction. They can ask whether a learner can explain a choice, handle an unfamiliar example or raise a relevant objection after the support is removed. The result may justify keeping the scaffold longer, changing it or retiring it. An engaging AI conversation is a learning opportunity. What the learner can subsequently do is the evidence that gives it educational weight.
