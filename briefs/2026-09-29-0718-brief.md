## Top story

**An AI chatbot asked vulnerable students to counsel it; the school still cannot say whether it helped them.** In a 28 September investigation, NPR revisited Dayton Public Schools' pilot at an alternative school for teenagers with behavioural and academic difficulties. The district and SchoolAI built “Jordan”, a social-emotional chatbot that presents its own problems and asks students for advice. It then grades their replies for engagement and authenticity. Dayton paid SchoolAI $72,600 last academic year for several AI products, including Jordan, and renewed at $78,650 this year. Administrators told NPR that students and families had been notified that school employees, an external evaluator and SchoolAI staff could view the chat logs ([NPR, 28 September 2026](https://www.kuow.org/npr-news/2026-09-28/schools-are-experimenting-with-ai-with-little-evidence-or-policy-to-guide-them)).

One student described feeling helped by the exchange, and the outside consultant hired to design and evaluate the pilot found enough positive feedback in logs and interviews to continue testing. He also said his concrete measures reach only as far as people's perceptions, and that Jordan is not ready for a district-wide rollout. Wichita Public Schools, in the same reporting, has held back a large student-facing rollout because it has yet to settle a success measure. It is instead trying bounded uses: AI literacy without chatbots in early grades, limited tools in middle school and an AI maths tutor for some older students ([NPR's district interviews](https://www.kuow.org/npr-news/2026-09-28/schools-are-experimenting-with-ai-with-little-evidence-or-policy-to-guide-them)).

For international schools, the uncomfortable question is sharper than whether Jordan is engaging. A bot that asks a young person to manage its apparent distress may elicit real disclosure precisely because it feels like a relationship. That raises questions about the purpose of the interaction, who reads the transcript, and what happens if a response signals harm. A consent notice and a happy student account cannot settle whether a social-emotional intervention is safe or effective. The pilot's useful lesson is methodological: define an outcome and an escalation path before counting conversations. A maths helper can be checked against later independent work; a relationship-like helper needs a far more exacting standard before a school calls it support.

## Other stories

- **Google Meet's new note-taking default begins today, with an important education-tier distinction.** Google's 22 September update says that from 29 September admins can pre-configure “Take notes for me” for meetings of three or more people. The setting is on by default for Business Standard and Business Plus, but off by default for Enterprise Standard, Enterprise Plus, Frontline Plus and Google AI Pro for Education. Users will be able to override the admin default in their Meet settings; both admin and user changes have an extended rollout that may take more than 15 days ([Google Workspace Updates, 22 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/new-google-meet-take-notes-for-me-settings-for-admins-and-end-users-take-effect-September-29th.html?m=1)). The education add-on is thus not in the automatic-on group, and the announcement does not identify a particular school's actual settings. Still, the practical boundary is a meeting's sensitivity, not its head count: a three-person parent, counselling or staffing call may qualify technically while being a poor candidate for an AI-generated Drive record. The useful question for the Google admin is what is configured for each organisational unit and what an individual host can switch on, followed by who receives and retains any notes. This is a read of settings, not a case for changing grants.

- **Anthropic's cheaper Sonnet 5.5 makes routine agent work easier to justify, but its numbers are the vendor's.** Released on 28 September, Claude Sonnet 5.5 is priced at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, unchanged from Sonnet 5. Anthropic says it generates output more than 30% faster and costs up to 30% less per task because it uses fewer tokens. It reports a 70.6% score on Terminal-Bench 4.0 against Sonnet 5's 10.3%, while saying Opus 5.5 remains stronger for complex, open-ended work requiring sustained judgement. The new model also inherits cyber safeguards and fallbacks because its cyber capability is comparable to Opus 5's ([Anthropic, 28 September 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-sonnet-5-5)). In a school, lower cost per completed task could make small, teacher-owned document and app workflows more plausible than a frontier model for everything. But a benchmark that grades agentic coding is not evidence that a generated worksheet is accurate or that an assistant will handle student information correctly. Test a representative task, including the final artefact and the human review time; “cheaper tokens” is a purchasing input, not an educational outcome.

- **A new privacy audit found AI disclosures missing from one-third of the ed-tech policies it examined.** A 23 September preprint by Meghna Manoj Nair and Rachel Greenstadt combines 12 interviews with ed-tech professionals and a coded audit of 48 platform privacy policies. The authors report that 33% of those platforms made no meaningful AI disclosure despite visible AI features, while 73% used only generic accountability and breach-response language. Their interviews describe privacy work repeatedly deferred while teams prioritised product features, growth and funding; K–12 platforms did better on children's consent where rules demand it, without the same improvement in AI governance ([Nair and Greenstadt, arXiv, 23 September 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28137)). This is a small, non-random platform sample and a preprint, not an estimate for all school software. Its concrete procurement use is the gap between “we collect these data” and “here is how the AI feature uses, shares, retains and deletes them.” A school's review should request those answers for the actual feature and data flow, rather than treating a generic privacy policy or a vendor's cloud-hosting assurance as the whole answer. It also helps explain why the Jordan pilot's log access matters as much as the bot's visible chat.

- **Europe's new education reports separate AI guidance from evidence of learning.** On 22 September the European Commission released a paper on generative AI in teaching and a Eurydice survey of digital education at school. Its summary says learning benefit depends on how generative AI is used and that long-term skill effects remain unclear. Across the systems studied, digital competence appears in lower-secondary curricula almost everywhere, but national or standardised assessment is uncommon; nearly two-thirds of systems have some AI strategy, guidance or policy framework. Infrastructure, school-level access and technical support still differ ([European Commission, 22 September 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/two-commission-reports-show-impact-artificial-intelligence-and-digital-technologies-teaching-and)). This is a European system-level comparison, not a classroom trial or a prescription for any particular international school. Its relevance is the distinction a school can easily lose: having policy language and device access does not mean pupils can demonstrate digital competence, nor that a particular AI intervention improves it. For Ed Tech Coaches, the useful translation is to attach an observable student performance to each proposed use, rather than counting licences, teacher badges or policy pages as learning.

## Sceptic's corner

The tempting answer to the Dayton story is “pilot carefully, then scale.” But *pilot* is a method only when someone has chosen an outcome that could disconfirm the idea. In NPR's account, Jordan's evaluator says his measures go no further than student and staff perception, while the district renews the contract and anticipates more social-emotional bots ([NPR, 28 September 2026](https://www.kuow.org/npr-news/2026-09-28/schools-are-experimenting-with-ai-with-little-evidence-or-policy-to-guide-them)). A teenager saying the bot felt relatable matters; it is testimony about experience. It is not a measure of improved wellbeing, better access to a human counsellor or reduced risk. Nor does a school get to assume that more disclosure is automatically beneficial when employees, an evaluator and a vendor may read the logs.

The opposite reflex—ban every AI experiment until a perfect study appears—also ducks the design work. Wichita's bounded approach shows a narrower path: teach what AI is before giving young pupils a conversational companion, and keep higher-stakes tools limited while success measures are unsettled ([same 28 September reporting](https://www.kuow.org/npr-news/2026-09-28/schools-are-experimenting-with-ai-with-little-evidence-or-policy-to-guide-them)). For a school weighing any student-facing helper, the trial should specify the population, data readers, stop condition, human escalation, comparator and a later task the student completes without the tool. The staffroom line is this: a pilot with no way to fail is a rollout with a smaller invoice. Jordan may yet help some children; the published evidence does not let us call that established.
