## Top story

**Gemini’s memory is spreading faster than its administrative controls.** Google is rolling persistent custom instructions out beyond Docs into Ask Gemini in Drive and Chat and the Gemini side panels in Gmail, Sheets and Slides. A user can tell Gemini to retain preferences about style, tone or formatting; those instructions then follow that user across the supported Workspace surfaces. The rollout began on 2 September and may take up to 15 days. Google lists no feature-specific administrator control, while making the capability available wherever the underlying Gemini surfaces are licensed and accessible ([Google Workspace Updates, 2 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/custom-instructions-for-gemini-in-Workspace-now-available-in-more-apps.html)).

For international schools, the immediate value is real. A teacher could retain a house style for parent messages, a preferred structure for lesson resources or a standing accessibility reminder instead of rebuilding the prompt each time. The risk is equally ordinary: a persistent instruction can become invisible context. A well-meant preference such as “always simplify the language” or “always produce three differentiated versions” may affect outputs in another app or task after the user has forgotten it is there. Because the setting is personal, a school cannot assume two staff using the same approved account environment are working from the same baseline.

This is not a reason to disable Gemini across Workspace. It is a reason to treat saved instructions as a user-controlled configuration layer, closer to a reusable template than a harmless convenience. Staff guidance should ask users to keep instructions task-generic, exclude personal or student data, review the Personalization panel periodically and disclose a consequential saved instruction when sharing an output for review. A small test should also establish whether instructions travel exactly as advertised across school licences and whether deleting one removes its influence from new interactions. Google has delivered consistency for the individual; it has not delivered consistency for the institution. The useful governance move is to make that distinction visible before personalised behaviour becomes part of routine work.

## Other stories

- **Washington, D.C. has published a staff AI stoplight with unusually specific boundaries.** The Office of the State Superintendent of Education released its first model policy for staff AI use on 1 September after a February survey found that only 45% of local education agencies had one. Red uses include student discipline, teacher-performance evaluation, physical surveillance and decisions about IEP or Section 504 eligibility. Yellow uses include reviewing or grading work, drafting IEP language and monitoring school-issued devices; green uses include lesson planning, adapting materials, tutoring plans, data analysis and communications, all with human review. The policy is optional guidance, not legal advice, and explicitly does not cover student use or procurement ([OSSE, 1 September 2026](https://osse.dc.gov/release/osse-releases-ai-model-policy-guide-responsible-staff-use-schools)). Its value for international schools is the separation of the task from the tool: an approved enterprise chatbot can still be inappropriate for a high-stakes decision. The weak point is the yellow band, where “human oversight” needs local definitions of permitted data, required evidence and who owns the final judgment.

- **D2L’s assessment story is directionally useful and commercially entangled.** A 2 September post drawing on D2L and Tyton Partners’ Time for Class work says 47% of faculty identify assessment design as the main teaching practice they are modifying because of AI, while 52% of students report that instructors have already changed assessments. It groups faculty as integrators, defenders relying more on proctoring, and a larger group making relatively few changes; integrators report better engagement outcomes ([D2L, 2 September 2026](https://www.d2l.com/blog/in-the-age-of-ai-how-do-we-know-learning-happened/)). The post does not, by itself, establish that integration caused engagement or learning, and its higher-education sample should not be poured directly into high school. The article also does not provide the survey’s sample frame, response rate or engagement measure, so the linked report—not the headline percentages—is the evidentiary object. The practical grain is better: assessment redesign is already becoming a workflow, not a policy slogan. The next useful examples are assignments that expose reasoning, checkpoints and revision decisions whether AI is used or not. “AI allowed” is not a design; the observable thinking is.

- **A principal and a student found that ChatGPT for Teens behaves better than a general chatbot, not that it solves classroom use.** EdSurge published a hands-on review on 1 September of the product OpenAI launched on 18 August for users aged 13–17. The platform includes Study Mode, homework-shortcut redirection, quizzes, learning visualisations and scheduled study hours. An Oklahoma principal found the system steered some study and risky-money prompts in constructive directions, while an Alabama eleventh-grader valued paced explanations and language practice but said long conversations could lose her requested teaching format ([EdSurge, 1 September 2026](https://www.edsurge.com/news/a-principal-and-a-student-reviewed-the-new-chatgpt-for-teens-they-had-plenty-to-say)). This is two informed users testing a product, not an efficacy or safety study. For international high schools, it does sharpen the evaluation script: test longitudinal instruction-following, shortcut resistance and failure recovery with actual teen tasks, then separate what the product defaults do from what school accounts, teachers and parents can reliably govern.

- **A Chinese secondary-teacher study links AI use with job satisfaction, but cannot tell us which caused which.** A paper published on 5 September surveyed 500 secondary teachers in mainland China. AI integration was positively associated with intrinsic job satisfaction (*c* = 0.333, *p* < .001); perceived work environment statistically mediated about 64% of the association, and the indirect relationship was stronger at higher reported AI literacy. The authors themselves limit the claim: this was cross-sectional self-report data from a non-probability sample, so the model supports association rather than causal effect ([Journal of Educational and Social Research, 5 September 2026](https://www.richtmann.org/journal/index.php/jesr/article/view/14904)). That caveat is the story. Confident teachers in supportive workplaces may be more likely to use AI and feel satisfied; AI may also help. A school PD evaluation should therefore measure workload, agency and support before and after a bounded implementation, not infer wellbeing from uptake or a cheerful end-of-session survey.

- **Microsoft’s new responsible-AI report shifts governance from models to connected systems.** Microsoft published its third annual transparency report on 1 September and says it has rebuilt its Responsible AI Standard around components of the technology stack—models, platform services and applications—and around whether Microsoft is developing or deploying each component. Core requirements remain, with scenario-specific controls layered on top. For agents that retain memory, use tools, access data and take actions, the company highlights identities, tool permissions and action monitoring as the relevant control objects ([Microsoft, 1 September 2026](https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2026/09/01/responsible-ai-in-2026-how-we-are-adapting-for-whats-ahead/)). This is a vendor’s account of its own governance, not an independent audit. The architecture still travels: a school reviewing an agent should ask which identity it acts as, what each connector can read or change, where actions are logged and how permissions are revoked. A model safety statement cannot answer those system questions.

## On the radar

- **PISA 2025 results arrive tomorrow with a large comparative view of AI use in schoolwork.** The OECD says more than 760,000 fifteen-year-olds in 91 countries and economies participated; the release covers science, mathematics, reading, computational problem solving, curiosity, perseverance, learner agency and evidence on how students use AI for schoolwork ([OECD launch page, current](https://www.oecd.org/en/events/2026/09/the-launch-of-the-oecd-programme-for-international-student-assessment-pisa-2025-report.html); [media advisory, 1 September 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/media-advisories/2026/09/oecd-to-launch-pisa-2025-on-8-september-2026.html)). The report is scheduled, not yet available. Watch definitions and denominators before turning country comparisons into school claims, especially whether “use” distinguishes occasional help from sustained dependence.

- **Toddle’s international-school AI event is five days away.** The free virtual How Schools AI programme on 12 September advertises participation from leaders at more than 3,000 schools and sessions on assessment, teacher support, leadership and protecting student understanding, with Jay McTighe, Laura Knight, Sabba Quidwai, Matthew Savage and Armin Martin among the named speakers ([Toddle, event scheduled 12 September 2026](https://www.toddleapp.com/events/how-schools-ai/)). This is a vendor-hosted programme, not neutral evidence. Watch for concrete school artefacts—task designs, decision rules and measures—not the size of the registration counter.

- **Google Vids can now turn documents into narrated video summaries, with no feature-level switch.** Google says Docs, PDFs and Word files can generate scripts, narration and visuals in Vids. It is available now on Rapid Release domains, with Scheduled Release rollout beginning 5 September, for Education Plus and several other tiers; Google lists no administrator or end-user control for the feature ([Google Workspace Updates, 2 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/turn-google-docs-pdfs-and-word-files-into-video-summaries-in-Google-Vids.html)). Watch whether citations, omissions and accessibility survive conversion. A fluent video can make a weak summary harder to inspect than the document it replaced.

## Sceptic's corner

The attractive claim in Google’s announcement is consistency: save instructions once, then let Gemini honour them across the places work happens. Consistency is useful. It is also not the same thing as institutional alignment.

A personal instruction can be consistently wrong, stale or inappropriate for a particular audience. It can encourage a teacher to produce recognisably uniform materials while quietly flattening reading demand, tone or disciplinary nuance. Because Google lists no administrator control for the feature, the institution cannot see “same platform” and infer “same configuration” ([Google Workspace Updates, 2 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/custom-instructions-for-gemini-in-Workspace-now-available-in-more-apps.html)). Microsoft’s governance framing is useful here: once AI has memory, data access and tools, controls belong around identities, permissions and monitored actions, not only around the model ([Microsoft, 1 September 2026](https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2026/09/01/responsible-ai-in-2026-how-we-are-adapting-for-whats-ahead/)).

The answer is not an approved school-wide super-prompt. That would turn local professional judgment into another invisible default. Keep personalisation personal, but make consequential instructions reviewable: short, generic, free of sensitive data, periodically cleared and visible when an output is handed to someone else. Then test the work itself. The practical line is: a system that remembers your preferences has not learned your standards. Memory saves repetition; judgment still has to be renewed task by task.
