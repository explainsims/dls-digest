## Top story

**Malaysia launched a homework AI that puts parents in charge of the chat.** Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim launched AI Temu AI in Putrajaya on 25 November. The package brings together DELIMa 3.0, the latest version of the national digital learning platform, the education ministry's AI literacy guide, and Cikgu ILMU, a tool from YTL AI Labs. Anwar also listed offline AI learning resources. There's a difference between announcing a chatbot for schools and launching it alongside guidance on what children and adults are meant to do with it. Malaysia is trying the second approach. [Launch speech, 25 November](https://www.pmo.gov.my/en/speeches-en/teks-verbatim-yab-pm-peluncuran-prakarsa-ai-pendidikan-negara-ai-temu-ai/).

YTL says Cikgu ILMU will be free for Malaysian parents, with material aligned to the national curriculum from Year 1 through Form 5 in Bahasa Melayu, English, Chinese and Tamil. Parents can photograph a homework question for a worked explanation or generate a quiz that a child completes in a separate interface. The child can do the quiz without a chat window, while the system still marks the answers and reports suggested areas for practice to the adult. That division matters because it gives the adult a chance to review the material and leaves the child with something to solve. The company describes how the product works. How well the explanations, questions and marking hold up across four languages is a separate question for an evaluation. [YTL AI Labs, 25 November](https://www.ilmu.ai/news/cikgu-ilmu-launch/).

For international schools, the design is worth studying even where the local curriculum differs. Take a familiar homework problem and sketch who sees each step. The adult can inspect the explanation and decide whether to show a hint. The student can explain a first attempt. The teacher can ask whether the method matches what was taught. Each step is a chance to spot a plausible error before it becomes someone's new misconception. A four-language launch widens the audience. The quality of those handoffs will decide how much help families actually get.

## Other stories

- **A high-school class gave lower self-ratings after an AI ethics course.** Yujing Chen and Jessica Shuk Ching Leung analysed results from 45 tenth-graders in one class over ten weeks. Students examined issues including the ethics of self-driving vehicles and the consequences of automation for platform workers, mapped stakeholders and prepared policy proposals for simulated community presentations. On the researchers' questionnaire, all four dimensions of students' self-reported views fell, including how they understood the role of evidence and their sense of responsibility. Interviews with six students suggested that some had begun to give more conditional answers and consider competing interests. The authors interpret that as possible intellectual humility. One teacher, one class and no comparison group leave the cause open, and the evidence-related scale was less internally consistent than the other three. It's a reason to read what a student writes alongside the confidence rating. Ask them to show which assumption changed, then give them a different case and see whether they can recognise the same trade-off. A lower score on a survey can start a useful conversation in that setting. [Chen and Leung, 24 November](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s43031-026-00181-z).

- **US teachers' reported classroom AI use barely moved in the latest EdWeek survey.** The EdWeek Research Center surveyed 590 teachers from August to October and reports that 62% used AI in their classrooms at least a little this school year, from 61% in 2025 and 34% in 2023. It also reports that 64% had had at least one session of AI professional development by October. There's room for quite a lot of change inside those numbers. A teacher who tried a single demonstration and a teacher who regularly creates practice tasks both clear the threshold for some classroom use. The PD figure doesn't reveal whether teachers got time to trial a task, inspect the results or ask what happens if a student submits personal information. A school tracking its own progress can get more out of asking for one example from this term and a short account of what the teacher changed after using it. That gives a department something it can show a colleague who is still deciding when the tool would earn its place. [Education Week Research Center reporting, 25 November](https://www.edweek.org/technology/why-teacher-ai-use-has-plateaued-and-what-could-come-next/2026/11?view=register).

- **A Wisconsin pilot will let teachers follow small-group maths discussions across languages.** In a 23 November Wisconsin Public Radio interview, lead researcher Shamya Karumbaiah said her team plans to try its tool in six classrooms next year, with a focus on Grades 4–8. It records small-group discussion, transcribes speech and can translate it for the teacher, with models running on local servers. UW–Madison's 18 November grant announcement said the team received US$350,000 from Multilingual by Design and is extending earlier science-classroom work into maths, working with the Multilingual Learning Research Center and WIDA. The obvious use is making it easier for a teacher to hear a mathematical idea shared in a language they don't speak. The useful test is whether the tool preserves that idea when students switch languages mid-sentence or several classmates speak over one another. It matters that the teacher can go back to the group to check. Any school considering a similar recording tool would also need to know who can listen, how long the audio is kept and what the alternative looks like for a child who declines. [Wisconsin Public Radio, 23 November](https://www.wpr.org/news/pilot-program-ai-aid-teachers-language-barriers-wisconsin). [UW–Madison, 18 November](https://education.wisc.edu/news/uw-madison-education-researchers-receive-grant-to-pilot-ai-tool-for-educators-and-multilingual-learners/).

- **A field study in northern England found that guidance was often scarcer than devices.** SHINE published Ashiya Dewan's *Learning in the Age of AI* on 25 November. The report draws on summer fieldwork mainly in Middlesbrough, with additional perspectives from Sunderland and Rotherham, and includes staff interviews and pupil focus groups from Year 2 to Year 12. In participating schools, staff and pupils described widespread device access alongside uneven adult guidance. Pupils recognised that AI could be wrong and said they preferred help that showed how to approach a problem. These are accounts from a small, deliberately selected set of schools. They give a school a better question about homework than whether every student has a device. Can a student who has no one available at home see a worked example, ask for a clue and practise on similar material? For a class that relies on homework for its next lesson, the answer matters as much as the list of accounts the school pays for. [SHINE, 25 November](https://shinetrust.org.uk/2026/11/25/learning-in-the-age-of-ai/).

## Sceptic's corner

Giving an adult the chat window ought to be safer than giving a child the same window. The adult can decide which help fits, notice a bad explanation and stop the conversation. Cikgu ILMU's design gives that adult a real opportunity to intervene. It also gives the machine a second job the moment a child takes a quiz. The score and advice to the parent still come from an automated judgement. The quiet failure is easy to imagine. A child uses a valid method that the system marks wrong, so the adult spends the next evening trying to fix a misconception that belongs to the software. [YTL AI Labs, 25 November](https://www.ilmu.ai/news/cikgu-ilmu-launch/).

There's also a cost hidden inside the word *parent*. The north-of-England interviews suggest that adults' availability and confidence vary even where devices are common. Putting an adult into the design is a sensible safeguard if someone has time to do the job and the school provides a route for families who need help. A short worked example, a chance to practise at school, or a way to challenge a mark can matter more than adding another AI explanation. The test for this kind of homework tool is whether a child can show the same understanding the next day, with a new question and a human who can follow the reasoning. [SHINE, 25 November](https://shinetrust.org.uk/2026/11/25/learning-in-the-age-of-ai/).
