## Top story

**The story worth carrying into the day is the guidance gap.** A new Gallup / Walton Family Foundation survey reports that only 18% of U.S. public K-12 teachers say they have received formal guidance from school administrators on AI use, even though prior Gallup work found that six in ten teachers already use AI for work; Axios framed the same finding bluntly: "AI's education explosion leaves teachers in the dark" ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/710534/teachers-receive-no-formal-guidance.aspx), [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/27/ai-education-teacher-guidance-polling)).

For international schools, this is the bit to underline. The AI problem is no longer whether teachers will touch the tools. They already are, unevenly, privately, and with different risk thresholds. The institutional work is to turn "I tried Gemini/ChatGPT for planning" into a small set of visible, defensible norms: what data stays out, what uses are encouraged, what outputs must be checked, what student-facing uses need approval, and what counts as professional judgment rather than automation.

The practical consequence for Ed Tech Coaches is that AI guidance should not arrive as a grand policy document first. It should arrive as a usable staffroom artifact: three green-light workflows, three red lines, two example prompts, and one escalation path. Governance that teachers cannot remember becomes decoration. Governance they can use before period 3 becomes culture.

## Other stories

- **OpenAI is moving from campus deals to national education infrastructure.** OpenAI's May Education for Countries update says Estonia is leading a nationwide, research-driven ChatGPT Edu deployment, with Singapore joining the programme after work with its Ministry of Education ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/the-next-phase-of-education-for-countries/)). For schools, the interesting part is not "use OpenAI"; it is the deployment pattern: localisation, educator preparation, and research framing before mass rollout.

- **Google's AI Educator Series is now live.** Google says the first wave of 20+ ISTE+ASCD-aligned micro-trainings is available, designed as short, stackable modules for K-12 and higher-ed educators in the U.S. ([Google for Education](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-educator-series/)). Even if the programme is U.S.-framed, the format is useful: bite-sized staff learning beats one heroic workshop when teachers are already overloaded.

- **NotebookLM is becoming a workflow component, not just a study toy.** Google Workspace Updates now lets users bring NotebookLM notebooks into Workspace Studio flows as AI knowledge sources for automations, available to several education add-on tiers ([Google Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/05/notebooklm-in-workspace-studio.html)). For Google Workspace schools, this is a quiet but important shift: source-grounded notebooks can become repeatable staff workflows, not merely one-off summaries.

- **AI Studio plus Firebase is lowering the floor for internal app prototypes.** Google's Firebase team says AI Studio can now detect when a prompt-built app needs data storage or identity and offer to set up Firestore and Firebase Authentication with approval ([Firebase Blog](https://firebase.blog/posts/2026/03/announcing-ai-studio-integration/)). This matters for small school tools, but the warning label is large: prototype ease does not remove the need for ownership, auth rules, logging, data retention, and a real handoff path to IT.

- **The counter-movement is widening from phone bans to screen restraint.** Axios reports growing parent, educator, and lawmaker pressure to curb classroom screen use, with AI guardrails getting folded into a wider "less tech in class" push ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/30/no-screen-school-technology-parents-ai)). That is not anti-AI by default. It is a useful reminder that teacher AI adoption and student screen exposure are separate arguments and should be treated separately in policy.

## Sceptic's corner

The hyped claim this week is that the big AI companies are finally solving the education problem by wrapping models in teacher training, study modes, and education-branded workspaces. There is progress there. Secure workspaces matter. Source-grounded tools matter. Training matters. But the claim quietly swaps the hard problem for the easy one.

The easy problem is access: give teachers an account, a course, a prompt library, and a cheerful promise about productivity. The hard problem is institutional judgment: which uses are professionally acceptable, what student data may enter which system, how teachers disclose or verify AI-assisted work, and how a school prevents "AI saves time" from becoming "teachers now absorb more invisible work."

So the useful posture is neither blocking nor cheerleading. Treat vendor education products as raw material, not policy. A school that cannot explain its norms without naming a product does not yet have AI governance; it has procurement with a nicer brochure.
