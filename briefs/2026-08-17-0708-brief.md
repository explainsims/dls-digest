## Top story

**The spreadsheet has quietly become an application builder.** Google’s Sheets canvas, announced on 13 August, allows a user to ask Gemini for an interactive interface over a Sheet using natural language. A Kanban board, dashboard or whiteboard can be generated without formulas or conventional development. More consequentially, the result is read-write: moving a card or adding an entry changes the underlying cells immediately, and edits in the Sheet appear in the canvas. The mini-app inherits the Sheet’s existing sharing settings ([Google Workspace Updates, 13 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/use-google-sheets-canvas-to-visualize-data.html?hl=en)).

That makes a useful capability accessible to people who would never open an app builder. A teacher could turn a class dataset into an interactive exploration; a department could put a usable front end over a planning grid; a school team could make a lightweight tracker without exporting data to another service. Yet the same ease removes the moment when somebody normally notices that a document has become software. A generated control is not merely showing information when dragging it rewrites the source, and inherited sharing is only as sound as the Sheet’s previous permissions.

The interface can also change how collaborators interpret authority. Cells look provisional; a polished tracker can look like an approved system even when both sit over identical data and logic. That perception matters in schools, where a prototype can rapidly become the place colleagues record a decision simply because it is convenient. Visual finish should not be allowed to outrun ownership or validation.

The feature is available by default wherever Gemini in Sheets is enabled, requires Workspace smart features, begins with English-language web accounts, and carries per-user usage limits. A sensible school pilot should therefore use copied, disposable data and test the unglamorous edges: permissions, accidental writes, formulas, accessibility and change history. Test an imperfect Sheet with protected ranges, formulas, merged cells and mixed access; clean data conceal dangerous writes. Sheets canvas deserves attention because it can widen who gets to design useful data interactions. It also widens who can create an application without realising that application governance now applies.

## Other stories

- **Canvas scheduled a new learner notebook to switch on globally on Saturday.** Notebook had been available but off since 18 July; it was due to become enabled by default for K–12 and higher-education customers across Canvas Core on 15 August. The cited product page documents that schedule, not a post-rollout production check. Learners can highlight course content, attach notes and reactions, and organise them by course without leaving the LMS ([Instructure, updated for 15 August](https://community.instructure.com/en/discussion/666428/notebook-for-learners-q3-2026-feature-overview)). Administrators can disable it, but the timing prompted objections because the default was set for a Saturday near the start of term; Instructure later supplied an advance opt-out form. Notes cannot currently be downloaded, and community tests found that annotations may become detached when source text changes. That limits portability and makes it unclear what a learner retains when a course closes or content is revised. The broader platform lesson is precise: administrative control after launch is not the same thing as an institution choosing when a learner-facing feature becomes part of teaching.

- **Google Meet can now transcribe people in the same physical room without a video call.** Eligible users can open Meet on web or mobile, start an in-person note-taking session, and let Gemini capture room audio. When the session ends, it produces a structured summary, action list and full transcript in a Drive document and emails the link. Android rollout began 11 August and web rollout no earlier than 14 August; Google AI Pro for Education is among the eligible editions, with access governed by existing Meet notes settings ([Google Workspace Updates, 13 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/take-notes-with-me-for-in-person-meetings-is-now-available.html?hl=en)). This changes the practical boundary of recording policy. A participant may reasonably understand a video call to be recordable yet not expect a phone on the table to create a durable transcript. Schools need to cover notice, consent, retention and access in face-to-face conferences, support discussions and family meetings, not only in scheduled video calls.

- **Colorado districts are drawing different developmental boundaries around the same tools.** Douglas County has tested classroom AI for roughly three years and uses Gemini, Gemini Notebook and Classroom. Littleton blocks district AI access for K–8 learners but allows approved Google tools in high school, while Denver has offered monitored access to several assistants. State survey data show 54% of responding teachers and school-based staff felt comfortable with AI, but only 32% felt prepared for integration changes ([Axios, 13 August](https://www.axios.com/local/douglas/2026/08/13/colorado-schools-classroom-ai-rules); [Colorado Department of Education](https://www.cde.state.co.us/site/tlccsurvey/)). Comfort and readiness are different constructs, and neither percentage establishes the right age threshold. The gap also warns against using staff sentiment as a proxy for implementation capacity: familiarity with a tool does not produce curriculum, assessment rules or child-safety practice. The useful approach is to make age-and-task boundaries explicit, examine classroom evidence and revise them instead of treating a single account setting as an educational policy.

- **Manchester is budgeting for adoption around the licence.** The University of Manchester is rolling Microsoft 365 Copilot out to 65,000 staff and students, including agents inside Word, Excel and PowerPoint. Student access is planned from September or October, with full rollout by the end of 2026. Almost 3,000 colleagues attended live learning in an April cohort; reported confidence rose from 24% beforehand to 80% afterwards. Policies were revised with staff networks and the Student Union, and the deployment remains inside existing Microsoft permissions ([ITPro, 11 August](https://www.itpro.com/business/digital-transformation/manchester-university-from-ai-initiators-to-ai-integrators)). Self-reported confidence is not proof of safe practice, while existing permissions can preserve existing oversharing. The rollout also needs outcome measures beyond attendance and confidence: whether people choose appropriate tasks, protect information and recognise unreliable output. Even so, the scale makes one cost visible: enterprise access requires staged cohorts, training, support and policy work. A licence quote that omits those lines is not a full implementation price.

- **A new child-safety study argues that refusal is a poor proxy for a safe chatbot response.** Researchers interviewed 19 practitioners working with young people in vulnerable situations: five social workers, five educators, three researchers, two psychologists, two therapists and two advocates. Participants examined chatbot responses to risky scenarios derived from earlier empirical work and identified behaviours that could harm or support young people. The authors argue that common safety evaluations overemphasise adversarial prompts and surface harms, and often assume refusal is the correct outcome without validating that assumption in real situations ([Cha and colleagues, 8 August](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07902)). This is a small qualitative study, not a model ranking, and the abstract does not establish how consistently practitioners agreed. Its contribution is a sharper evaluation question: who defined the appropriate response, which youth experiences informed the test, and what useful pathway remains after a chatbot declines to help? A system can avoid saying something prohibited while still abandoning a young user at the exact point support is needed.

## On the radar

- **Google Calendar has gained a useful security control with a permissive default.** Workspace administrators can now limit which invitation behaviours users may select, including accepting only known senders or requiring an email response first. Settings can vary by organisational unit or group, but the administrative default remains invitations from everyone ([Google Workspace Updates, 14 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/new-admin-controls-for-adding-invitations-to-Google-Calendar.html?hl=en)). Schools should evaluate the setting deliberately across staff and learner groups as it reaches their domains; the presence of a control does not itself reduce risk.

- **Canvas Notebook’s accessibility edge remains unresolved.** Community testers report that its highlight colours can make light text on dark course pages unreadable, while other tests found stale annotations after highlighted content moved or disappeared. Instructure has acknowledged the contrast report in the product thread, and administrators can disable Notebook by account or subaccount ([Instructure, August](https://community.instructure.com/en/discussion/666428/notebook-for-learners-q3-2026-feature-overview)). A scheduled default-on change should not outrank local accessibility checks on styled course pages before broad use, especially when the feature is explicitly presented as supporting effective study habits.

- **Child-safety evaluation may be moving from blocking bad answers to designing useful ones.** The practitioner study is scheduled for AIES 2026 and calls for safety infrastructure shaped by people who work directly with vulnerable young people ([Cha and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07902)). The practical next step would be an open, age-banded scenario set with expert rationales, disagreement records and escalation pathways that schools could inspect during vendor review. A single red-team score would erase the context the research identifies as missing, including the difference between silence and a genuinely supportive handoff.

## Sceptic's corner

The easy response to Sheets canvas is celebration: no-code development has moved into a tool almost every organisation already knows. That assessment is substantially right. It is also what makes the feature easy to misclassify.

Conventional app development is conspicuous. Somebody starts a project, selects a data source, requests permissions, tests a release and becomes responsible for its behaviour. Sheets canvas compresses the data, generated interface and collaboration boundary into one familiar file. Inheriting the Sheet’s sharing settings sounds safe because no new audience is added. But inheritance preserves a configuration; it does not establish that the old configuration suits a more browsable, more operational and fully read-write interface.

Natural-language creation does not eliminate technical consequences either. A card moved in a generated workflow can overwrite a formula, trigger an automation or change a status that colleagues treat as authoritative. The polish of the interface may lend more confidence to the underlying logic than it has earned. Requiring every classroom experiment to pass through a software committee would waste the best part of the release: teachers and learners can build useful data interactions quickly.

The proportionate boundary is consequence. A private prototype using disposable information can move fast. The moment a canvas becomes shared, read-write or connected to records, approvals, safeguarding, money or live operations, it should acquire an owner, a permission review and a test copy. The cost of making software has fallen again. The cost of an incorrect write, an over-broad share or an inaccessible interface has not.
