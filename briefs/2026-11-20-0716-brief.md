## Top story

**An AI maths tutor helped adults practise fixing their own mistakes.** In a [15 November Carnegie Mellon preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20061), Michael Asher, Meng Cao and Paulo Carvalho randomly assigned 400 adults who had missed a screening question on setting up a constraint equation to one of four kinds of feedback. After a wrong answer, they saw the correct answer, got a generic explanation, got a personalised AI explanation, or worked through the error with a tutor that asked follow-up questions. In the last group, participants corrected 131 of 206 errors during the dialogue. On post-test questions aligned with the skills they had practised, that group scored nine percentage points higher (0.24 standard deviations, p = .048) than the average of the two explanation groups. The researchers found no reliable advantage on the other post-test questions. The coached practice also took about 13 minutes, against about seven for the other conditions.

A [13 November experiment in the British Journal of Educational Technology](https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjet.70100?af=R) put a different kind of AI support in front of 16- to 18-year-olds at an English sixth-form college. Megan Ennion and Ros McLellan analysed 149 students assigned to Khanmigo Lite, an in-person maths tutor or unguided web search for deliberately above-curriculum problems. In the second set of problems, the AI group reported more effort and greater difficulty than the human-tutored group. The human-tutored group attempted the most questions in both sets, with a statistically significant gap versus AI in the first set only. The authors treat the section-level findings as exploratory. An attempt counted when a student started a problem, so this study tells us about engagement with difficulty and leaves learning gains unmeasured.

For international schools, the two papers give a maths department a sharper question than whether to permit an AI tutor. Does the activity require a first attempt, help the learner locate a specific error and get them to try again? A polished explanation can sit in the chat while the student moves on. A corrected line of working followed by a fresh problem gives the teacher something they can actually inspect.

## Other stories

- **Brightspace made teacher instructions take precedence in AI feedback.** [Minnesota State scheduled the 20.26.11 Brightspace update for 19 November](https://www.minnstate.edu/system/ims/index.html). In the [release notes, with the Lumi Feedback changes added on 5 November](https://community.d2l.com/brightspace/kb/articles/35700-november-2026-20-26-11), D2L says teacher-level instructions now take priority over administrator-level instructions in its paid feedback add-on. Automated assessment uses its own custom instructions. Regenerating feedback builds on the earlier output and leaves the grade in place unless the submission or rubric changes. The same release adds support for HEIC and HEIF submissions, a mundane change with an audience in any school where students photograph their working on Apple devices. The hierarchy is the bigger story. A school-wide default and a rule that teachers cannot override are two distinct product capabilities. Separate settings for feedback and automated assessment also mean a single test cannot tell you how both behave. A chemistry teacher may need feedback that reflects the discipline and leaves room for a second attempt. A school may also need a rule that stops feedback from disclosing a model answer. For Ed Tech Coaches working across learning platforms, this is a concrete scenario for an LMS walkthrough. A demo with all the instructions pointing in the same direction will never show who wins when they conflict.

- **The EU put the curriculum and the assessment on the same AI agenda.** On [19 November the Council of the EU approved conclusions on AI skills and competences](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/11/19/equipping-europeans-for-the-age-of-ai-council-approves-conclusions/). The [adopted text](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-15402-2026-INIT/en/pdf) invites member states to draw on existing resources, including DigComp 3.0 and the primary and secondary AI literacy framework, when updating curricula, learning programmes and assessment. It couples that work with initial and continuing teacher education, age-appropriate whole-school policy and evaluation of effects on existing inequalities. It also asks the Commission to build on the school-age framework for other stages of life. The assessment language matters because the same syllabus can require confident use of a tool in one task and reliable independent performance in another. The tension worth taking into a curriculum meeting is between practising with AI and seeing what students can do independently. A class can reasonably have students ask an assistant to critique a claim, then ask them to explain which criticism they accepted and why under conditions that make their own reasoning visible. Buying the same assistant across departments cannot decide where each of those assessments belongs.

- **Nigeria put a readiness check before the AI deployment.** Nigeria's communications ministry [reported the unveiling of the national EdTech strategy on 17 November](https://fmcide.gov.ng/fg-global-partners-drive-nigerias-digital-education-transformation/). The same launch introduced AskFME, a public information chatbot, and a public portal for the national education data infrastructure. The [strategy's AI governance annex](https://education.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Nigeria_National_EdTech_Strategy-Nov-2026.pdf) sets out proportionate checks on infrastructure, teacher capacity, data, governance and technical support. For AI-enabled products, it sets out an assurance path from establishing an educational need through impact and privacy assessments, a pilot and independent evaluation before scaling. It also keeps human judgement in high-impact decisions, including learner assessment and disciplinary action, with routes to challenge and correct AI-influenced decisions. The strategy explicitly gives a literacy activity or controlled pilot a different readiness threshold from an advanced service that relies on substantial data. That's a sensible distinction for an international school. A lesson on how training examples influence a classifier can run without feeding a system anything about the students in the room. A tool recommending who needs intervention is a much heavier proposition. Putting both through one generic AI approval form would conceal most of the work attached to the second.

- **Anthropic's first trade put a price on expectations.** Anthropic [priced 980 million shares at US$83 each on 18 November](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-prices-initial-public-offering). It was selling about 956 million of those itself, which implies roughly US$79.4 billion gross to the company at the offer price, before costs and assuming completion. The offering was scheduled to close on 20 November subject to the customary conditions. [Nasdaq reported](https://www.nasdaq.com/newsroom/anthropic-marks-ipo-at-nasdaq) that shares began trading on 19 November and opened at US$111.50. The numbers describe expectations about the company as a whole. For a school buying a service built on Claude, the more interesting bit of paper is the contract with the service provider. What happens to a year of teacher-created materials if the quota changes, the vendor swaps models or the school moves to another product? Two classroom products can call the same model and offer very different answers. A usable export and a commitment on price and access for the term tell procurement more about continuity than the first trade does.

## On the radar

- **Sorsogon has a December AI literacy activity for Grades 4–12.** A [19 November memo from the Philippine Department of Education's Sorsogon division](https://www.depedsorsogon.com.ph/files/issuances/261119154905_%5BUM%5D%20Devcon%20Kids.pdf) relays a voluntary DEVCON Kids programme for the 7–18 December end-of-term block. The facilitator plan offers pair work and a single-projector option. One exercise has learners train a classifier to distinguish fish from rubbish, then examine mistakes. A [19 November UNESCO Santiago teacher workshop](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/online-workshop-teachers-dive-ai-hands-experience-learn-about-artificial-intelligence-and-bring-it) was scheduled around the same activity, with adaptations for different subjects and limited connectivity. The thing to look for in December is what learners can explain about a misclassification after the exercise. A completed activity and an explanation of the model's error tell a teacher different things.

- **The EU's child-safety proposal is due for its first ministerial policy debate.** The Council's [16 November background brief](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/media/naabgjir/background-brief-eycs-november-2026-en-final.pdf) lays out the draft EU KIDS Act ahead of the [20 November agenda](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/meetings/eycs/2026/11/20/). The draft includes safety-by-design duties for AI chatbots and companions as well as other services. Its staged account ages of 13 and 15 concern social media and video-sharing platforms. The Irish presidency is due to present a progress report, and negotiations are ongoing. For schools comparing the same product on a managed account and a home account, watch which protections attach to the service itself and which depend on how a young user signs in.

## Sceptic's corner

The tempting inference from the Carnegie Mellon result is that an AI tutor becomes effective once it learns to ask questions. Look at what was bundled into that condition. The adults got additional attempts, prompts tied to their immediate errors and nearly twice the practice time. The comparison groups got feedback and moved on to the next problem. In the tutoring group, the system also had a stopping rule. If the learner still had no correct equation after six tutor replies, it provided and explained the answer. In the [authors' results](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20061), the advantage appeared on questions closely related to what the adults had practised, and personalising an explanation by itself did not reliably improve performance. The study supports the package of guided correction as tested. It leaves open how much of the gain a teacher could reproduce with a pencil, a carefully chosen hint and six more minutes.

There is a straightforward way to see the difference at school. Give both groups the same time and a plausible way to get help. Let one revise with a prepared hint and the other use the tutor. Watch whether they can identify what went wrong and fix it. Give both groups a new problem without help a few days later, including an item that needs the same idea in a different setting. The busy chat, the quick fix and the delayed answer each report something different. The first two can tell a teacher where to look. The last is where a claim about learning has to earn its keep.
