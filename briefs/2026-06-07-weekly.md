**Friday, 5 June pickup:** a new NPR/Ipsos poll sharpened the disagreement inside schools. Among 545 US K-12 teachers, 74% said AI has greater implications than previous technological changes. Fifty-nine percent said it is eroding teacher-student trust and 57% said it makes students' knowledge harder to assess. At the same time, 78% want responsible AI use in the curriculum, while only 33% report a formal student-use policy ([Ipsos](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/teachers-concerned-about-impact-ai-students-critical-thinking)).

## What Does It All Mean?

The important question is no longer whether students should use AI in the abstract. It is what kind of performance an assignment is supposed to reveal.

Most school guidance begins as an acceptable-use framework: named platforms, age restrictions, protected data, disclosure requirements, and prohibited behaviour. All of that matters. It does not solve the assessment problem in the Ipsos findings. Teachers lose confidence when a finished product no longer distinguishes independent understanding from skilful use of support or effortless generation.

Teacher and student adoption are also moving at different speeds. Ipsos found that 62% of teachers use AI for work, but only 23% use it during lessons at least weekly; 69% of teacher users say it improves their productivity. Gallup's May research found that just 18% had received formal administrative guidance on workplace AI use ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/710534/teachers-receive-no-formal-guidance.aspx)). AI is becoming ordinary in preparation and administration before schools have settled what its use should mean for learning evidence.

Research suggests that the better organising unit is the learning task, not the product. Stanford's 2026 review identified only 20 high-quality causal studies among more than 800 AI-and-K-12 papers. Students often performed better with AI available, but their later unaided results were mixed. Systems designed to offer hints and guide reasoning appeared more promising than general-purpose chatbots that supplied answers ([Stanford SCALE](https://scale.stanford.edu/research-in-action/understanding-evidence-base-ai-k12-education)). The OECD similarly recommends developing knowledge and skills without GenAI, with educational GenAI, and then with general-purpose GenAI ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html)).

A workable response is a three-mode contract attached to school tasks:

1. **Baseline mode:** AI is absent. The task establishes what a student can retrieve, reason through, explain, or create independently.
2. **Scaffold mode:** AI may question, hint, translate, model feedback, or identify possible errors, but it may not supply the final reasoning or artefact. The student's process remains visible.
3. **Open mode:** AI can be a full collaborator because the learning target includes orchestration, verification, critique, or production with AI. Consequential uses are disclosed and claims remain the student's responsibility.

Four small labels can make each mode usable: the evidence being collected, permitted support, required process record, and protected data. That is brief enough to appear on an assignment and stable enough to survive product changes.

For international schools, this is more durable than beginning with a catalogue of approved prompts. Ed Tech Coaches can help departments interpret the modes for different subjects while keeping a common language across the school. A mathematics proof, a laboratory analysis, a language translation, and a media project need not share identical rules, but each should state whether the school is measuring independent performance, supported learning, or capable AI collaboration.

AI policy should therefore do more than control access. It should preserve the validity of the inference a teacher makes from student work.

## Behind You! Behind You!

**Utah has made Gemini a statewide option rather than a single-district experiment.** The Utah State Board of Education says the 2026-27 initiative could reach about 680,000 students and 28,000 educators, with local agencies retaining the choice to participate. It combines managed accounts, an AI-literacy curriculum, teacher training, and assurances that conversations will not be used to train models ([USBE, updated 3 June](https://schools.utah.gov/publicrelations/_publicrelations_/_2026/USBEAdoptsGeminiEducationPressRelease.pdf)). It is a substantial implementation case, though its professional learning and evidence model are closely tied to one provider.

**A new risk review offers caution with its own caution label.** Researchers synthesised 22 empirical K-12 studies and organised reported risks around wellbeing, intellectual agency, and institutional readiness. Their practical recommendations include scaffolded use, process assessment, AI literacy, and teacher development. They also note that the evidence base is small, heterogeneous, geographically concentrated, and not formally quality-appraised ([Computers & Education: Artificial Intelligence](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666920X26000226)).

**AI literacy for young children need not mean independent chatbot use.** In a small design-based study, four pre-K and kindergarten teachers co-designed seven activities around unplugged play, tangible coding, and guided comparison with a social robot ([study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666920X26000317)). The finding should not be inflated, but the design principle travels: learning how AI works can be separated from increasing young children's exposure to generative systems.

## Sceptic's Corner

This week's claim to press is **"AI is destroying critical thinking."**

Teachers' concerns are operational evidence, even when they are not causal evidence. The Ipsos results describe weakened confidence in authorship, assessment, and classroom trust. High-school teachers were more negative than elementary teachers. Treating that as simple technophobia would be unserious.

It would be equally unserious to turn a perception poll into proof of cognitive decline. The sample was 545 US teachers and the overall margin of sampling error was plus or minus five percentage points. Stanford's review says the causal literature is still sparse. A recent meta-analysis of 34 K-12 studies found a moderate positive average effect for AI agents, but higher-order-thinking results were not statistically significant, upper-secondary effects were negligible, and almost half of the observed effects were negligible or negative ([meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958826000473)).

The contradiction is the finding. "AI" is not a coherent classroom intervention. A hinting tutor, a general answer generator, a teacher planning assistant, and a relationship-simulating bot have different designs, users, and risks.

The sensible conclusion is precise: AI can improve performance while support is present, but that does not establish durable independent learning. A better AI-assisted answer demonstrates what a learner and a system produced together. It does not, by itself, demonstrate what the learner can now do.
