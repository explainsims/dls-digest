## Top story

**Google has put Gemini in charge of deciding which Drive files are sensitive.** On 28 August, Google opened Gemini-based data classification in Drive to supported Workspace customers. Instead of training a classifier on files that administrators first label by hand, an admin writes instructions, chooses the label and scopes the files; Gemini interprets those instructions and applies labels. Owners and editors with the right permissions may accept or change a machine-applied label, while audit logs record the original classification and any human modification. The open beta is rolling out through 30 September for Enterprise Plus, Frontline Plus and Google AI Pro for Education ([Google Workspace Updates, 28 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/gemini-based-data-classification-in-Google-Drive-is-now-available-in-open-beta.html)).

The security logic matters more than the convenience. Labels can drive data-loss-prevention rules, retention and audit investigations; Google explicitly positions the feature as a way to stop agentic workflows reaching or acting on sensitive material. That makes classification part of the AI permissions layer, not merely records housekeeping. In a school Drive, the distinction between an ordinary planning document and counselling, safeguarding, admissions or assessment material is exactly the distinction an agent must not infer casually. Availability matters too: Education Standard and Plus are not listed unless paired with Google AI Pro for Education, so a school cannot assume the control follows merely from having a paid education edition.

For international schools, this is promising control-plane technology with a familiar catch: the same model family now helps decide what the model family may reach. Human review exists, but Google does not publish school-specific accuracy, error rates or a confusion matrix for these categories. Before treating it as protection, test it against a labelled corpus that contains the ugly edge cases—student names in filenames, copied tables, scans, multilingual records and deceptively bland titles. Measure false negatives separately from false positives. A missed confidential file is a privacy failure; an over-labelled curriculum folder is a workflow problem. Those are not interchangeable errors, and an attractive coverage percentage can hide the first inside the second.

## Other stories

- **Discovery Education has put a Gemini retrieval layer inside its Google Classroom add-on.** The teacher-facing interface, announced 31 August, lets a teacher describe a topic, grade, standard, reading level or available time and retrieve material from the district’s licensed Discovery collection without leaving Classroom. It enters an Early Feedback Program with selected US districts this autumn; Elko County School District is named, and the available collections include Experience, Science Techbook and two Social Studies products. Discovery says results respect existing district content-governance settings and describes its own security and compliance credentials, but supplies no retrieval-accuracy, standards-alignment or teacher-time data for the new interface ([Discovery Education, 31 August 2026](https://www.discoveryeducation.com/details/discovery-education-launches-conversational-ai-in-google-classroom-add-on-connecting-teachers-to-trusted-k-12-content/)). The architecture is worth watching in international schools: constrain generation with licensed curricular content and meet teachers inside the LMS. The acceptance test is not whether the chat sounds grounded; it is whether sampled results actually point to the right source, standard, age and licence boundary. Require a visible source trail as well: a response grounded somewhere in an approved library is weaker than a claim a teacher can open and inspect.

- **OpenAI expanded ChatGPT for Teachers to 55 school systems across 20 US states, covering more than 100,000 additional educators and staff.** Its 26 August announcement says signed General Offers now cover 15 states, with California under a separate agreement, and that district leaders can move educators into managed workspaces with role-based controls. OpenAI reports that data in those workspaces is not used to train its models by default. It also reports more than 1.9 million educator messages about time-saving tasks between 1 January and 16 July: 900,000 on reports and progress, 800,000 on lesson planning, and more than 100,000 each on substitute plans and teacher evaluation ([OpenAI, 26 August 2026](https://openai.com/index/bringing-chatgpt-for-teachers-to-more-us-school-districts/)). These are vendor telemetry and partnership counts, not evidence of time saved or learning improved; the announcement gives no independent evaluation, uptake distribution or rejected-use record. Still, the procurement move is real: adoption is shifting from individual teacher accounts toward statewide legal terms, managed identity and implementation support—the level at which an international school should compare an AI service.

- **Ask Gemini in Google Chat turns a messaging surface into a cross-Workspace command line.** Rolling out from 26 August to Google AI Pro for Education and eligible business and enterprise plans, it can search Gmail, Drive and Calendar; summarize conversations; capture actions; create content; schedule meetings; and manage tasks. It replaces the existing Gemini side panel in Chat for English-language accounts, but old side-panel conversation history will not migrate. Administrators can export that history, and exports place it under “Gemini in Workspace,” not “Google Chat.” The feature is on by default when Gemini in Chat and Workspace Intelligence across apps are enabled; users must also enrol in Workspace Smart Features. Promotional higher limits end on 1 October, after which limits will apply ([Google Workspace Updates, 19 August; rollout 26 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/ask-gemini-in-chat.html)). For Ed Tech Coaches, a professional-learning demo should show the access boundary, export trail and post-promotion limit alongside the clever query. Cross-app usefulness is cross-app exposure by design, and the name of the interface is a poor guide to the data it can reach.

- **OpenAI says ChatGPT hosts up to 70 million weekly conversations devoted to testing knowledge.** Its 26 August “continuous learning” report also says US classwork and homework prompts peak above 460 million messages a week during the school year, rise on Sunday evenings and remain above 180 million a week in summer. The company describes the underlying work as privacy-preserving analysis and presents examples such as misconception checks, extra practice, differentiated assignments and translated family communication ([OpenAI, 26 August 2026](https://openai.com/index/learning-never-stops/)). Scale is the story; efficacy is not. Conversations are not learners, prompts are not completed tasks, and the published page does not give sampling, classification, error, user denominators or outcome detail sufficient to turn message counts into a learning claim. For international schools, this is useful evidence that after-hours AI help is already normal behaviour. It strengthens the case for teaching students how to verify and disengage, but it cannot tell us whether any of those millions retained knowledge without the tool.

- **Two teacher educators have offered a better starting sequence than “prompt engineering first.”** University of Delaware researchers Stephanie Smith Budhai and Rachel Karchmer-Klein argue that teachers should begin with purpose, foundational literacy and the system’s limits. Their 31 August guidance proposes comparing thin and contextual prompts, checking responses against other sources, inspecting generated images for missing groups, and comparing peer feedback with AI feedback. Budhai calls the risk of bypassing foundational work “never-skilling”; Karchmer-Klein’s formulation is that AI may be a problem-solver but is also a problem to solve ([University of Delaware, 31 August 2026](https://www.udel.edu/udaily/2026/august/artificial-intelligence-classroom-budhai-karchmer-klein/)). This is expert guidance rather than a tested intervention, and some accompanying claims about cognitive harm outrun the evidence presented on the page. The sequence is nevertheless teacher-usable: establish what learners must first be able to do, expose failure modes, then let AI into a task where comparison and judgement remain visible. Judge the PD by changed task design and later student performance, not teacher confidence at the door.

- **UNESCO’s Digital Learning Week will put procurement and sovereignty beside pedagogy.** From 8–11 September, more than 25 education ministers and over 1,000 in-person participants are due in Paris for a joint statement on education as a common good in the age of AI. UNESCO says it will publish policy briefs on safety, sovereignty, procurement, cost models and age-appropriateness, plus new survey data on AI in higher education ([UNESCO, 25 August 2026](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/education-age-ai-ministerial-statement-be-adopted-unescos-digital-learning-week?hub=80094)). The briefs—not the ministerial prose—may give international schools a useful counterweight to vendor-authored governance templates.

- **Kazakhstan’s deadline for national school-AI standards is today.** A presidential decree called for standards for AI in secondary education, plus a continuous professional-development plan, by 1 September; the programme also covers personalised learning, infrastructure, teacher preparation and student-data protection, while specifying that AI must not replace teachers ([Government of Kazakhstan](https://www.gov.kz/memleket/entities/mfa-stockholm/press/news/details/1222864?lang=en)). The English government page still states the promise rather than linking the resulting instruments. Watch for the actual standards, their assessment rules and whether the publication catches up with the deadline.

## Sceptic's corner

Automated classification sounds like the missing answer to the data-governance problem: label everything, attach DLP, and let the agents operate only inside the green zone. Google’s new Drive beta makes that architecture tangible, with administrator instructions, user correction and audit logging ([Google Workspace Updates, 28 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/gemini-based-data-classification-in-Google-Drive-is-now-available-in-open-beta.html)). It would be easy to confuse that machinery with a decision.

A label does not know why a file exists, whether its contents were lawfully collected, whether today’s user should combine it with another dataset, or whether a generated action is educationally appropriate. It can govern reach; it cannot supply purpose. Ask Gemini makes the distinction sharper because a single surface can search across Gmail, Drive and Calendar and then take actions ([Google Workspace Updates, 19 August; rollout 26 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/ask-gemini-in-chat.html)). Better discovery makes a wrong classification more consequential, not less.

The answer is not to dismiss automated labels. At school scale, manual classification will be incomplete before lunch. Use the model for coverage, but require a human-owned taxonomy, a gold-set test, separate false-negative reporting, periodic drift checks and hard exclusions for the highest-risk collections. Then keep task authorization above file authorization: access to a correctly labelled document is not permission to summarize, merge or act on it.

The staffroom line is blunt: classification can tell the agent where the fence is. It cannot decide who should open the gate.
