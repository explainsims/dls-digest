## Top story

**Reusable AI agents are becoming governable school objects. That is the shift to watch.**

OpenAI's 28 May Enterprise/Edu release notes add new controls for ChatGPT workspace agents: model choice, reasoning-effort settings, role-based publishing permissions, guided setup, speech output, and smarter Slack-thread behaviour. The same release stream says workspace agents are now generally available for Business, Enterprise, and Edu, with admin visibility and action safeguards. ([OpenAI Help Center](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/10128477-chatgpt-enterprise-edu-release-notes))

This is not a procurement recommendation. It is a category signal. The object schools will need to govern is no longer just the chatbot conversation. It is the reusable agent: who built it, what sources it can see, which actions it can take, whether it can be published to colleagues, whether students ever touch it, and when it expires.

Google is moving in the same direction from the other side. At I/O, Google said AI Studio will connect directly to Workspace data, build tools over Sheets and Drive, deploy early apps to Google Cloud, and support managed agents through the Gemini API. ([Google I/O 2026](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/ai/google-io-2026-all-our-announcements/)) For international schools and Ed Tech Coaches, the immediate move is not "everyone builds agents." It is: define the approval shape before the first useful prototype becomes folk infrastructure.

- **AFT has put age bands and screen limits into the AI-in-schools argument.** Randi Weingarten's 10-point plan calls for no screens, including online assessments, for pre-K through grade 2; no student-facing AI in elementary school; supervised student-facing AI beyond that; and no social-companion chatbots under 16. ([AFT press release](https://www.aft.org/press-release/devices-down-eyes-hands-weingarten-calls-screen-bans-ai-limits-active-learning-major), [speech PDF](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/2026/weingarten_npc_speech-may-27-2026.pdf)) It is US-union politics, yes, but the categories travel: elementary, secondary, companion, supervised, unsupervised.

- **Brookings is arguing that companion bots need a public-health frame, not just product warnings.** Its May policy brief treats companion bots as products that simulate human connection, can retain memory, and are increasingly embedded in mainstream platforms. ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/From-bans-to-recalls-A-public-health-framework-for-AI-companion-bots-1.pdf)) This gives schools evidence for the line between learning assistant and relationship simulator.

- **CoSN's 2026 State of EdTech report is blunt on capacity.** More districts report AI guidelines than last year, but cybersecurity remains the top priority; 58% say they are understaffed for instructional technology support; and procurement still overweights safety while underweighting accessibility, evidence, usability, and interoperability. ([CoSN](https://www.cosn.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/U.S.-State-of-EdTech-2026.pdf)) Translation: governance documents are arriving faster than the people needed to make them real.

- **Google's AI Educator Series is now live.** The first wave has 20+ micro-training sessions, built with ISTE+ASCD and framed as short, stackable AI literacy training. ([Google for Education](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-educator-series/)) This is usable as baseline professional-learning language, not as the whole professional-learning plan. It tells schools what Google thinks "AI-ready educator" should sound like in public.

- **Anthropic raised another enormous round, and the school implication is procurement weather.** Anthropic says its latest funding is meant to expand compute, research, and enterprise products as Claude becomes more embedded in work. ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/series-h)) The takeaway for schools is dull but important: the major AI vendors are capitalised to push into every workflow. Local governance has to assume persistence, not a passing fad.

## Sceptic's corner

The phrase to squeeze today is **"governed agent."**

It sounds clean. It sounds like a permission screen has solved the problem. But a governed agent is only governed if the institution can answer the boring questions after the demo glow wears off: who owns it, what does it know, who can publish it, what logs exist, what happens when the source document changes, how a bad response gets reported, and whether it is quietly becoming a workaround for a process nobody wanted to fix.

That matters because the useful agents will not look dangerous. They will look helpful: a rubric explainer, a meeting-note summariser, a parent-email drafter, a course-resource finder, a spreadsheet checker. Fine. Use them. But name them as institutional objects, not clever prompts. Once an agent is shared, scheduled, connected to Drive, or allowed to act in another app, it has crossed the line from personal productivity into school infrastructure.

The vendor roadmaps are clear enough now. Agents will be cheap to make and easy to share. The adult move is to make the registry before the sprawl, not after the first department has accidentally built a shadow platform.
