## Top story

**A writing chatbot drew more talk about ideas.** In a [5 November preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.07792v1), Lucile Favero and colleagues report an exploratory classroom study with 65 students from Theory of Knowledge classes at four Swiss IB schools. Students drafted an argument independently and were randomly assigned to one of two chatbots for revision. Both used the same model and interface, gave students separate modes for thinking and writing, and were instructed to leave the actual drafting to the student. MAIKE added a way to diagnose an argument, ask a question about a specific weakness, and revisit the issue after the student revised.

The difference shows up in what students talked about. In the coded dialogue, 56% of turns with MAIKE were about ideas, compared with 44% in the comparison group. Among the 62 students included in the essay-score analysis, 12 of 30 using MAIKE improved their combined score for cogency, rhetorical effectiveness and rebuttal, alongside 13 of 32 using the other tutor. More moves from discussion of an idea to a substantive edit were associated with higher gains within the MAIKE group. That last result describes a pattern among students in one condition. It is a lead for the next experiment, since students were assigned a tutor and chose how to use it.

For Theory of Knowledge teachers, the most practical detail is the return from question to draft. The exchange worth looking for is the one that changes what a student can defend. Imagine a draft arguing that a new technology makes people more independent. The tutor asks which people, in what circumstances, and what would count as a counterexample. The student narrows the claim and explains the limit in their own words. The draft gives a teacher something specific to ask about, even if the conversation that prompted the revision stays with the student. A transcript can be long, fluent and of very little consequence to the argument.

## Other stories

- **OpenAI put the control group back in the picture.** On 6 November, OpenAI and biotechnology company Ligo [introduced BioBasicsBench](https://openai.com/index/measuring-how-ai-agents-handle-wet-lab-science/), 100 tasks involving raw lab evidence, from images of gels to microscope footage. At the xhigh reasoning setting, GPT-6 Astra averaged 66.7% of available credit and GPT-5.6 Sol 43.8%. One example shows a negative control plate with no colonies. The older model relied on their absence, while Astra noticed the label named the wrong antibiotic and rejected the control. In a separate cloning example, Astra grouped the bands correctly but overrode the experimental comparison with an assumption about which PCR product should be larger, selecting the wrong colonies. For a secondary science department, the two examples are a better pair than either benchmark score. Give students a model interpretation and the source image, and ask them to point to the piece of evidence that licenses each conclusion. The label on the rim of a plate can matter more than the most convincing sentence in the explanation.

- **A database agent scored higher when it could carry notes across sessions.** In a [6 November engineering report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/components-of-agent-memory), Anthropic describes a database task in which an agent answers one question, receives the correct solution and can save notes for a later session. On that task, Claude Opus 5.5's mean reward was 0.838 with the memory store and 0.655 without it. Some earlier models kept guesses. The stronger ones recorded validated rules and flagged mistakes. The report's Pokémon example makes the cost of overdoing this visible. One run of Sonnet 3.7 produced 413 memory files and earned one badge, while a run of Fable 5 earned four with nine files plus a top-level note. That comparison changes the model as well as its note-taking behaviour, so it illustrates the problem rather than measuring the effect of tidy notes. For a school agent that revisits policies across sessions, the question is who can correct a saved rule after the policy changes. A rule with its source and an expiry or review date gives someone a place to start. A confident summary with no provenance is just harder to argue with.

- **Google's India experiment puts data permissions inside a storytelling app.** [Google's India team announced on 3 November](https://blog.google/intl/en-in/products/dreambeans-is-now-available-to-everyone-in-india/) that Dreambeans, a Google Labs experiment, was expanding to adults in India on Android and iOS. Google says the app is available across 14 languages and makes daily collections of stories using information from the Google apps a person selects, including Calendar, Gemini, Gmail, Photos, Search and YouTube. The suggestions sound modest enough, from ideas for things to make to local events. The access required to make those suggestions personal is the part worth noticing. This is an adult consumer launch, so the school relevance is a design question. If a future tool recommends reading or activities to students, an approver ought to be able to explain the difference between a list of interests the student typed, activity the system inferred, and information pulled from a linked service. A pupil can be comfortable asking for a weekend reading recommendation without expecting a calendar entry to shape it.

- **Bristol schools had to advise families before knowing who was exposed.** [ITV reported on 4 November](https://www.itv.com/news/westcountry/2026-11-04/childrens-data-leaked-by-criminals-in-bristol-schools-cyber-attack) that files from Bristol City Council's school network had been published online after the September attack on its Trading with Schools IT platform. The [council's update](https://www.bristol.gov.uk/residents/schools-learning-and-early-years/school-it-incident) puts the number of files that may have been taken and published at about 320,000. Parents and carers were informed through schools on 3 November and given advice about scams while specialists worked out which schools and people were affected. The file count gives a sense of the investigation's workload. A family still needs to know whether its own information was in the files and when it might find out. A school that relies on a shared provider could prepare two notices. The first explains what is known and gives families a way to verify follow-up messages. The second goes out once someone can name the records involved. Both need an owner even when the provider is doing the forensic work.

## On the radar

- **A Hong Kong workshop asks who does the thinking on 11 November.** The [Education University of Hong Kong programme](https://ai4edu.eduhk.hk/workshop2026/) schedules a hybrid English-language session with Lucas Kohnke and Fridolin Ting on Wednesday. Participants compare two versions of an activity, then redesign one of their own and get peer feedback. One of the lenses, AI-ICAP, asks who is doing the cognitive work when a student and a model collaborate. The workshop itself is this week's development, and the programme gives no posting date. The activity design holds a question for teachers. If the learning goal stays constant and the division of labour changes, what do students have to explain for themselves?

- **Anthropic's revised Usage Policy takes effect on 12 November.** In its [8 October announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/2026-usage-policy-update), the company clarifies its existing qualified-human-review and disclosure duties for covered high-risk uses. New requirements address models connected to equipment that takes autonomous physical actions and could injure someone. A qualified operator must be able to observe and stop it, and the equipment has to remain safe if Claude disconnects. For a robotics programme, that last clause suggests a better demonstration than showing a successful run. Cut the model's connection and see what the machine does before asking whether the AI belongs anywhere near the controls.

## Sceptic's corner

The tempting claim is that a chatbot preserves student thinking as long as it only asks questions. There is a real advantage in making a student write their own sentences. But a question can still pick the issue, supply the assumptions and direct attention away from a defensible position. The [MAIKE preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.07792v1) acknowledges what happens if its diagnosis misreads an essay. It can steer the student towards a weakness that isn't there, and the student gets no signal from the interface that the question may be misdirected.

The comparison matters here. Both tutors were told to withhold essay text and both had Think and Write modes. The researchers compared two complete systems in one session on one prompt. So a bump in idea-level dialogue is evidence that the interactions differed. To find out whether students got better at challenging a premise, a teacher would need to hear them challenge a new premise later, with the tutor out of the exchange.

One small classroom test is to bring one accepted suggestion and one rejected suggestion from a draft. Ask the writer to defend both decisions against the text and say what would change their mind. The student who gives a good reason for disregarding the chatbot has shown something a polished second draft alone can't tell you.
