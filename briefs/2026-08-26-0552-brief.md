## Top story

**The strongest signal in new AI-literacy data is not enthusiasm but the eight-hour divide.** A National Girls Collaborative Project study released on 19 August and published online on 24 August surveyed 552 K–12 education professionals and supplemented the survey with focus groups involving 47 people. Sixty-seven per cent said they used AI professionally at least several times a week, and 87% reported using chatbots. Yet 37% had received no AI-related professional development and only 11% had completed more than eight hours. Among that small, more-trained group, 74% reported frequent professional use, against 16% of respondents with no training ([NGCP report, released 19 August 2026](https://ngcproject.org/sites/default/files/downloadables/2026-08/NGCP-LandscapeStudy-Report-FINAL.pdf); [NGCP summary, 24 August 2026](https://ngcproject.org/resources/shaping-inclusive-ai-future)).

The more revealing result is what people used AI for. Quick answers, administration and instructional-material creation dominated; student-centred applications were less common. The leading barriers were student misuse at 42%, data privacy at 38% and concern about AI’s social role at 37%. Respondents with some experience wanted practical implementation support, not another introductory tour. For Ed Tech Coaches, that argues for tiered professional learning built around actual decisions: selecting a permitted task, protecting data, checking the artefact, and testing whether the student can still perform without AI.

The numbers need discipline. This was a US landscape survey recruited partly through professional networks and snowball sampling, so the 74-to-16 gap is an association, not proof that eight hours of training caused adoption. The report also contains an internal methods discrepancy: its executive summary says eight focus groups, while the methods section says ten 60-minute sessions. Even so, the operational message survives. One-off exposure creates awareness; sustained practice can create judgement. The useful measure for international schools is not how many staff attended or opened a chatbot, but whether a teacher can make and explain a better instructional choice after the support ends.

## Other stories

- **OpenAI has turned workspace administration into a state-changing conversation.** The Admin plugin launched on 25 August for ChatGPT Work and Codex lets authorised administrators inspect adoption and credit use, add or remove members, update groups, diagnose effective permissions, control model and feature access, and approve or deny usage-limit and spending requests. It can also automate recurring checks and route requests into Slack or Microsoft Teams; OpenAI says each action remains bounded by the operator’s existing role and returns a structured confirmation ([OpenAI, 25 August 2026](https://openai.com/index/introducing-admin-plugin/)). The release names ChatGPT Work, not ChatGPT Edu, so this is not an education-entitlement claim. It is a procurement warning about the direction of travel: the assistant is becoming an admin console with verbs. Any future school deployment needs a catalogue of permitted writes, separate approval for broad-impact changes, immutable logs and a known-good read-back. Natural language makes administration easier to request; it does not make the consequences less exact.

- **Zero retention is being redesigned for risks that emerge across a conversation.** On 19 August OpenAI previewed Private Safety Processing for eligible API customers using Zero Data Retention. Under ZDR, prompts and responses are not retained after processing, personnel cannot review customer content, and enterprise data is not used for training unless the customer opts in. The proposed system would detect patterns across related interactions while content stays on customer-controlled infrastructure or in OpenAI storage encrypted with customer-controlled keys; OpenAI would receive only a narrow risk signal. Early-customer testing is under way, with rollout and a technical paper planned for September ([OpenAI, 19 August 2026](https://openai.com/index/offering-zero-data-retention-for-frontier-models/)). This is an architecture preview, not an audited guarantee and not the policy for ordinary ChatGPT accounts. Potential child-sexual-abuse images remain an explicit retention-and-review exception. For international schools, the useful distinction is precise: no model training, no routine retention, no human access and no derived safety signal are four different promises.

- **A satellite has found the air-pollution cost of an AI data centre from orbit.** A preprint submitted on 23 August used NASA’s TEMPO instrument to estimate nitrogen-oxide emissions from the gas-fired plant serving SpaceXAI’s Colossus 2 facility near Memphis. After calibrating its method against continuous monitoring at four US power plants, the team estimated average emissions of 730 ± 185 kilograms per hour from February to mid-August 2026, about sixteen times the 47-kilogram-per-hour level expected for 41 permanent turbines operating with best-available controls ([Gauld and colleagues, submitted 23 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22153)). That comparison is not a regulatory finding: the site was still relying heavily on temporary turbines without the planned controls, and the remote-sensing estimate is a preprint with substantial uncertainty. It does make AI’s material footprint observable rather than rhetorical. A school sustainability conversation should distinguish the marginal cost of one classroom request from the infrastructure choices made to serve demand at scale, while still asking vendors for energy, water and local-pollution evidence.

- **Google has made Microsoft-to-Workspace migration a first-party Education feature.** Two 25 August releases made advanced-mode imports generally available for OneDrive files and permissions, and for Teams channels, group chats and direct messages. The tools can run concurrent batches, tune throughput to the Microsoft licence tier and use a planning utility to estimate corpus size and migration time. Google lists Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus, requires a super administrator, and charges no separate tool or infrastructure fee ([Google Workspace Updates: OneDrive, 25 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/introducing-data-import-for-microsoft-OneDrive-An-easier-faster-and-higher-fidelity-migration-to-Google-Workspace.html); [Google Workspace Updates: Teams, 25 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/introducing-data-import-for-microsoft-Teams-An-easier-faster-and-higher-fidelity-migration-to-Google-Workspace.html)). The zero price removes one budget line, not the governance work. The practical value for international schools is not an invitation to migrate. It is leverage over future platform exits: permissions and private conversations are now in scope for a supported move. Any real exit test still needs counts, exceptions, retention mapping, sampled fidelity and proof that the source was decommissioned rather than left as a silent second archive.

- **Meeting participants are getting a physical pause button for AI notes.** Google announced on 25 August that eligible Meet hardware touch controllers will gain a visible badge plus controls to start, stop and resume Take notes for me, removing the need to join from a laptop in Companion mode. Early Preview rollout is scheduled for 31 August and broader Rapid and Scheduled Release rollout for 8 September. Education access requires Google AI Pro for Education, compatible Meet hardware and its separate licence, although a device may expose the control when it joins an otherwise eligible meeting ([Google Workspace Updates, 25 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/control-take-notes-for-me-directly-from-Google-Meet-hardware-touch-controllers.html)). This is not yet completed rollout. The design is still worth noticing: visibility and immediate pause belong in the room, not on the administrator’s policy page. In schools, a button would complement rather than replace a rule for notification, sensitive agenda items, transcript ownership, retention and who may restart capture.

## On the radar

- **Sensitive Chat spaces can now hide their membership list.** Google’s 25 August notice says owners and managers can limit full-member visibility to owners, owners and managers, all members or the entire organisation; rollout began on 24 August for every Workspace edition. All members remains the default, there is no admin control, and anyone who posts still exposes their name and profile ([Google Workspace Updates, 25 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/restrict-who-can-view-member-lists-in-Google-Chat-spaces.html)). Watch student-support and external-partner spaces in practice: a privacy option delegated to space owners needs a creation template or it will remain unused.

- **Grouped pivot fields will survive a trip through Excel.** From 25 August, Google Sheets retains custom date, time and numeric groupings as reusable pivot-table fields and preserves them when importing or exporting `.xlsx` files. Rapid Release rollout began that day; Scheduled Release begins 14 September, with no admin control ([Google Workspace Updates, 25 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/preserve-and-reuse-grouped-pivot-table-fields-in-Google-Sheets.html)). This is small but audit-friendly: assessment and operations workbooks crossing platforms should lose fewer silent transformations. Watch whether formulas, filters and charts preserve the same grouping semantics in real files. Run one known-good control workbook.

- **A two-day AI-literacy convening has put classroom design after foundational understanding, not inside the same afternoon.** UC San Diego scheduled its first session for 22 August around capabilities, limits, ethics and community perspectives, followed by a second session on 19 September for planning, student-work analysis and assessment design ([UC San Diego AI Literacy Collaborative, accessed 26 August 2026](https://ai-literacy.ucsd.edu/)). The page is a programme notice and does not confirm that the first session ran or publish outcomes. The sequence is the watch item: spaced application and return may produce better teacher judgement than a single showcase day, if participants bring artefacts and evidence back.

## Sceptic's corner

The headline-friendly reading of the NGCP report is that more than eight hours of professional development turns reluctant teachers into frequent AI users. The 74% versus 16% gap is large enough to invite a tidy prescription: buy the hours and adoption follows.

The study does not establish that. Respondents were not randomly assigned to training, and people already interested in AI are more likely to seek longer professional learning, use the tools frequently and answer a survey about AI literacy. The report’s strongest quantitative relationship may therefore capture appetite and access as much as programme effect. Frequent use is also an ambiguous outcome when the same report says activity clusters around quick answers, administration and material creation rather than student learning ([NGCP report, released 19 August 2026](https://ngcproject.org/sites/default/files/downloadables/2026-08/NGCP-LandscapeStudy-Report-FINAL.pdf)).

That does not make professional learning optional. It makes adoption the wrong finish line. A credible international-school programme should record the decision a teacher was trying to improve, inspect the resulting classroom artefact, collect student work, and include an AI-free transfer check where learning is claimed. Revisit the same teacher after the novelty period and ask what they stopped doing as well as what they adopted. Eight hours may be enough to move beyond button tours. Only evidence from practice can show whether it moved judgement. The point is not more AI use; it is better educational decisions.
