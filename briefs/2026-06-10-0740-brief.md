## Top story

**Anthropic has shipped its most capable model to the public — with a built-in chaperone.** Claude Fable 5, released yesterday, is what Anthropic calls a Mythos-class model: state-of-the-art on nearly every benchmark it tested, priced at $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output — less than half the cost of the restricted Mythos Preview it descends from ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-fable-5-mythos-5)). The unusual part is the architecture of restraint. Classifier-based safeguards cover offensive cybersecurity, most biology and chemistry requests, and large-scale capability distillation; when one trips — under 5% of sessions, by Anthropic's count — the user silently gets an answer from Claude Opus 4.8 instead. All Fable 5 traffic carries a mandatory 30-day data retention. A twin model, Claude Mythos 5, is the same system with the cyber safeguards lifted, available only to roughly 150 vetted defensive-security organisations through Project Glasswing ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/09/anthropic-mythos-claude-fable-5.html)).

The capability claims live in engineering, science, and finance — Stripe says it compressed a 50-million-line codebase migration from two months to a day; protein-design work reportedly accelerated roughly tenfold — and the announcement says nothing at all about education ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-fable-5-mythos-5)). But three details land in classrooms anyway. First, Fable 5 is included at no extra cost on paid Claude plans from June 9 to 22, then requires usage credits — so every teacher and older student with a personal subscription has frontier capability this fortnight, whether or not anyone planned for it. Second, the biology/chemistry safeguard means most science questions quietly route to Opus 4.8: a chemistry student is often not talking to the model on the label. Third, the 30-day retention requirement is a concrete data-handling fact for anyone evaluating Claude-based tools.

For international schools the stack question barely moves — in a Google-ecosystem school, Claude is not core infrastructure. What moves is the planning assumption. The persistent gap in most schools is guidance, not tools; this morning the capability ceiling rose again and the price halved, which makes "students will have access to this anyway" the only safe default. It also strengthens the case for mode-based task labels over tool lists: any policy that names specific models went stale at 9 a.m. Hanoi time. Write rules about modes of work; the tool roster will keep doing this.

## Sceptic's corner

Two lazy readings of yesterday are on offer, and both should be declined. The first: Anthropic warned days ago that frontier AI needs coordinated controls and may approach recursive self-improvement, then sold the dangerous thing at half price — so the safety talk is theatre ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/09/anthropic-released-claude-fable-5-its-most-powerful-model-publicly-days-after-warning-ai-is-getting-too-dangerous/)). The second: the classifiers held through 1,000+ hours of external red-teaming, so the problem is handled ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-fable-5-mythos-5)).

The document itself is more honest than either take. Anthropic concedes the safeguards are tuned stricter than ideal, that they catch harmless requests, and — the line worth underlining — that Fable 5's measured misaligned behaviour is "similar to that of Opus 4.8." The engineering gains sit at the misuse boundary, not the alignment core.

For schools, the practical wrinkle is epistemic rather than existential. A student often cannot know which model answered them: the Opus fallback is silent and lands disproportionately on science topics. Any disclosure norm phrased as "state which AI you used" just became harder to satisfy precisely. And mandatory 30-day retention as the entry price for capability is a procurement precedent worth clocking now, before it appears in an ed-tech contract. Treat vendor safety architecture as a product feature to verify — not a moral guarantee, and not a substitute for knowing what the task was measuring.
