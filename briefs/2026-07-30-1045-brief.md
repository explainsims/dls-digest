## Top story

**The largest school-based survey yet of how teenagers actually use AI found the dominant pattern is emotional, not academic.** The Jed Foundation published preliminary findings on 23 July from more than 5,500 middle and high school students across 21 US schools and districts, collected between October 2025 and April 2026. Four in five students had used some form of AI and more than half used it for schoolwork — but nearly three in five said they turned to AI when they felt sad, stressed or lonely, nearly a third used it for relationship or problem-solving advice, and more than one in ten used it for companionship. Students who used AI for emotional or mental-health support, problem-solving advice, during difficult emotions, or for companionship "were more likely to have poorer mental health, were more lonely, and had a greater history of suicidal thoughts and behaviors" ([The Jed Foundation, 23 July 2026](https://jedfoundation.org/the-jed-foundation-jed-releases-preliminary-research-on-how-teens-are-using-artificial-intelligence-ai-and-what-it-means-for-their-mental-health/)). JED's vice president of research and evaluation, Kayla Anderson, framed that as an association, not a cause ([WJLA, 24 July 2026](https://wjla.com/news/nation-world/capitol-hill-briefing-spotlights-ais-concerning-growing-role-in-teen-mental-health-jed-foundation-youth-mental-health-technology-policy)).

The demographic splits run in opposite directions: high school and rural students were more likely to use AI at all, while middle school and urban students were more likely to reach for it during difficult emotions — so the youngest cohort in the sample is the one using it closest to distress. And the sample is not a representative frame but schools already enrolled in JED's own programmes, with the full report not due until the autumn ([EdTech Innovation Hub, 30 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/nearly-three-in-five-students-say-they-turn-to-ai-when-sad-stressed-or-lonely-jed-research-finds)). Zainab Okolo, JED's senior vice president of policy, advocacy and government relations, drew the line at substitution, saying AI should not take the place of in-person interactions with peers and trusted adults ([WJLA, 24 July 2026](https://wjla.com/news/nation-world/capitol-hill-briefing-spotlights-ais-concerning-growing-role-in-teen-mental-health-jed-foundation-youth-mental-health-technology-policy)).

Almost every school AI policy in draft right now — including the district framework below — is an academic-integrity document governing what a student submits. This data says the modal adolescent interaction with a chatbot is not a homework shortcut but a private conversation at an hour when nobody else is available. The counselling pathway and the AI policy are being written by different people, in different rooms, and rarely read side by side.

## Other stories

- **Fifteen US states now have chatbot-specific laws on the books, and the drafting is converging on minors.** A Future of Privacy Forum tracker, updated weekly and confined to bills that have passed at least one chamber, counted 98 chatbot-specific bills across 34 states plus three congressional proposals as of July; fifteen states have signed legislation into law ([Stacker, 24 July 2026](https://kioncentralcoast.com/stacker-ai/2026/07/24/states-are-rushing-to-regulate-ai-chatbots/)). The provisions are unusually specific about age. Several states, including Colorado, Idaho, Iowa and Nebraska, require platforms to give parents tools to oversee or control a minor's account; Connecticut mandates an AI-disclosure reminder every hour for users under 18, against every three hours for adults. The same analysis cites KFF polling from March 2026: one in six US adults had used AI for mental health advice in the previous year, rising to 28% among 18-to-29-year-olds. Set against the JED figures, this is a legislature responding to a measured behaviour rather than a hypothetical one — and the specificity travels: a vendor selling into a US district now ships parental controls and session-level disclosure by default, a feature list any school anywhere can ask to have switched on.

- **A large Texas district wrote its AI framework by starting with where AI is not allowed.** Katy ISD's new AI framework, presented to its board days before 28 July, bars generative AI chat tools — Google Gemini named among them — for elementary students, permits limited, supervised AI there for reading and personalised learning, and allows district-approved generative AI in high school with teacher permission; unreviewed platforms, and any falling outside federal education-law compliance, are excluded outright. Assistant superintendent Sanée Bell put it plainly: "Students will learn how to use AI...And also when it is and is not appropriate." University of Houston professor Jaclyn Ocumpaugh named the precondition — "I think we want to make sure that our teachers really understand how these things work before we turn them loose in the classroom" — and the misconception behind it: "There are a lot of people that think this is basically Google on steroids, and it's not" ([ABC13, 28 July 2026](https://abc13.com/post/katy-isd-sets-new-framework-artificial-intelligence-use-classrooms/19587218/)). Most district frameworks this summer are adoption plans with caveats. This one runs the other way — a prohibition floor with graduated release upward — and whatever one makes of where the age line falls, a framework whose first question is what a nine-year-old should not be doing with a chatbot starts somewhere very different from one asking which licence to buy.

- **ASEAN's education ministers made AI one of three regional priorities for the next five years, and Singapore now chairs it.** The ASEAN Five-Year Work Plan on Education 2026-2030 was launched on 22 July during the 14th ASEAN Education Ministers Meeting in Singapore, with artificial intelligence in education, STEM, and holistic development as its three focus areas; Singapore's education minister Desmond Lee framed it as "taking practical steps to prepare our people for the future" ([Bernama, 22 July 2026](https://asean.bernama.com/news.php?id=2584164)). Singapore holds the education-sector chairmanship for 2026-2027 and will run signature programmes on AI in education and STEM, including a forum drawing in China, Japan, South Korea and dialogue partners ([Xinhua, 23 July 2026](https://english.news.cn/20260723/954dc093c51844d994986bd2a1986038/c.html)). Vietnam's deputy minister of education and training Le Quan attended ([VietnamPlus, 23 July 2026](https://en.vietnamplus.vn/vietnam-attends-asean-education-ministers-meeting-and-related-meetings-in-singapore-post348837.vnp)). Regional work plans rarely bind an international school, but they shape the professional-development market, the conference circuit and the vocabulary ministries use — and a school in Ho Chi Minh City now sits inside a bloc whose declared education agenda names AI first, a nearer frame for a board conversation than an imported European or American one.

- **More than a thousand people who build frontier models asked Washington to help build the machinery for slowing them down.** A statement titled "Pacing the Frontier" circulated on 28 July carrying signatures from employees at OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta, backed by the nonprofits Guidelight AI Standards and Encode AI. Reported counts moved as it stayed open — 1,171 in one account, 1,178 in another — and the signatories are senior: Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei and several co-founders, plus OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, with Sam Altman not among them ([Trending Topics, 29 July 2026](https://www.trendingtopics.eu/1100-employees-at-openai-anthropic-meta-and-google-call-for-ai-slowdown/)); OpenAI chief research officer Mark Chen and Google AI-safety VP Anca Dragan also appear on the list. The ask is not a pause. Trending Topics renders it as Washington being asked to "support an international effort to develop the technical and governance instruments needed to deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development"; the letter is deliberately non-prescriptive about what those instruments are, with compute transparency, shared evaluation protocols and export controls coming from commentary rather than the text. Both OpenAI and Anthropic endorsed it at company level within hours ([explainx.ai](https://explainx.ai/blog/pacing-the-frontier-ai-employees-letter-july-2026)). It changes no school's timetable. What it changes is the credibility cost of one particular staffroom line: "the people building this aren't worried" has stopped being a defensible thing to say.

- **The largest open-weight model ever released came out of China on Sunday, and anyone can run it on their own hardware.** Moonshot AI published the weights for Kimi K3 on 26 July, a day ahead of its stated target: 2.8 trillion total parameters with 104 billion active — 16 of 896 experts selected per token across 93 layers — a 1,048,576-token context window, and native text, image and video handling via a 401-million-parameter vision encoder, under a custom Kimi K3 licence ([Hugging Face model card](https://huggingface.co/moonshotai/Kimi-K3)). Nathan Lambert calls it "the strongest open model ever released" and reckons that "either the open-to-closed or American-to-Chinese model performance gap has been reduced from the debated 6-9 months to something shorter, say 3-5 months" — pointedly declining to say which of the two moved ([Interconnects](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/kimi-k3-the-open-weights-escalation)). It is emphatically not a school tool: self-hosting starts at eight H100 80GB GPUs, a data-centre purchase, not a server-room one ([explainx.ai](https://explainx.ai/blog/kimi-k3-open-weights-2-8-trillion-parameters-july-2026)). The reason to track it anyway is that open weights remain the only architecture under which an institution with a hard data-residency constraint can run a near-frontier model without a vendor contract at all — and the distance between that option and the frontier just halved.

## On the radar

- **Microsoft is paying eighteen university labs to find what its own red team misses.** The External Red Team Alliance, announced on 27 July, pairs a global academic network with an operational specialist network, giving unrestricted gifts — no deliverables, no product requirements — to labs on six continents, among them Carnegie Mellon, Harvard, IIT Madras, KAIST, UCL, Pretoria and São Paulo. The stated problem is that safety testing inside a single organisation leaves failure modes needing domain expertise, multilingual understanding and regional knowledge undetected ([Microsoft Security Blog, 27 July 2026](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/07/27/enhancing-ai-security-through-global-ai-red-teaming/)). Worth watching whether independent capacity survives being funded by the thing it audits.

- **A paper submitted this month puts a number on what a red-team pass can actually prove.** Bandana Kaur's "What AI Red-Team Evaluations Can and Cannot Prove", submitted 23 July, derives an "evidential ceiling" — a measurable limit on how far any single evaluation result can move confidence in a safety claim under a fixed budget — and argues the boundary "is calculable rather than merely a matter of judgment". The regimes diverge sharply: modest benchmarks can certify high-frequency harms at a stated standard, while for rare catastrophic harms current benchmarks fall "several orders of magnitude short". The method extends to adaptive and automated red-teaming ([arXiv, 23 July 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21735)).

- **Meta funded thirty smart-glasses projects, and the education ones are about learning in the flow of the day.** Nearly $2 million went to 30 US organisations selected from almost 500 applications across 18 states, split between Accelerator grants for groups already deploying AI glasses and Catalyst grants for developers building on the Wearables Device Access Toolkit. Immerse Inc. is doing hands-free real-time language tutoring for more than 200,000 learners; Easter Seals Greater Houston's BridgingApps programme is piloting with people who have low vision, people with intellectual and developmental disabilities, and people experiencing cognitive aging; United Spinal Association is documenting accessibility barriers ([EdTech Innovation Hub, 30 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/meta-awards-nearly-2-million-to-30-us-ai-glasses-projects)).

## Sceptic's corner

The line already forming around the JED numbers is causal: chatbots are making adolescents lonelier and more depressed. It is an easy inference and the research does not support it. The sample comes from schools already enrolled in JED's own programmes rather than a representative frame ([EdTech Innovation Hub, 30 July 2026](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/nearly-three-in-five-students-say-they-turn-to-ai-when-sad-stressed-or-lonely-jed-research-finds)), and Anderson herself presented the relationship as an association rather than a cause ([WJLA, 24 July 2026](https://wjla.com/news/nation-world/capitol-hill-briefing-spotlights-ais-concerning-growing-role-in-teen-mental-health-jed-foundation-youth-mental-health-technology-policy)).

The competing explanation is at least as plausible and considerably duller. A student who is already distressed at one in the morning has a narrow menu: wake a parent, message a friend who is asleep, wait until a counsellor has a free period on Thursday, or open an application that answers instantly and never looks tired. Selection into AI use by distress would produce precisely this correlation with no causal contribution from the technology. The middle-school-and-urban skew in the emotional-use finding is, if anything, mild evidence for the selection reading — those are the cohorts with the least autonomy over their own time.

None of which is a reason to file the study away — only to read it as measuring something other than AI. What it measures is friction: a pastoral pathway with a response time counted in days and a social cost counted in being seen walking into an office, losing to something with neither. That is actionable without settling the causal question. Whether or not the chatbot is doing harm, it is winning on availability — and availability is a design variable a school controls.
