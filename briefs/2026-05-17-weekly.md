# Week ending Sunday, 17 May 2026

They are putting AI into curriculum planning, reporting, communications, and governance work. The market is now building both the classroom-facing fluency layer and the institutional-control layer at the same time. ## What Does It All Mean?

The useful question this week is not "which vendor is safer?" That is too flat. The better question is: **when AI becomes part of school infrastructure, what kind of school artefact is it allowed to touch?**

Canvas exposed the obvious class of artefact: names, emails, course enrolments, message contents. That is traditional student-data territory, and the vocabulary around it is reasonably mature: breach notification, FERPA, state AG reporting, incident response, third-party processor, remediation. The US Department of Education's late-May privacy webinars are almost comically well-timed here: one session on transparency, one on incident response and vetting educational technology. ([Protecting Student Privacy](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/?src=rn))

Toddle points at the less mature class of artefact. Its PYP Refresh workshop framed Toddle AI as a curriculum-realignment assistant: revise units, unpack 2025 PYP updates, strengthen planning, use the Curriculum Design Assistant in breakout work. That is not student-grade data, but it is not nothing. Curriculum maps, teacher-authored units, assessment language, intervention notes, school-home communication templates, and local adaptation decisions all reveal how a school thinks. They are institutional knowledge, not just files.

This is where the weekly needs to leave the breach story and do the slower work. The OECD's PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy project defines the coming assessment domain around students' ability to act critically and ethically in AI-mediated environments. The EU Council's conclusions put teachers at the centre: AI should preserve teacher autonomy, learner well-being, data protection, accountability, and risk awareness. Those two policy moves are not about picking Gemini over Claude over ChatGPT. They are about whether schools can still name the human judgement in a system once the workflow has been partially handed to software. ([OECD PISA](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/programmes/pisa.html), [EU Council](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/05/11/ai-in-education-council-calls-for-human-centred-approach/))

That is also why OpenAI's admin controls matter more than the demo videos. Workspace agents in ChatGPT Edu can connect tools, schedule runs, ask approval for sensitive steps, expose analytics, and give admins visibility through a compliance API. This is the boring layer that turns AI from teacher enthusiasm into governed service. Google's AI Educator Series is the other half: broad professional fluency, currently US-first, with Gemini and NotebookLM as training currency. One side teaches people what to do. The other side lets institutions see and constrain what was done. Neither is sufficient alone.

For international schools, the framing to carry is simple: **classify artefacts before classifying tools.** Student records are one class. Teacher-authored curriculum is another. Operational workflows are a third. Public-facing communications are a fourth. Once those classes exist, the vendor questions become sharper: which artefacts enter the model workflow; where are they processed; can they train a model; who can export them; what logs exist; what happens when a free-teacher surface touches the paid-school environment. That is a better rubric than "AI feature: yes/no."

## Behind You! Behind You!

**The retracted ChatGPT-learning paper is the research story that will keep mattering after the breach news cools.** Ars Technica covered the retraction of an influential paper that had claimed strong learning benefits from ChatGPT. Ben Williamson's caveat is the one to remember: the headline finding may persist in policy and vendor decks even after the paper is gone. ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2026/05/influential-study-touting-chatgpt-in-education-retracted-over-red-flags/))

**The EU Council conclusions landed in the exact register international schools borrow from.** Teacher autonomy, well-being, inclusion, data protection, accountability, risk awareness: not flashy, but that is policy language with legs. It gives school leaders a non-vendor vocabulary for saying "AI, yes, but not as automation theatre." ([EU Council](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/05/11/ai-in-education-council-calls-for-human-centred-approach/))

**OpenAI's compliance story is becoming a governance asset.** The agent launch is not just "make a bot." The useful bit is admin visibility into configurations, updates, runs, connected tools, approvals, and suspension. Schools will ask for exactly this layer once agents move from pilots to department routines. ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/fr-CA/index/introducing-workspace-agents-in-chatgpt/))

**Instructure's Free-for-Teacher shutdown deserves its own post-mortem slot.** The vendor says both the April 29 and May 7 activity used a Free-for-Teacher account, and that the product is temporarily shut down while a safer return path is designed. Free teacher entry points are innovation channels. They are also trust-boundary tests. ([Instructure](https://www.instructure.com/incident_update))

## Sceptic's Corner

**"Human-centred AI" can become wallpaper if schools don't operationalise it.** The EU wording is good. So is OECD's critical-literacy direction. But the phrase only earns its place if a school can point to concrete constraints: approval gates, data-classification rules, curriculum-artifact handling, parent-facing disclosure, teacher opt-out boundaries, and logs that a human can actually read. Otherwise "teacher at the centre" becomes the new "personalised learning" — true in the slide deck, foggy in the room.

**Vendor fluency training is not the same as institutional judgement.** Google's AI Educator Series may be useful; it will almost certainly become common reference currency. But training people to use Gemini and NotebookLM is not the same as teaching them when to refuse a tool, how to inspect data flows, or how to recognise a retracted evidence claim when it reappears in a sales deck.

**The safest-sounding product may be the one that touches the most sensitive non-student data.** A curriculum assistant looks calmer than a gradebook breach because no one says "social security number." But curriculum is a school's intellectual infrastructure. Once AI systems help rewrite it, schools need governance language for teacher-authored and institution-authored knowledge, not just student records. That is the blind spot this week exposed.
