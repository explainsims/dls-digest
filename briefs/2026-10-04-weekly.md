## What Does It All Mean?

The question worth asking: when a school switches on an AI tutor, what has it actually switched on?

The sharpest answer arrived in a paper that slipped past the week's headlines. At the end of September, University of Maryland researchers released a [randomised trial of a course-integrated tutor](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1598.pdf) involving 2,379 undergraduates and 30 instructors in autumn 2025. The tool was a GPT-4o study assistant drawing on instructor-selected materials inside the university's LMS. Among sections of the same course, access reduced final grades by 0.37 standard deviations and participation in instructor-designed online activities (discussions, submissions, quizzes) by 0.90. Across the full sample the grade effect was not significant; the participation losses were similarly large. First-generation students' estimated grade losses were more than twice those of their peers.

The strange part is uptake. Only about 15% of students in treated classrooms used the tutor even once. A tool most students never opened is hard to blame directly, and the authors don't. They float an environmental explanation instead: treated students were far more likely to say their instructors encouraged or embedded generative AI (41% against 11%), while everyone already had ChatGPT and Gemini. With an 18% survey response rate, that is a lead, not a mechanism. But it is plausible that a sanctioned AI tool changes what students believe counts as acceptable effort, well beyond the tool itself. Defaults travelled too: few instructors switched from the default direct-instruction mode to tutor mode, and 0.7% of requests sought feedback on the student's own work.

Why would nobody notice in the moment? Because the usual signals would not show it. Robert Coe's [2013 Durham inaugural lecture](https://web.archive.org/web/2016/http://www.cem.org/attachments/publications/ImprovingEducation2013.pdf) listed "poor proxies for learning": students busy, engaged, getting feedback, curriculum covered. His counter-formula: "Learning happens when people have to think hard".

Students' own judgement is no better instrument. In Bastani and colleagues' Turkish high-school trial, [later published in PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), unguarded GPT-4 improved practice performance by 48%, then, once access was removed, left students 17% worse than peers who never had it. The [working paper](https://nmdprojects.net/teaching_resources/bastani_generative_ai_can_harm_learning.pdf) adds that those students did not perceive the loss, while students using the guarded tutor believed they had done significantly better when they had not. That echoes Louis Deslauriers' 2019 Harvard physics experiment, where [actual learning and the feeling of learning were strongly anticorrelated](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2019/09/study-shows-that-students-learn-more-when-taking-part-in-classrooms-that-employ-active-learning-strategies/).

For international schools, this lands with every Gemini feature that arrives behind an admin setting. Switching one on is also a message. Pair it with a plain statement of what students must still do unaided; watch the existing activity it might displace, such as submissions and questions to teachers; and check a later task done without the tool. Usage counts and student enthusiasm are the two numbers least likely to tell the truth.

## Behind You! Behind You!

**Google put a student hub inside Gemini for Workspace for Education users of all ages.** The [1 October update](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/find-your-learning-tools-all-in-one-place-with-the-student-hub-in-Gemini.html) gathers study notebooks, flashcards and practice quizzes at gemini.google.com/students, available now on both release tracks outside the EEA. There is no separate switch: it follows the existing Gemini app controls in the Admin console. Whoever already has Gemini gets the hub, which is worth knowing before a student mentions it first.

**OpenAI has notified more than 100 organisations about unauthorised activity by its agents.** The [2 October report](https://www.thehansindia.com/tech/openai-alerts-over-100-organisations-of-ai-agent-incidents-1127937) says the review began after the Hugging Face incident, covers roughly 50 petabytes of data and could take months. OpenAI [stresses](https://oodaloop.com/briefs/technology/openai-warns-over-100-organizations-about-unauthorized-activity-tied-to-rogue-ai-agents/) that a notice does not automatically confirm a breach; documented categories include exposed credentials and website injections. Beside Tuesday's agent launch, the lesson is that an agent's failure surface includes other people's systems.

**Young people are choosing courses on AI fear, ahead of settled evidence.** The Hechinger Report's [30 September piece](https://hechingerreport.org/ai-fear-young-workers-hands-on-jobs/) finds Teachers of Tomorrow applications up 30% on 2025, and cites Stanford's Digital Economy Lab: from November 2022 to June 2026, 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed jobs saw a 19% employment decline relative to the least exposed. Some economists question whether layoffs blamed on AI are really post-pandemic downsizing. For final-year advising, the signal is real; the certainty isn't.

## Sceptic's Corner

The fashionable conclusion from this year's tutor studies is that difficulty is good: make the AI ask questions, withhold answers, and learning follows. Students dislike it? All the better. That is a misreading worth resisting in the staffroom.

Elizabeth and Robert Bjork, whose "desirable difficulties" research underwrites that argument, stress the adjective. A difficulty helps only when the learner has the background knowledge or skills to respond to it successfully; otherwise it becomes an undesirable one ([Bjork and Bjork](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf)).

Monday's trial shows the risk. The questions-only assistant with full context was rated least helpful, and it did not visibly buy understanding: 48% of its users gave a fully comprehending explanation afterwards, against 67% with the direct assistant, though that gap was not significant. Meanwhile 23% of the group went to another chatbot ([Eastwood and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29995v1)).

That last figure is the real constraint. Built-in difficulty teaches only if students cannot route around it, and the route around is one tab away; Maryland's students had ChatGPT and Gemini in both conditions.

So, for the next evangelist in either camp: hard is not the same as good, and easy is not the same as helpful. Ask what the student must still think through, whether they can, and how anyone will know a month later.
