# Tuesday, 12 May 2026

## Top story

**The KKR class action dropped Friday. Aaron Hinds, a University of Denver alum, filed in SDNY on Friday, 8 May 2026, asserting negligence against KKR and Instructure — "KKR failed to honor its responsibility to protect personal data." The *In re PowerSchool* PE-ratification pattern just traveled, and the leak deadline lands tonight.**

The complaint flagged in Monday's brief as "imminent within days" was filed three days before today's brief. Per Bloomberg Law, plaintiff Aaron Hinds names KKR (current owner) and Instructure as co-defendants in the Southern District of New York, on negligence — same architecture that survived the motion to dismiss in *In re PowerSchool* against Bain Capital in SDCal. Bloomberg's coverage cites the PowerSchool precedent explicitly when explaining the theory's viability: PE owner "largely unable to convince a federal court to dismiss the claims" was the load-bearing line in February's ruling, and the plaintiffs' bar is now testing whether KKR's 2024 take-private of Instructure attracts the same treatment. Filing date matters: 8 May 2026 (Friday US time) lands *before* the 12 May leak deadline — meaning the litigation is now docket-active going into the leak. ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/kkr-instructure-sued-after-data-breach-of-canvas-edtech-tool), [ClassAction.org](https://www.classaction.org/data-breach-lawsuits/instructure-may-2026))

The deadline itself is holding at end-of-day Tuesday, 12 May 2026 — confirmed in coverage filed this morning. Per The Register's filing dated Tuesday, 12 May 2026, Instructure has now publicly characterised the two intrusion events as "distinct events involving different systems and circumstances" — meaning the late-April compromise (via the Free-For-Teacher account program, ~3.65 TB across ~275 million records affecting ~8,800 institutions) and the 7 May login-page defacement are being narrated as architecturally separate incidents. The "no core learning data compromised" line is also new — Instructure's framing is that "course content, submissions, credentials" was not in the stolen dataset, only "usernames, email addresses, course names, enrollment information, and messages." That last item (messages) is the load-bearing one: the corpus of teacher-student private messages was always the live concern. ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/05/12/double-canvas-intrusion-confirmed-as-shinyhunters-resets-leak-deadline/5238361))

The school-by-school extortion pivot is the procurement-relevant news that hasn't fully landed yet. ShinyHunters has been contacting individual institutions directly with separate ransom demands, with the same 12 May deadline, asking schools to "contact us privately to negotiate a settlement" regardless of whether Instructure has paid. Infosecurity Magazine framed this last week as a structural shift: extortion economics in education are bifurcating between vendor-level and institution-level payment, and a school that pays its own ransom does so without knowing whether the vendor has also paid (and therefore whether the data is genuinely contained). For procurement committees this is harder than the breach-as-vendor-event story — it forces a separate decision under time pressure with imperfect information. As of filing time, neither Instructure nor any named institution has publicly confirmed payment. ([Infosecurity Magazine](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/shinyhunters-escalates-canvas/), [Halcyon ransomware alerts](https://www.halcyon.ai/ransomware-alerts/education-sector-in-the-crosshairs-shinyhunters-extortion-campaign-against-instructure))

The international school context: the Hinds complaint in SDNY against KKR is the precedent-traveling case flagged Friday — *plaintiff theory naming the PE owner under the In re PowerSchool pattern*. Procurement committees this fall will be writing rubrics under the new precedent that "active class action against PE-backed ed-tech vendor" is a real and citable thing, not a theoretical one. The follow-on rubric question that gets sharper today: does the rubric distinguish *vendor settling and protecting institutional data* (PowerSchool's Naviance arc, $17.25M preliminary approval) from *vendor whose schools were independently extorted*? Today's extortion pattern is structurally different from PowerSchool in that second axis. The 16 May Toddle "PYP Refresh" webinar on Friday now sits firmly inside both the leak-day news window *and* a Friday-filed-complaint news window — Toddle's positioning toward Canvas-architecture security questions on Friday will be visible in whether they touch the topic at all.

## Sceptic's corner

**Instructure's "two distinct events" framing is procurement-defensible, and that's the point.** Today's confirmed-fact disclosure ("the April 29 and May 7 intrusions were separate events involving different systems and circumstances") does important work for Instructure in the rubric conversations that are about to happen — it lets a procurement-defender narrate "one breach, contained" rather than "ongoing exposure," which is the harder story to defend in front of a committee. The architectural reality may well be that the two events were independent, in the same way the September 2025 Salesforce-tied incident and the April 2026 Free-For-Teacher compromise were independent. But the *disclosure pattern* is now visible: each event narrated as separate, each with its own scope statement, each with its own "core learning data not compromised" qualifier. The cumulative effect is that three breach incidents at the same vendor in eight months read in procurement decks as three separate items rather than a single trajectory. That's not bad-faith framing — it's the SEC-Item-1.05 materiality framework working as designed — but a procurement committee using a "count of breaches" axis in its rubric is structurally vulnerable to that framing.

**The school-by-school extortion pivot is the bigger structural news than the leak itself.** Whether the dump ships tonight or not, the precedent that ShinyHunters has established in the last fortnight is the one that travels: an extortion group can simultaneously negotiate with the vendor *and* extort the affected institutions directly, on the same deadline. That doubles the procurement-defence surface for any RFP-cycle vendor evaluation. The plain-English question for the rubric is: "Does our LMS contract specify how vendor and customer coordinate on third-party extortion of the customer's data held by the vendor?" The honest answer for every K-12 contract reviewed publicly is that it doesn't, because nobody wrote a contract clause for "the attacker contacts the school district directly." That gap is the procurement-rubric gap that will get a clause in the next RFP cycle, and the institutions writing that clause first will publish boilerplate that everyone else uses.

**The retracted-paper pattern Watters flags persists in this week's coverage.** Multiple education-vendor pieces this week cite the "AI good for students" finding pattern when arguing for AI-procurement decisions; none cite the corresponding retractions. The bar for which studies stay cited is moving, slowly, in the wrong direction for evidence-led procurement. ([Audrey Watters — Broken Record](https://2ndbreakfast.audreywatters.com/broken-record/))
