## Top story

**New York City has chosen an age boundary instead of an AI free-for-all.** For 2026–27, the largest US school system is barring student-facing generative AI from 2K through Grade 8. High-school access is limited to approved programmes: every student must complete two 45-minute AI-literacy modules, and five centrally vetted pilots cover Quill, Edia, Brisk Teaching, Playlab and Intel AI-Ready Schools. The guidance even publishes expected dosage, from 15 minutes a week for Quill to one period a week for the Intel course. Separate screen rules prohibit routine one-to-one device use through Grade 2 and recommend daily caps of 30 minutes in Grades 3–5 and 45 minutes in Grades 6–8 ([New York City Public Schools, 2 September 2026](https://www.schools.nyc.gov/about-us/policies/guidance-on-artificial-intelligence)).

The detail is more useful than the ban headline. Assistive technology, multilingual-learner support, assessments and centrally approved instruction remain available. Staff may use approved AI for planning and operations, but not for grading, behaviour monitoring, counselling, special-education documents or decisions about placement, promotion and graduation. The existing review checks privacy and security; the system now says it must add algorithmic bias, equity and instructional effectiveness.

International schools should not copy this policy whole. They should use it to interrogate universal traffic-light charts. Age, task, supervision, dosage and evidence are separate controls, and New York has written all five into one public architecture. An EC–12 school can ask whether a Grade 4 tutor and a Grade 11 career simulation really belong under the same permission. The weakness is equally instructive: NYCPS calls its pilot evaluation plan limited and has not published outcome measures. A one-year moratorium earns its name only if the year produces evidence strong enough to change the next policy. Otherwise it is merely a ban with an expiry date.

## Other stories

- **Workspace Studio can now move and copy Drive files, reply in Chat and answer email threads.** Google’s four new Flow steps move agentic automation from drafting into consequential action. Administrators may disable each step and can require user approval where an action may share data outside the organisation. Drive and Chat controls began rolling out on 1 September, with features due from 8 September; Gmail controls and features follow on 8 and 14 September. The steps are listed for Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus as well as the two AI add-ons ([Google Workspace Updates, 2 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/automate-drive-gmail-and-google-chat-actions-with-new-steps-in-Workspace-Studio.html?hl=en)). The first safe school prototype is not a clever mail responder. It is a de-identified folder-copy flow with a named owner, a destination allow-list, a duplicate test, an audit read-back and a kill switch. External-sharing approval matters, but an automated internal misfile can still expose a student record to the wrong group.

- **Gemini’s custom instructions now follow a user across five Workspace surfaces.** A preference saved in Ask Gemini in Drive or Chat can shape responses there and in the Gemini side panels for Slides, Sheets and Gmail; the user can view and manage the instructions in Personalization settings. Rollout began yesterday for customers with access to the relevant Gemini surfaces, and Google says there is no administrator control ([Google Workspace Updates, 2 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/custom-instructions-for-gemini-in-Workspace-now-available-in-more-apps.html?m=1)). That turns a personal convenience into a persistent input to school work. An educator may save a tone, rubric habit or output format in one app and forget that it will influence another. It also means two colleagues can ask the same question against the same file and receive differently conditioned answers. School guidance should treat these instructions like templates: inspectable, resettable and inappropriate for student names, confidential context or standing instructions to bypass verification. Consistency is useful only when the hidden premise remains valid.

- **Google Vids will turn a document into a narrated video without an admin switch for the feature.** The new workflow accepts Google Docs, PDFs and Word files, then generates a script, narration and visuals. It is available now on Rapid Release domains and begins Scheduled Release rollout on 5 September for Education Plus, Google AI Pro for Education and Teaching and Learning customers; Google lists no feature-specific administrator or end-user setting ([Google Workspace Updates, 2 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/turn-google-docs-pdfs-and-word-files-into-video-summaries-in-Google-Vids.html?hl=en)). Schools could cheaply create multilingual staff explainers or alternate representations of dense guidance. The workflow also creates a seductive compression problem: a fluent video can omit a qualification, misread a table or invent a visual relationship that the source never made. Format conversion is not evidence preservation. Test fidelity against the original, preserve a source link and transcript, and do not feed it a document whose audience was never meant to include the eventual viewers.

- **A large US survey says classroom AI use has outrun both training and parent understanding.** Morning Consult surveyed 1,019 K–12 professionals and 1,029 parents online in July for IBM, with a reported margin of error of three percentage points for each group. Among classroom educators, 76% in middle school and 73% in high school reported at least weekly AI use, versus 45% in elementary school; only 20% of educators said they had received extensive AI training. Seventy-seven per cent of parents wanted input into classroom AI use, yet 20% said they clearly understood the guidance their child received. IBM released the results alongside a fellowship for up to 100 New York-area education leaders this autumn ([IBM, 2 September 2026](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-09-02-new-ibm-study-finds-ai-adoption-is-outpacing-k-12-readiness)). The sponsorship and self-report design rule out prevalence or impact claims. The governance signal still travels: a school should be able to show families its age bands, permitted tasks, data rules and appeal route before it celebrates adoption.

- **State AI guidance is talking around the academic-integrity problem.** Chalkbeat reviewed the District of Columbia and at least 37 states with school-AI recommendations. It found that guidance often spends little space on cheating, while more than a third of states warn schools not to rely solely on AI detectors and frequently offer little beyond acceptable-use language. Half of the ten largest US districts explicitly discourage detection tools. The reporting also finds no settled definition covering the grey zone between outlining, copy-editing and submitting generated prose ([Chalkbeat, 2 September 2026](https://www.chalkbeat.org/2026/09/02/state-ai-guidance-schools-skirts-cheating-teachers/)). That is not a reason to buy a better detector. Ed Tech Coaches can help teams work at the assessment-pattern level: declare permitted assistance, preserve process evidence, include an oral or in-class checkpoint, and ask students to explain consequential choices. Make the evidentiary burden proportionate to the consequence. A policy that names misconduct without redesigning evidence simply transfers an unsolved detection problem to each teacher.

- **A five-student statistics study usefully shows what a tiny qualitative study can and cannot tell us.** University researchers observed undergraduates using Rtutor.AI to turn plain-language requests into R code for a multivariable scatterplot. Students with stronger prior statistical knowledge structured prompts around graph components and used outputs for reflection; weaker understanding produced more open-ended trial and error, and the tool sometimes selected analytical elements before the student had done so. The authors argue that constrained automation may remove syntax friction without replacing the need for statistical judgement ([American Statistical Association, 1 September 2026](https://magazine.amstat.org/blog/2026/09/01/studentsusingai/)). Five participants, one task and an older version of the tool cannot establish a learning effect or support a product decision. The value for school design is granular: decide explicitly which friction is expendable and which thinking must remain observable, then inspect student traces rather than assuming a constrained interface preserves cognition. The narrowness is a feature for hypothesis formation, not a licence to generalise.

## On the radar

- **Kansas is considering one instructional-technology policy that includes purpose, access, digital wellbeing and AI.** The draft goes to the State Board next week and would require school systems to address intended use, student access, digital citizenship, wellbeing and artificial intelligence ([Kansas State Department of Education, 2 September 2026](https://www.ksde.gov/news-center/news-releases/2026/09/02/state-board-to-consider-draft-policy-for-instructional-technology)). Watch the actual draft, board amendments and whether districts receive an implementation template. Treating AI as part of the wider technology ecology could be more useful than an isolated chatbot policy, but only if the final instrument contains decisions rather than headings.

- **The UAE is extending its AI curriculum across public and private schools.** The Cabinet says a public-school pilot produced positive results, approved national implementation across both sectors, and reviewed a programme to train 22,000 educators in AI-supported teaching, assessment, curriculum analysis and lesson planning ([UAE Government Media Office, 2 September 2026](https://prod.mediaoffice.ae/en/news/2026/september/02-09/mohammed-bin-rashid-chairs-uae-cabinet-meeting)). No evaluation design, cohort size or outcome figures are publicly available. The implementation documents will matter to international schools and national providers: curriculum sequence, teacher assessment, language coverage, student-data rules, implementation timetable and what private-school compliance actually requires.

- **Google Meet room hardware is moving from ChromeOS to Android.** Google says existing ChromeOS devices retain their published support, extending to September 2030 for recent models, while new large-format Android devices are planned for 2027 with Logitech, Neat and HP Poly among the partners ([Google Workspace Updates, 1 September 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/transitioning-google-meet-room-hardware-to-focus-on-Android-AOSP-ongoing-ChromeOS-support-unchanged-and-up-to-September-2030.html)). This is a procurement-clock story for school AV teams: inventory model-specific support dates before replacing functional room hardware, and require any new purchase to state its operating-system path, update window and third-party meeting support rather than merely its Meet certification.

## Sceptic's corner

The easy split this morning is prohibition versus progress. New York bans student-facing generative AI through Grade 8; IBM reports frequent secondary-school use; state guidance struggles to say what to do about integrity. Pick a side, attach a principle, and the argument writes itself.

The policy details resist that theatre. New York permits assistive technology, teacher planning, high-school literacy modules and tightly dosed pilots while excluding AI from consequential staff decisions ([New York City Public Schools, 2 September 2026](https://www.schools.nyc.gov/about-us/policies/guidance-on-artificial-intelligence)). That is not anti-AI. It is a claim that exposure should follow developmental purpose and institutional evidence. IBM’s survey does not rebut it: reported frequency says nothing about learning quality, and the same survey finds limited training and weak parent understanding ([IBM, 2 September 2026](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-09-02-new-ibm-study-finds-ai-adoption-is-outpacing-k-12-readiness)). Nor does an integrity crisis prove that detectors or blanket prohibitions work; the available guidance is thin precisely because the evidence is thin ([Chalkbeat, 2 September 2026](https://www.chalkbeat.org/2026/09/02/state-ai-guidance-schools-skirts-cheating-teachers/)).

The sceptical position should apply to both camps. Do not call a ban evidence-based merely because it is cautious. Do not call widespread use inevitable merely because a survey reports it. For each age band, publish the allowed purpose, supervision, dosage, evidence collected, review date and threshold for stopping or expanding. The line for school leaders is simpler: a pause is only responsible if someone uses it to learn.
