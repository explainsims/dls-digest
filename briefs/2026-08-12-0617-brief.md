## Top story

**OpenAI has put the brake and accelerator beside each other.** On 7 August the company said preliminary tests of its unreleased Astra model were strong enough that it could not rule out the Critical cyber threshold in its Preparedness Framework. That threshold means a system may be able to find and develop functional zero-day exploits across hardened real-world systems without human help, or devise and execute a novel attack from only a high-level goal. OpenAI has paused Astra work that does not meet stricter controls, including isolated testing, restricted network and tool access, stronger model-weight protection, universal monitoring and sandboxed execution ([OpenAI, 7 August](https://openai.com/index/responding-next-frontier-critical-cyber-capabilities/)).

Three days later, OpenAI released GPT-5.6-Cyber to approved Daybreak Red users. The specialist model is deliberately trained to reduce refusals on advanced dual-use tasks. In OpenAI's internal completion test it answered 95.0% of requests involving exploit chains, authentication bypass and privilege escalation, against 1.5% for ordinary GPT-5.6 Sol and 57.3% for GPT-5.5-Cyber. OpenAI says the new model remains High rather than Critical, and reports that it found a two-vulnerability Chrome V8 chain, at least five mobile-operating-system flaws, three critical database vulnerabilities and more than 400 kernel privilege-escalation issues. Access controls include identity verification, monitoring, legal attestations and mandatory hardware security keys for individual accounts from 1 September ([OpenAI, 10 August](https://openai.com/index/expanding-daybreak-as-the-cyber-defense-window-narrows/)).

The education relevance is not that schools need an offensive-security model. It is that one vendor has now published a live example of capability being separated by user, purpose, environment and control strength rather than by a single approved-tool label. That is the better shape for any school agent: ordinary tasks should not inherit network access, production credentials or broad file permissions merely because a more capable workflow sometimes needs them. The less theatrical lesson is the useful one. Access is a design decision, not a compliment paid to the user.

## Other stories

- **An AI-first private-school chain is attempting scale before the evidence has caught up.** Alpha School plans to grow from roughly a dozen campuses to about 50 during the 2026 school year, opening in 27 markets including Boston, Chicago, Denver, Nashville and Raleigh. Founder MacKenzie Price says pupils spend no more than two hours a day using adaptive software for core subjects, with the rest devoted to workshops and life skills. Axios notes that independent research remains limited, while Penn State professor Gerald LeTendre argues that AI-school models risk treating tutoring and teaching as the same thing ([Axios Dallas, 6 August](https://www.axios.com/local/dallas/2026/08/06/alpha-schools-ai-north-texas); [Axios Boston, 7 August](https://www.axios.com/local/boston/2026/08/07/alpha-schools-ai-boston)). The scale makes this more than a curiosity, but not yet a result. International schools should read the model as a useful provocation about timetable design and personalised practice, not as evidence that two hours of software replaces a coherent curriculum, disciplinary expertise or classroom culture. The first questions are about attrition, comparable cohorts, transfer beyond software-selected tasks and outcomes after pupils leave the model.

- **Excel-to-Sheets conversion has stopped flattening two common data structures.** Google's two 11 August releases say imported Excel tables now arrive as native Sheets tables, while pivot tables backed by table ranges retain their live links instead of becoming static grids. Separately, Connected Sheets can now take a grid range as a multi-value query parameter and display readable aliases for BigQuery column names without changing the underlying database reference ([Google Workspace Updates: imports](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/improved-file-import-google-sheets-tables.html); [Connected Sheets](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/new-usability-features-connected-sheets-google.html)). This is unglamorous and directly useful in Google Workspace schools. A departmental workbook, external dataset or exported report can now survive migration with less invisible repair. The catch is precisely that improved fidelity encourages confidence: formulas, named ranges, filters, charts and permissions still deserve a known-good comparison after conversion. Two representative files and a short import checklist would be more valuable than a broad announcement.

- **ChatGPT's free tier is becoming capable enough that account governance matters more than feature scarcity.** OpenAI announced on 6 August that GPT-5.6 Luna would become the default for free users, with unlimited ordinary text chats and a Think control for harder questions. The company says responses containing at least one factual error were 62% less common than with the previous GPT-5.5 Instant default; paid users receive an updated Sol model plus control over reasoning effort ([Axios, 6 August](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/06/openai-chatgpt-upgrades-luna-free-paid)). That is a vendor comparison, not an independent accuracy rate, and unlimited excludes other products such as Codex. The education consequence is still real: teachers and students can reach a stronger assistant without a formal rollout or a deliberate model choice. Guidance that assumes weak free tools will quietly expire. Durable boundaries need to state which account to use, what data may enter, when AI involvement must be disclosed and what evidence still needs independent checking.

- **Google's AI reorganisation is a reminder that platform stability and product ubiquity are different things.** Demis Hassabis is leaving the Google DeepMind chief-executive role to become its chair and Alphabet's chief scientist. DeepMind chief technology officer Koray Kavukcuoglu will run the unit as a senior vice-president reporting to Sundar Pichai; longtime Google scientist Jeff Dean is also leaving to form a startup with three colleagues. Axios places the change against a delayed Gemini 3.5 Pro, researcher departures and stronger frontier competition, while Google says the leadership moves were not linked to model delays ([Axios, 6 August](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/06/googles-ai-leadership-shuffle)). Schools do not need to read executive tea leaves. They do need to remember that Classroom and Workspace integration can feel permanent while the research organisation beneath Gemini is being reworked. That argues for stable teaching practices, exportable source material and evaluations tied to tasks rather than model names. A roadmap is a vendor's present intention; it is not part of a curriculum.

- **Meta's newest AI manifesto makes a business model sound like a constitutional principle.** Mark Zuckerberg published a 6,500-word case for widely distributed, open AI on 10 August, arguing that concentrated control is the greater risk and imagining personal agents able to tutor, invent and advise at expert level. The same day Meta released the local Muse Glimmer model, and promised developer access to the more capable Muse Spark 1.2 ([Associated Press, 10 August](https://apnews.com/article/df8a4e7d7825470d09e8090367457c2c)). Open weights can genuinely improve inspectability, local deployment and adaptation; Meta also benefits from a world in which models are commodities distributed through products reaching billions of people. Both statements can be true. For any school, the important distinction is between openness of model weights, openness of training evidence and actual control of the surrounding service. A downloadable model may reduce data leaving a device while increasing local responsibility for updates, filters, logging and misuse. Local deployment also moves the maintenance question into view: who patches the model-serving stack, checks its outputs after an update, and retires a version when its surrounding software is no longer supported?

## On the radar

- Columbia University began a two-part [AI literacy course for student-facing administrators](https://etc.cuit.columbia.edu/events/student-facing-administrators-ai-literacy-basic-training-august-2026) on 11 August, covering prompting, security and workflow integration, with a second session on 13 August and follow-up clinics. Watch for whether the materials become public and whether the role-based framing separates advising, records work and student support rather than teaching generic chatbot technique. A reusable role map would travel better across education than another universal prompt guide. The useful evidence will be the decisions participants can make afterwards, not course completion.

- Illinois held its [state AI-guidance introduction](https://www.k12webinars.com/) on 10 August. The webinar promised practical use cases, legal and policy considerations, and customisable implementation tools. The guidance itself is not today's news; the current question is what survived contact with district leaders. Watch for a recording, templates and the first evidence that the document can be turned into decisions without every school independently digesting a large framework. The best signal would be a role-specific tool that exposes disagreements instead of hiding them.

- EDUCAUSE's [AI Shockwaves series](https://events.educause.edu/webinar/2026/navigating-ai-shockwaves-in-higher-education) moved from use cases on 6 August to organisational confidence on 10 August, with data readiness and cybersecurity sessions due on 17 and 19 August. The title is marketing; the sequence is sensible. Watch whether the later sessions define AI-ready data as governed, permissioned and current rather than merely centralised, and whether their cyber advice reaches agent permissions and audit logs rather than stopping at phishing awareness. Higher education is the setting; the control questions travel directly into schools.

## Sceptic's corner

OpenAI's pair of announcements invites a flattering interpretation: the lab discovered a dangerous threshold, paused, built stronger controls and then placed related capability with trusted defenders. Parts of that are genuinely better than a silent release. The awkward detail is that the company is also the evaluator, threshold-setter, access authority and beneficiary of the new programme.

The published safeguards are concrete but uneven. Hardware keys reduce account takeover; they do not prove that a customer has a legitimate target. Legal attestations establish a rule; they do not enforce scope inside a tool session. Monitoring can catch suspicious activity only if the signals are visible and the response arrives before an agent completes the action. OpenAI itself recommends isolation from sensitive production systems and the open internet, which is less a reassurance than an admission that identity checks are not the containment layer.

Education should resist importing the drama while keeping the architecture. Most school AI use is nowhere near critical cyber capability. Yet the pattern scales down cleanly: capability should be matched to the smallest necessary permission, risky actions should require review, logs should be readable, and known-bad tests should prove that boundaries hold. Trust is not a user category conferred at sign-up. It is a claim that has to survive the next tool call.
