## Top story

**South Korea is bringing entrance-exam security into high-school finals.** Acting Education Minister Choi Eun-ok took a new plan for school assessments to Cabinet on 24 November, after the controversy over rules for last week's national university entrance exam. The ministry says smart glasses had already figured in cheating incidents in middle- and high-school assessments this year. High schools are first in line to apply the new measures during second-semester finals. Middle schools are to use the plan as a reference when tightening their own supervision. [South Korean Ministry of Education, 24 November](https://www.moe.go.kr/boardCnts/viewRenew.do?boardID=294&boardSeq=107814&lev=0&m=020402&opType=N&page=1&s=moe&searchType=null&statusYN=W).

The plan calls for phones and other electronics to be collected, bags to be kept away from exam rooms and school Wi-Fi to be switched off. It envisages metal-detector checks for all students, with advance notice to students and parents and prior consent. Some seniors had already started their finals on Monday, so the ministry gave schools steps they could use immediately, including using detectors when staff suspect a violation or a student re-enters the room. The proposed academic penalty for possessing or using a prohibited smart device moves from zero in the affected subject to zero in every subject examined that day. The ministry also plans to recommend that schools strengthen their disciplinary rules, with expulsion as a possible sanction at high-school level. [Education ministry summary on Korea's policy portal, 24 November](https://www.korea.kr/news/policyNewsView.do?newsId=148976387).

That change gives school leaders two distinct decisions. Running a room fairly requires a way to handle a discrepancy at the door without delaying everyone else's paper. Imposing a penalty across several subjects requires a reliable record of what happened, a chance for the student to respond and someone with authority to decide on the evidence. At an international school, those decisions may sit with different people. The greater the consequences of the first report, the more it matters that the invigilator knows precisely what to record before the next session starts.

## Other stories

- **Teachers delegated lesson drafts and kept the learning objectives.** Muhammad Saleh Farooq, Muhammad Ahmad Khan and Muhammad Jon Raza interviewed 13 teachers, four administrators and one student in Punjab, Pakistan, and coded teachers' responses across 12 tasks. Nine teachers accepted a draft or substantial AI help for lesson planning. None accepted AI-drafted learning objectives, and those willing to use AI for marking wanted it to have the rubric or marking criteria. Three government-school teachers declined to use AI to mark quizzes even when they had an answer key. They thought students expected to see the teacher's effort. The preprint draws on reported practice and interview scenarios in a small, purposive sample, with no observation of how teachers checked actual outputs. Its practical insight is that the smallest unit for a school AI guideline may be a step inside the task. A teacher can ask for four practice examples, check each against the textbook and still own the decision about what students are learning and why. [Farooq, Khan and Raza, arXiv preprint, 20 November](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.27533v1).

- **UNESCO puts time for professional learning on the timetable.** UNESCO and the International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030 launched their new Global Report on Teachers in Addis Ababa on 23 November. UNESCO says 45% of countries with complete data require continuing professional development across every level from pre-primary through secondary education. That figure describes requirements in policy. The report also describes unequal access, especially in rural, underserved and crisis-affected settings, and argues for learning tailored to career stage and context, including support for AI. Its recommendations cover mentoring, peer collaboration and working conditions that give teachers time to learn. Ed Tech Coaches can put that into practice by designing the follow-up as carefully as the workshop. Give teachers time to bring one assignment from their own subject, try the method, and come back with where it helped or failed. A school can count attendance on the first day and learn far more about classroom fit from the work teachers bring back. [UNESCO launch statement, 23 November](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/new-unesco-global-report-lifelong-teacher-professional-development-must-be-right).

- **Google plans to bring Drive files and Gemini chats into AI Mode in Search.** Its 23 November announcement starts the opt-in Wallet rollout first, with Drive and the Gemini app following in the coming weeks. Google's help pages describe the connected-content experience as a personal-account feature for adults and list Drive access in English in the US. The help text also says summaries and excerpts from connected files can be used to train generative models, and disconnecting an app leaves existing Search Services History for a user to manage separately. For a school, the relevant copy might be the one a teacher downloaded to edit at home and saved to a personal Drive. A data-handling rule that only names the school account leaves that copy unaddressed. The announcement is a reminder to describe what can travel with a file, and the terms that apply to whichever account has the copy. [Google Search announcement, 23 November](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/google-search-personal-intelligence-apps/) · [Google Search Help](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/16859283?co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop&hl=en).

## On the radar

- **Singapore and China have agreed to test education AI together.** Their education ministries signed a memorandum on 23 November. The National University of Singapore's AI Centre for Educational Technologies will work with the Shanghai Institute of AI for Education at East China Normal University. The agreement covers testing technology, exchanging AI-enabled teaching practices and developing ways to assess vocational and human skills. The first protocols and results are worth watching. A classroom test that can travel between two systems would have to say what counted as a successful lesson and how the researchers judged it in each curriculum. [NUS AI Centre for Educational Technologies, 23 November](https://aicet.comp.nus.edu.sg/aicet-to-lead-ai-in-education-cooperation-under-new-singapore-china-mou/).

- **Qatar has launched a curriculum-linked school AI project.** The education and communications ministries and Qatar University launched two projects with Scale AI as technical partner on 17 November. The Smart Assistant is intended to tailor school support to a pupil's level and help teachers plan and monitor progress. The separate university project supports academic advising. The school project is worth following for how teachers can check what the assistant tells a pupil. A useful evaluation would show the source the tool used, how it handles material outside the expected unit and what happens when a teacher corrects an answer. [Qatar Ministry of Education and Higher Education, 17 November launch](https://www.edu.gov.qa/en/News/Details/21369000) · [Qatar News Agency, 17 November](https://qna.org.qa/en/news/news-details?date=17%2F11%2F2026&id=qatar-launches-two-national-ai-projects-to-enhance-school-learning-university-academic-advising).

## Sceptic's corner

Giving a teacher an AI draft can save time. The attractive version of that claim assumes the review will be quick because the teacher already knows the subject. The Punjab interviews suggest what can disappear from that calculation. Teachers had to check outputs against the textbook, consider the class and sometimes show students what care had gone into the work. The study gives those costs names. It leaves the size of the time saving an open question. [Farooq, Khan and Raza, 20 November](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.27533v1).

Take an exit ticket that asks pupils to explain why two fractions are equivalent. Generating eight plausible questions is quick. The teacher still has to inspect whether a student can answer by spotting a pattern on the page, using a shortcut the class already knows, or giving the very explanation the lesson was meant to develop. All three can produce the same answer. The review gets easier if the tool shows the objective and expected reasoning beside each question, and a colleague can help examine an uncertain item. UNESCO's case for time to learn with peers has a practical application here. [UNESCO, 23 November](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/new-unesco-global-report-lifelong-teacher-professional-development-must-be-right).

The fair comparison is two versions of the same task, each ready to hand to pupils. Count the minutes through checking and correction, then see which version elicits the reasoning the teacher meant to assess. That is the saving a department could defend when someone asks what improved.
