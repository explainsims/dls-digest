## Top story

**Google is retiring Gems, and education gets a different migration clock.** In its 30 September announcement, Google set out a transition to skills: reusable instructions applied inside a Gemini conversation, including several skills in one prompt, rather than opening a separate customised assistant. Workspace skills start rolling out on 5 October for Rapid Release domains and 19 October for Scheduled Release; Gemini-app skills start on 13 October. Education Gems will be withdrawn no sooner than 1 June 2027, three months after the earliest business and enterprise date. These are scheduled transitions, not features confirmed in a school's account today ([Google Workspace Updates, 30 September](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/skills-gemini-app-workspace.html)).

The migration has a seam worth noticing. Skills currently do not sync between the Gemini app and Workspace. Workspace skills require an eligible licence, including Google AI Pro for Education, and users aged 18 or older; Gemini-app skills are available to all ages. Remaining app Gems will become inactive draft skills requiring review and activation ([Google's admin transition guidance, updated 30 September](https://knowledge.workspace.google.com/admin/generative-ai/gemini-app/about-the-transition-to-skills)). The education guide says Gems created in Classroom or a third-party LMS through Gemini LTI will not automatically migrate, because skills will not be available directly in those tools. Google instead points educators toward Gemini Notebook and teacher-led Guided Learning; the latter is still described as coming soon to Classroom ([education transition guide](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/gemini_gems_skills_transition_guide.pdf)).

For international schools, the useful distinction is a staff production recipe versus a student learning experience. A rubric-formatting skill can preserve instructions; it cannot by itself preserve the teacher's view of student activity, curriculum sources or a tutoring boundary. Classroom, an LMS integration and the standalone Gemini app have different migration paths; each school needs to identify the surface it actually uses. The practical first move is to identify any genuinely used Gems and their surfaces before rebuilding them. Carry the instruction, source files, sharing arrangements and intended learner behaviour into a test together. A new button with the same name is a poor continuity check; a teacher completing the same task, with the same permitted data and visible review, is a better one.

## Other stories

- **A new classroom-trust report gives AI policy a conversation to have, rather than another paragraph to sign.** Harvard's Center for Digital Thriving published *An AI Policy Isn't a Playbook* in September, with fresh reporting on 30 September. It draws on 1,018 US public-school teachers and principals surveyed in April–May 2025, plus interviews including 31 young people aged 15–19 in April–June 2026. Among educators who reported an AI dilemma and described it, 74% of teacher descriptions and 69% of principal descriptions concerned cheating; those are not percentages of all educators. Young people described hiding AI use, pressure to keep up, and sometimes deliberately weakening their work to avoid accusations. The report offers five classroom activities, including mapping acceptable and unacceptable uses before an assignment ([primary report and playbook](https://digitalthriving.gse.harvard.edu/an-ai-policy-isnt-a-playbook/); [30 September coverage](https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/news/story/new-report-ai-impacting-trust-students-teachers-136874802)). The activities have been refined through practice, not tested in controlled studies. That still gives an Ed Tech Coach something useful to try: put three uses of AI against one actual assignment and ask students to explain where the boundary lies. Keep the learning expectation explicit, and let the discussion reveal ambiguities before an accusation has to resolve them. Listening is a design input; it need not mean abandoning standards.

- **Google's document APIs can now propose changes for review, with a failure mode the developer must actually check.** The 30 September Workspace release adds programmatic comments across Docs, Sheets and Slides, plus suggested edits in Docs. The stated rollout begins that day and may take up to 15 days; the corresponding MCP-server comment support remains a developer preview ([Google release](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/programmatic-comment-and-suggestion.html)). Google's updated Docs documentation shows a suggestion mode and warns that document-model changes may commit while associated comment or suggestion threads fail to save. It supplies a response field for checking whether those thread updates were saved ([Docs API guidance, updated 30 September](https://developers.google.com/workspace/docs/api/how-tos/suggestions)). For school workflows, this could let an assistant flag a broken citation, inconsistent rubric wording or a missing accessibility description in the document where a teacher can review it. But a suggestion is only a human checkpoint if someone remains responsible for accepting it. An integration that reports success after an API response without reading back the comment may leave a revised document with its review trail missing. The sensible prototype is a small, non-sensitive document: confirm the location and suggested change, test a rejected suggestion, and check the final visible state. Institutional access to the API does not settle which third-party application should receive access to school files.

- **An audit of online history materials found that trusted platforms can still carry a narrow source base.** In a 28 September research release, University of Kansas scholars Anna Yonas and Stephen Jackson described their analysis of 46 sets of middle- and high-school resources across OER Project, PBS, DIG and C3 Teachers. They report that 85.7% of the primary sources in the examined materials were recorded by colonizers, with few from colonized peoples. Some resources also framed imperialism as a pros-and-cons exercise; the pattern differed by platform, and the researchers praised OER Project for including varied perspectives and questions about resistance ([University of Kansas, 28 September](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/study-finds-online-educational-resources-present-imperialism-in-positive-light-put-students-in-inappropriate-discussions)). This is fresh institutional research coverage, not a classroom experiment. For an international school, this is an ed-tech quality problem before AI touches it: a credible logo does not make a resource's framing neutral. A source-grounded chatbot can faithfully reproduce a lopsided source pack. The useful teacher check is whose voice appears, what the task asks students to adjudicate, and whether the sources support that question. Asking students to examine how a system operated or how people resisted it is a different intellectual task from asking them to balance somebody else's rights against an empire's gains.

- **Anthropic's robotics index separates technical exposure from the price of replacing a person.** Its 30 September study estimates that present-day robots can perform 74% of US physical job tasks in at least some settings, representing 34% of working hours, but are cost-competitive for only 0.3% of job tasks. The authors use Claude with web search to assess task capabilities and estimate deployment costs, drawing on occupational data and documented robot deployments, sales or demonstrations. They distinguish purpose-built robot environments, structured human workplaces and unstructured settings; the headline includes capability in the most restricted environments ([Anthropic research, 30 September](https://www.anthropic.com/research/what-work-can-robots-do)). This is a vendor-produced exposure model with judgement calls and approximate costs, not a forecast that three-quarters of physical jobs disappear. For careers guidance, the distinction travels well: a machine welding a prepared joint does not mean it can position materials, inspect quality and manage every awkward part of a welder's day economically. Students need to understand jobs as bundles of tasks, relationships and constraints, rather than choose subjects from a list of supposedly safe occupations. The study also complicates the easy claim that only desk work faces AI disruption. Technical and vocational pathways deserve AI literacy too, alongside the physical and interpersonal expertise that makes work useful in the first place.

- **Google Vids pairs a more expressive synthetic voice with more flexible captions; the second deserves equal attention.** On 29 September Google said it had upgraded Vids voiceovers and avatar narration to Gemini 3.8 Flash Lite TTS, claiming more natural pacing and faster generation. On 30 September it added caption controls for font, typography, position and animation. Both posts say the features are available now, with voiceover availability including Education Plus and Google AI Pro for Education; the caption update also lists Education Fundamentals and Standard ([voiceover update, 29 September](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/create-more-natural-expressive-ai-voiceovers-in-Google-Vids-with-upgraded-Gemini-3.8-Flash-Lite-TTS.html); [captions, 30 September](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/customize-style-of-your-captions-in-Google-Vids.html)). Naturalness is Google's product claim, not independent evidence of better instruction. For teacher-made explanations, an intelligible script, correct pronunciation and readable captions matter more than an impressive narrator. Caption styling can help a multilingual learner follow a demonstration, or make it harder if animated words compete with the diagram. The useful review is to watch once with the sound off and once on a small screen, checking technical terms, contrast, timing and whether captions obscure the thing being explained. A polished voice can make an error sound more assured; a reviewed transcript gives teachers and learners something concrete to check.

## On the radar

- **A new literacy evaluation is a useful control for the AI professional-learning conversation.** SRI's 29 September final report on Reading Apprenticeship describes sustained training and coaching for secondary teachers, with implementation across 19 North Carolina districts and expansion to six in Pennsylvania. Its matched-school analysis found positive effects on science course grades after one and two years, but no effects on attendance or end-of-course standardised tests; classroom implementation was limited ([SRI report](https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/evaluation-of-accelerating-literacy-through-self-regulation-collaboration-and-persistence-reading-apprenticeship-north-carolina-final-report/)). Watch the implementation evidence when judging teacher development: attendance at training and changed classroom practice are different outcomes. A school buying AI PD should expect that distinction to be measured too.

- **Encrypted Slides gained a PowerPoint import route, within an existing beta.** Google's 24 September update says eligible organisations can import and convert PowerPoint files into client-side encrypted Slides. Existing CSE interoperability beta participants have access; new applicants need acceptance. Education Standard and Plus are among the eligible editions ([Google Workspace Updates, 24 September](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/now-in-beta-import-and-convert-your.html)). Watch whether this closes a real document-handling gap for institutions already using client-side encryption. An import feature is not a general recommendation to turn encryption on: ownership, key access and the practical ability of an authorised colleague to open a protected file still determine whether the workflow works.

## Sceptic's corner

The attractive promise in the skills transition is portability: write an instruction once and take it between AI products. Google's use of the Markdown-based SKILL.md format makes that more plausible, and its announcement explicitly invites importing skills created elsewhere ([30 September announcement](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/09/skills-gemini-app-workspace.html)). But a portable instruction is not a portable institutional control. The same text asking an assistant to check citations can run with different source access, permissions, model behaviour and output tools. Even within Google's products, Gemini-app skills currently do not appear automatically in Workspace, and education Gems embedded in Classroom or an LMS have a different destination from app Gems.

That is not a reason to reject the standard. It is a reason to define what is supposed to survive the move. If a teacher's existing helper uses three approved readings, declines to write the final essay and exposes student progress, copying its instructions establishes only one part of continuity. The source pack, refusal behaviour and teacher visibility each need a separate check. Google's education guide describes replacement learning tools; it does not promise that any arbitrary Gem will retain its behaviour through conversion ([transition guide](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/gemini_gems_skills_transition_guide.pdf)).

The purchasing question is therefore smaller than whether a vendor supports skills: can the institution reproduce a useful, bounded workflow after the model, account or interface changes? Reusable prompts may reduce setup work. They do not remove the need to demonstrate the result to the person who will teach with it.
