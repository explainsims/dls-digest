## Top story

**More educators have had AI training, and the follow-up is thin.** In its [16 November reporting on a nationally representative US survey](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/teachers-pinpoint-whats-missing-in-professional-development-about-ai/2026/11), Education Week says about two-thirds of 852 teachers, school administrators and district leaders surveyed from 26 August to 6 October reported receiving at least one session on using AI in their work. That is up 11 percentage points from the previous year's 56%. In interviews, educators singled out familiar problems: too little time to work on their own materials, too little feedback while they were trying things, and too little room to talk about drawbacks, including what to do about suspected misuse in assessed work.

A [separate survey of 268 US teacher educators](https://epe.brightspotcdn.com/ca/91/82f32d9547cebf6209d6641c33db/ai-and-teacher-preparation-survey-report-final-11-13-26.pdf), reported in the [same 16 November package](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/ai-raises-big-questions-for-the-future-of-teacher-education/2026/11), shows how uneven the preparation can be before a teacher even starts. Sixty per cent said decisions about whether to include an AI focus were left to individual instructors. Fourteen per cent reported ongoing training with follow-up from their institution. Thirty-four per cent wanted that format and another 34% wanted more than one session without ongoing follow-up. These percentages describe the teacher educators themselves. Neither survey tests whether a participant can judge an AI output or whether a different format improves teaching.

For international schools, the useful design question is what staff can bring back to a second session. An English teacher could bring a rewritten discussion question and the evidence from the text that made them discard the first draft. A maths teacher could compare two explanations of the same wrong answer and show where one skipped the student's reasoning. Both can work from invented examples in the training room. A colleague should be able to reproduce the judgement in a fresh example a fortnight later. An attendance figure can tell a leader whom the launch reached. I would give the second session the job of showing what travelled back to a classroom.

## Other stories

- **Gemini separates chat history from model training.** In a [16 November announcement](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/gemini-app/updated-controls/), Google said the Gemini app will let users retain a chat history while opting out of generative AI training. It says chats started with the new History setting off will still be retained for around two months so it can respond, process feedback and protect users and its services. The company plans to start a global rollout over the coming weeks. The [Workspace privacy documentation](https://knowledge.workspace.google.com/admin/generative-ai/generative-ai-in-google-workspace-privacy-hub) sets out the terms for eligible managed accounts, including its commitment that Google does not have people review content or use it to train generative AI models outside the domain without permission. This is a useful distinction for a teacher who opens the same app under two accounts. In a training screenshot, identify the signed-in account and specify what the control governs: whether a conversation appears in history, how long it is retained, and whether it can go into model training are three separate questions. The account is the first thing to check before using the setting to make a decision about school data.

- **An Asia-Pacific AI training fund backs local delivery.** On [11 November, AVPN announced](https://avpn.asia/about/press/ai-opportunity-fund-phase-three-partners/) 28 implementing partners across 19 markets for the third phase of its Google.org- and Asian Development Bank-backed fund. India's Pratham Education Foundation plans to adapt existing material for critical classroom use, including when to set AI aside. JA Korea plans to pair training with formal certification for public school educators and outreach to rural teachers. The Raspberry Pi Foundation is joining as a strategic partner to help local organisations adapt Experience AI content. AVPN also announced a learning platform in 16 languages that uses a short assessment to recommend a pathway. The numbers describe the scope of a programme whose next phase is getting underway. The detail to watch is who changes the material. A translated example can still assume class sizes, connectivity or tool access that local teachers rarely have. A local partner with licence to change the task, the tools and the assumptions can make a more meaningful adaptation. That is worth checking before adopting a catalogue's ready-made pathway.

- **A physicist shows where an AI contribution fits in a proof.** Nathaniel Craig's [15 November first-person account](https://openai.com/index/step-toward-defining-the-standard-model/) describes the collaboration behind his [6 October preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09093), which proposes a candidate mathematical definition of the Standard Model on a spacetime grid. Craig says his strategy was to represent the same force fields on successively finer grids. GPT‑6 Astra suggested how to carry those fields onto finer grids, showed that the comparisons would converge and took the lead on the detailed proof. Craig names three lattice theorists who inspected the argument and describes work still to do, including checking a condition at finite grid spacing that helps connect the construction to a quantum theory with conserved probabilities. The news this week is the researcher's account, which gives a science class an unusually concrete example of AI disclosure. A student could use the same four headings on a much smaller investigation: the method I brought, the step the model contributed, the check somebody else can reproduce, and the limitation that still matters. Each heading asks for a different kind of evidence.

- **An accessibility study asks which parts of a task should survive an adaptation.** In [a 13 November exploratory study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.2017771/full), Gao and colleagues examined 63 digital products, collected 158 stakeholder questionnaire responses, and used five of eight interviews for their core analysis. The stakeholder work considered possible activities for autistic children in China. The lion-dance scenario captures the design question. A lion can appear as artwork during any generic exercise, or two learners can take complementary roles and coordinate movements. Lowering the sound level could make the second design easier to enter while keeping coordination as the task. Removing the need to respond to a partner changes what the task teaches. The study enrolled and observed no autistic children and measured no effects on engagement or learning. Its useful product is a question for school teams to take into a review with learners: which demand is part of the learning goal, and which makes it awkward to show what you can do? The answer may differ even for pupils working on the same activity.

- **Ofcom wants stronger protections built into more platforms.** On [16 November the UK regulator set out additional online safety measures](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/platforms-must-take-stronger-action-to-combat-child-sexual-abuse-material-online-under-new-ofcom-rules). They include stronger detection duties for certain services and banning users who generate, upload or share child sexual exploitation and abuse content. On anti-grooming rules, Ofcom says services already in scope must use highly effective age checks to determine which accounts need protections or extend those protections to all users. It expects the new duties to come into force from early 2027, subject to parliamentary process. The practical school question for an app with messaging or sharing is what a student account can do on the first day, before anybody has had a chance to find a menu or make a report. A vendor can talk through its safety features from an administrator account and leave that question unanswered. A demonstration with a student account can show who can contact it, where a report goes and which protections require a school administrator to turn them on.

## On the radar

- **ISTE+ASCD briefs teams on the 2027 AI Innovator Student Challenge.** ISTE+ASCD's [16 November kickoff webinar](https://iste-ascd.org/ai-innovator) followed the 3 November opening of submissions. Teams are educator-guided, identify a community problem tied to a UN Sustainable Development Goal, and design a solution that uses AI. Entries close on 7 February. The contest might turn up useful examples of pupils testing whether AI adds anything to a design. A local problem such as confusing recycling labels admits a printed prototype that a team can test alongside any AI idea. That comparison would say more about the project's value than the fact students got a model to run.

- **An external safety assessor will say what it was allowed to inspect.** OpenAI [announced its first phase of embedded assessments on 16 November](https://openai.com/index/our-first-phase-of-embedded-assessments/). In its [own account that day](https://transluce.org/embedded-assessment-pilot-with-openai), Transluce describes a six-week pilot, due to start in early December, examining selected post-training alignment evaluations. It plans to report what extra access it requested, what it received and how restrictions shaped its conclusions. Its agreement limits public findings to released models. For school buyers who see independent testing invoked in a pitch, the report's eventual account of its scope may offer questions worth borrowing. Which product and settings were tested, and how much of the result can a school actually read?

## Sceptic's corner

A device dashboard makes it tempting to put a number next to the quality of a lesson. Minutes are easy to report. In a [16 November Stanford interview](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/11/classroom-screen-time-stanford-expert), Hari Subramonyam asks what intellectual work the device allowed and what it displaced. He gives the example of a magazine project: selecting material, organising it and deciding how to reach readers are part of the learning. A design tool might free up time to make and revise those decisions. A generator can also make the decisions on a student's behalf. The dashboard will record activity in either case.

The case for managing time is real. If a class routinely loses ten minutes to notifications or switching tabs, a routine that gets those minutes back can help. An aggregate limit can also double as a cap on access. In the [University of Sydney's 16 November response](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2026/11/16/should-australia-ban-screens-from-classrooms-experts-respond-digital-detox.html) to Australia's digital detox debate, Catherine Smyth points to text-to-speech and other assistive uses that let students do the assigned work. Count them without context and a successful adjustment can look like a classroom that needs to cut back.

Give the clock the question it can answer. For the learning question, ask students to show a choice the task required them to make. A magazine team can show two versions of a page and explain what changed for the reader. That takes a few minutes and gives a teacher something a usage report cannot supply.
