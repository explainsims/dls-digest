# Week ending Sunday, 31 May 2026

## The Week That Was

The week did not belong to one announcement. It belonged to a narrowing gap between policy language and operational reality. Google kept pushing AI literacy training and Workspace-grounded Gemini as the ordinary school stack; Microsoft put a student study agent inside managed education tenants; OpenAI's Edu notes made reusable workspace agents look more like admin-governed objects than clever prompts; and CoSN's 2026 State of EdTech report reminded everyone that the people expected to govern all this are already underwater. ([Google AI Educator Series](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-educator-series/), [Microsoft Education](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/education/blog/2026/05/study-and-learn-ai-built-for-your-student/), [OpenAI Help Center](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/10128477-chatgpt-enterprise-edu-release-notes), [CoSN](https://www.cosn.org/tools-and-resources/resource/u-s-state-of-edtech-2026/))

The counter-current was just as visible. AFT called for age-banded screen and AI limits; Brookings argued that companion bots need a public-health frame; Canvas kept the sector's attention on breach response; and the US Student Privacy Policy Office spent the week teaching incident response and ed-tech vetting. The room is no longer asking whether AI will enter schools. It is asking which adult system catches it when it does. ([AFT](https://www.aft.org/press-release/devices-down-eyes-hands-weingarten-calls-screen-bans-ai-limits-active-learning-major), [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/from-bans-to-recalls-a-public-health-framework-for-AI-companion-bots/), [Instructure](https://www.instructure.com/incident_update), [SPPO](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/?src=fpco))

## What Does It All Mean?

The useful answer this week is that AI governance has become too broad a phrase. It now has at least three different jobs, and schools keep trying to make one committee document do all three.

The first job is **use governance**: what staff and students may do with AI, at what ages, in which accounts, and with which data. That is where AFT's age bands, Google's AI Educator Series, and the AILit exemplars belong. They are about routines, boundaries, and judgement in classrooms. ([AILit Framework](https://ailiteracyframework.org/blog/bringing-ai-literacy-to-life-exemplars-for-classroom-use/))

The second job is **platform governance**: which vendors, models, connectors, logs, admin controls, residency settings, and breach obligations a school will accept. Canvas and Naviance are the cold shower here. The issue is not only whether a product has an AI feature. It is whether the school's identity, student data, messages, analytics, and integrations can be understood when something goes wrong. ([ClassAction.org](https://www.classaction.org/news/17.25m-powerschool-settlement-resolves-class-action-over-alleged-interception-of-confidential-student-communications))

The third job is **workflow governance**, and this is the one that will sneak past people because it looks helpful. A teacher builds a rubric helper. A department builds a source-grounded policy explainer. A coach builds a trip-absence workflow. A leader asks an agent to watch a sheet and draft reminders. None of these feels like a new platform. Each one can become school infrastructure if it is shared, repeated, connected to records, or relied on by colleagues.

That third layer is where Ed Tech Coaches should spend more time than the public AI conversation usually allows. The point is not "make an AI app." It is to design small school workflows where the app makes no decisions, sends no uncontrolled messages, writes to no external system without approval, and keeps human accountability visible. Start with a human workflow that already exists, define what must remain human, then decide what technology may record, notify, or display.

This also reframes teacher publishing. AI Studio, GitHub, cloud deployment, workspace agents, and school-owned prototypes are not separate from governance; they are where governance becomes real. A school can ban too much and push useful work into shadows, or approve too vaguely and let every working demo become an unofficial system. The adult middle is boring and powerful: register the workflow, name the owner, define allowed data, set expiry, require review before consequence, and keep the human act human.

That is the sentence to carry forward: governance is no longer mainly a policy about tools. It is a design discipline for school work.

## Behind You! Behind You!

**Companion bots are separating from study tools.** Brookings' public-health frame is useful because it refuses to treat simulated relationship as just another chatbot category. For schools, the line should be simple: learning support, course-grounded help, and relationship simulation are different risk classes. Do not let one acceptable tool launder the others. ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/from-bans-to-recalls-a-public-health-framework-for-AI-companion-bots/))

**CoSN's capacity warning is the quiet centre of the week.** More districts have AI guidelines, but the report still foregrounds cybersecurity, staffing gaps, procurement weaknesses, interoperability, and support capacity. That matters because schools can write excellent AI norms and still fail if the work lands as invisible labour on IT, Ed Tech Coaches, and a few keen teachers. ([CoSN](https://www.cosn.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/U.S.-State-of-EdTech-2026.pdf))

**Khan Academy is publishing the kind of AI-tutor evidence worth respecting.** Its recent Khanmigo write-up talks about observations, interviews, transcript analysis, latency, item correctness, and cognitive engagement. It still does not prove that AI tutors solve school learning. It does show what serious iteration looks like: measure learning behaviour, not just usage or applause. ([Khan Academy](https://blog.khanacademy.org/how-khan-academy-is-building-a-better-ai-tutor-our-most-recent-learnings/))

**The boring privacy calendar is becoming the useful calendar.** The 2026 Naviance settlement hearing on 2026-06-10 and the Canvas forensic follow-through are not glamorous, but they are the reference points for parents and administrators when someone asks what student-data risk actually costs. Keep them close to any LMS or AI-platform conversation. ([ClassAction.org](https://www.classaction.org/news/17.25m-powerschool-settlement-resolves-class-action-over-alleged-interception-of-confidential-student-communications), [Instructure](https://www.instructure.com/incident_update))

## Sceptic's Corner

The phrase to squeeze after this week is **"teacher efficiency."**

It is not wrong. Teachers do need time back. A good AI routine can remove clerical sludge, make a blank page less blank, or turn a compliance ritual into something that actually helps a student. Lovely. Let us have more of that.

But efficiency is a slippery moral alibi. It can mean "a teacher spends less time on the part of the work that was never educational." It can also mean "a teacher now supervises a larger pile of machine output, handles more edge cases, and absorbs the institutional risk because the tool made the first draft look easy." Those are not the same thing.

The test is where the saved time goes and where the judgement remains. If AI shortens a workflow so the adult has more attention for a student, a colleague, or a better task, good. If it turns the adult into a tired approver of opaque system behaviour, call that what it is: workload displacement with nicer branding.

The useful school question is not "does this save time?" It is "which human act does this protect?" If nobody can answer, the efficiency is probably being harvested by the system, not returned to the teacher.
