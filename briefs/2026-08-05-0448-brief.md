## Top story

**The AI school is becoming a chain.** Alpha School plans to expand from about a dozen campuses to roughly 50 during the 2026 school year, opening in 27 markets including Atlanta, Boston, Charlotte, Chicago, Denver, Nashville and Raleigh. Founder MacKenzie Price told Axios that pupils spend no more than two hours a day on adaptive software for core subjects, then move into workshops and projects; the adults are guides rather than licensed teachers delivering lessons, guide salaries start at $100,000, and tuition runs from $45,000 to $75,000 a year ([Axios, 2 August 2026](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/02/alpha-schools-ai-expansion-50-campuses)).

That is more interesting than another clever-tutor demo because scale changes the unit of scrutiny. Alpha is now testing whether an entire school can separate instruction from teaching: software handles pacing, practice and mastery checks; highly paid adults handle motivation, projects and relationships. Price argues this reduces screen time and increases human contact. Penn State education professor Gerald LeTendre supplies the necessary brake: tutoring and teaching are not interchangeable, and a strong tutor still needs to sit inside sustained contact with a qualified teacher. Expansion proves demand and access to capital, not learning, transfer or pastoral quality.

For international schools, the useful move is neither imitation nor dismissal. Alpha makes visible the design choices ordinary schools often leave implicit: which learning is safe to compress, what counts as evidence of mastery, where subject expertise must remain human, and whether time saved by software is genuinely returned as discussion, making and feedback. Its price and self-selecting family cohort also make naïve comparisons with comprehensive schools fairly useless. Watch the chain because fifty sites will create operational evidence faster than twelve; refuse the founder’s preferred metric until independent researchers can distinguish the software, the guides, the family cohort and the extraordinary fee.

## Other stories

- **OpenAI says its unreleased Astra model produced ten substantial mathematical advances for about $2,000 in inference.** The 1 August release spans sphere packing, coding theory, non-sofic groups, arithmetic circuits, quantum games, lattice cryptography and Ramsey theory. OpenAI says Astra generated the arguments, humans prepared them into manuscripts with the model, and the model then formalised each result as a machine-checkable Lean certificate; the company explicitly credits the system rather than claiming human authorship ([OpenAI, 1 August 2026](https://openai.com/index/ten-advances-in-mathematics/)). Independent specialists still need to judge significance and exposition, but the verification architecture matters now: novel output, human editorial work and formal proof are three different layers. In a senior-secondary classroom, that distinction is more useful than a fresh list of prohibited tools. If a model can produce an argument that passes a proof checker, an assessment cannot infer student understanding from correctness alone; it needs visible problem selection, explanation, transfer and oral defence. Astra raises the ceiling, but it also makes the evidence problem wonderfully less deniable.

- **England’s education department lost 607,000 contact records through the portals people use to ask it for help.** The Department for Education confirmed that attackers accessed records from its Help Desk Self-Service Portal and the Turing Scheme Portal, including names, job titles, phone numbers and work email addresses belonging to senior school leaders, university staff and officials. The department says 607,000 is a record count rather than a count of unique people, that different data sets cannot be joined, and that no other data was accessed; it has referred the incident to the ICO and is working with the NCSC and National Crime Agency while the portals are repaired ([ITPro, 30 July 2026](https://www.itpro.com/security/data-breaches/head-teacher-data-accessed-in-department-for-education-cyber-attack)). This is not a student-record breach, but it is an excellent spear-phishing kit: role, institution and direct contact route bundled together. For school leaders, the lesson is that a convincing help-desk message should now be treated as an identity claim to verify out of band, especially when it asks for an account reset, MFA change or urgent document review. The boring support portal remains one of the most useful doors in the building.

- **Ninety-eight teenagers wrote a more operational school-AI policy than many adults have managed.** Students representing all 50 US states met at the Edward M. Kennedy Institute in Boston and passed their simulated Students First Act by 82–16. Their bill would start AI literacy when classroom devices are introduced, ban AI on graded tests, permit oral defences and handwritten checks, require students to disclose permitted use, and prevent an AI-detector score from becoming a verdict without review by two school officials. It also requires teachers to publish a clear usage policy each semester and personally investigate suspected misuse; when a school chatbot detects a request for emotional support, the agreed route is to alert a human rather than provide the support itself ([NPR Illinois, 30 July 2026](https://www.nprillinois.org/2026-07-30/adults-have-struggled-to-set-rules-for-ai-in-school-these-teens-figured-it-out)). The bill is not law, but AASA plans to send it to 10,000 school leaders. Its sharpest contribution is procedural: permission, disclosure, evidence and appeal are separate questions. Student voice here did not produce a permissive slogan; it produced due process.

- **The White House has completed a frontier-model review framework that the public will not be allowed to review.** AI companies examined the voluntary scheme on 4 August. Axios reports that it covers closed-source, state-of-the-art models presenting national-security risk, excludes open models, creates a 30-day pre-release government review period, restricts employee access during that window and keeps the cyber-capability benchmarks classified. Companies were encouraged to submit systems close to release rather than early prototypes ([Axios, 4 August 2026](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/04/inside-trump-ai-framework)). There is a defensible security reason not to publish a hacking test verbatim; there is no automatic reason the scope, thresholds and accountability process must also disappear. For schools buying downstream products, government review will therefore be a vendor assurance rather than inspectable evidence. It may reduce catastrophic cyber risk while telling us almost nothing about age suitability, data retention, bias, instructional quality or whether a classroom agent follows the wrong instruction with great competence. A classified safety gate is still a narrow gate.

- **Locking up phones appears to reduce phone use without automatically improving attainment.** A study circulated by the National Bureau of Economic Research, brought back into the live policy debate on 2 August, finds substantially lower phone use in schools with restrictions and signs that wellbeing improves the longer a policy remains in place. Average effects on test scores stay close to zero, while the researchers find little evidence of changes in attendance, classroom attention or perceived online bullying. Roughly two-thirds of US states now have legislation limiting school phone access, and nearly 5,000 schools use lockable pouches ([Axios, 2 August 2026](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/02/school-cellphone-ban-study-scores-attendance)). That is not a case against a clear school phone boundary; removing an ambient distraction can improve the conditions for teaching without producing learning by itself. The dry bit is that a pouch is excellent at storing a phone and disappointingly poor at planning a lesson. Define the outcome before calling the policy successful.

## On the radar

- **Google Pics is due to enter supported Workspace education accounts from 18 August.** Google announced the date on 17 July: the Nano Banana-powered image editor will run as a standalone app and inside Docs, Slides and Sheets, generating and editing images, text and individual elements ([TechRadar, 17 July 2026](https://www.techradar.com/pro/google-takes-aim-at-canva-and-adobe-as-its-ai-image-editor-pics-gets-an-official-rollout-date)). The current watch is not creative potential but the education rollout sheet: admin control, student age bands, provenance marks and whether generated assets inherit Drive sharing and retention rules. The integration points make it infrastructure on day one, not a harmless optional website.

- **Stanford’s educator AI-literacy course began on 3 August with stance before tooling.** Victor Lee’s Professional Learning Extension frames the work around understanding AI, developing a principled position and connecting decisions to teaching and learning goals; full participation earns one continuing-education unit ([Stanford Graduate School of Education, 3 August 2026](https://ed.stanford.edu/events/ai-literacy-educators)). That ordering is worth watching because tool fluency ages quickly while a defensible decision rule travels. The test is whether a principled stance becomes decisions about age, data, disclosure and evidence rather than simply a more elegant personal philosophy.

- **The ICITL 2026 proceedings landed as the conference opened in Ljubljana on 4–6 August.** The programme includes work on the ELIZA effect when students confide in AI, an agentic teaching prototype and a comparison of expert and novice design-thinking with NotebookLM ([Springer, 20 July 2026](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-032-32112-1)). Three fashionable topics, but unusually useful ones: relational boundaries, agent oversight and whether the same tool changes cognition differently with expertise. Conference papers are not validated classroom practice and proceedings bundles vary in quality; the shift from whether to use AI toward what it does to relationships and expertise is the signal.

## Sceptic's corner

The claim to pressure-test is Alpha School’s neatest one: two hours of AI-supported academics creates both faster learning and more human time. It may. Adaptive practice, mastery pacing and immediate feedback are all credible mechanisms, and the timetable at least says where the recovered hours go. But the present public evidence cannot tell us which part of the bundle does the work.

Alpha’s expansion story combines a high-income family cohort, tuition of $45,000–$75,000, guides paid at least $100,000, small private-school conditions, proprietary software and intensive afternoon programmes. The independent literature is not yet an outcomes base: a February case study describes itself as document-based and draws exclusively on publicly available materials rather than student-level evaluation ([TechRxiv, 27 February 2026](https://doi.org/10.36227/techrxiv.177220375.51273405/v1)). Fifty campuses will multiply anecdotes long before they produce a clean comparison.

The usable judgment is deliberately narrower than the sales pitch: Alpha is a serious operating experiment and worth watching, but not yet a transferable evidence base. Borrow the questions it exposes—mastery, transfer, human contact, time allocation—and do not borrow the answer until independent data separates selection, software and staffing.
