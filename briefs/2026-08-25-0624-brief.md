## Top story

**Gemini’s generated simulations have crossed into managed Workspace.** Google’s consumer announcement on 9 April introduced interactive 3D models, charts and simulations in the Gemini app, but explicitly excluded Education and Workspace accounts. The material change arrived on 24 August: Google now says the feature is available immediately to all Workspace customers and users who meet its minimum-age requirements, with usage limits, and is switched on by default wherever the Gemini app is enabled. Administrators retain the existing organisation-level Generative AI control; end users get no separate switch ([Google Workspace Updates, 24 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/generate-interactive-simulations-and-models-in-the-Gemini-app.html); [Google, original consumer announcement, 9 April 2026](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/gemini-app/3d-models-charts/)).

The classroom attraction is obvious. A learner can rotate a molecule, alter velocity and gravity in an orbital model, or watch energy move through a pendulum without a teacher first building the simulation. For international schools built around Workspace, that shortens the path from a question to something inspectable. It also changes the guidance teachers need. The output is a model Gemini has generated for the prompt, not a vetted simulation from a curriculum repository. Google’s two announcements demonstrate interface capability; neither supplies an accuracy study, an account of the underlying numerical methods or evidence that interacting with the result improves learning.

The useful classroom pattern is therefore not simply generate and explore. Ask students to state which relationships the model should preserve before they run it, change one variable at a time, compare the result with a trusted representation, and identify where the generated model breaks. A polished visual can make a wrong mechanism feel more authoritative than wrong prose. The feature earns a place when it turns an invisible misconception into something students can test, not when animation itself is counted as understanding.

## Other stories

- **Chicago has replaced a district-wide student chatbot rollout with school-by-school discretion.** Axios reported on 24 August that Chicago Public Schools had dropped plans to provide Gemini chatbots through Google Classroom to high-school students this year. District officials say the decision was made in May after AI committees raised readiness questions and three schools tested Gemini through an early-adopters programme; officials may still activate it for individual schools under a future-readiness model tailored to local needs. Parent campaigners are continuing to ask what student data the district shares with Google ([Axios Chicago, 24 August 2026](https://www.axios.com/local/chicago/2026/08/24/chicago-public-schools-scrap-google-gemini-roll-out-in-2026)). This is a genuine new turn after the proposed Chicago moratorium: the district has not accepted a three-year ban, but it has abandoned one-size-fits-all entitlement. The architecture lesson travels. Domain-wide availability is technically tidy and instructionally blunt; a bounded school pilot can name the intended tasks, teacher support, evidence and exit criteria before access becomes a default nobody remembers choosing.

- **AI grading matched a Physics Olympiad team, while still struggling with the marks that require judgement.** A preprint submitted on 20 August tested GPT-5.5-based grading on 10,364 scanned pages from 520 handwritten submissions by 416 candidates across a national Physics Olympiad theory exam, a final selection camp and a university quantum-mechanics exam. Each script was graded twice against official rubrics without access to the human marks. Total-score correlations were 0.91–0.97, and the system selected the same five-person Olympiad team as the official process. A second pass using more explicit page-by-page and evidence-location instructions improved agreement, but exact partial credit remained the principal weakness, especially in experimental work ([Pathak and colleagues, arXiv, submitted 20 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20521)). That is impressive audit evidence, not a case for autonomous marking: correlation can preserve rankings while concealing consequential item-level disagreements. For school assessment, the strongest use is a second reader that points an examiner to disputed evidence under a detailed rubric, with the human still responsible for the mark and the appeal.

- **Google can now show administrators who is using Gemini in Chat, but not whether the use helped.** From 20 August, the Gemini reports dashboard includes Google Chat activity at organisation and individual-user level, including active users, adoption trends and engagement with summarisation and generation. Google also changed some user-initiated summarisation interactions to count as active, so trend lines may move because the metric changed rather than because behaviour did. The reporting is available now on Business and Enterprise editions and the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, not as a universal Education entitlement ([Google Workspace Updates, 20 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/view-google-chat-usage-metrics-in-Gemini-reports-dashboard.html)). This is useful governance plumbing for international schools only if the dashboard is read narrowly. It can reveal rollout reach and support needs; it cannot identify who benefits most, despite Google’s product framing, without a separate outcome measure. Individual activity should not become staff-performance evidence, and any baseline needs the metric-definition change written beside it.

- **A 23-educator study found that better AI video came from building refusal into the workflow.** Researchers presented a video-authoring pipeline with two gates: educators repeatedly reshape generated scripts using multimedia-learning principles, while automated measures flag problems in instructional coherence and synchronisation between narration and visuals. Across three topics with 23 educators, plus automated testing across seven science and philosophy topics, both gates improved the same instructional dimensions. The paper is four pages, appeared through a CHI workshop and does not establish student learning gains, so its claims should stay modest ([Kim, Baek and Kwak, arXiv, submitted 20 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19812)). The practical contribution is the workflow shape. Teacher guidance for generated resources usually teaches better prompting and then asks for a final check. This reverses the emphasis: define conditions that force a stop before polish outruns pedagogy. A usable school template would separate factual accuracy, cognitive load, visual-narrative alignment, accessibility and transfer evidence rather than offering one reassuring approve button.

- **A local learning-analytics model explains a privacy advantage and an accuracy trap at the same time.** A 21 August preprint distils a black-box estimator and its post-hoc interpretation into a two-billion-parameter open-weight language model that produces an individual estimate and a natural-language explanation offline on a commodity laptop. Under an oracle signal it recovered the effect surface at above 0.90 correlation and introduced no spurious covariates; on a national dataset, 98.8% of narrations passed the authors’ audit with no fabricated quantities. Yet when the upstream estimator was realistic, nearly all remaining error came from that source, narration quality was independent of signal quality, and decisions collapsed towards the majority action under severe imbalance ([Pan, Meng and Suk, arXiv, submitted 21 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21165)). Keeping student records on-device is a meaningful deployment property. It does not make the prediction fair or correct. Any school analytics pilot needs an audit of the upstream model, subgroup error and the action attached to a prediction, not merely a fluent explanation beside it.

- **New Zealand’s current NCEA support page keeps authenticity, workload and assessment format in the same frame.** Published on 25 August, the Ministry page extends the co-requisite transition through 2027, promises extra administration funding and restates that submitted reports were discontinued as a Level 1 external-assessment method from 2025 because of workload and authenticity concerns, including rapidly advancing AI ([New Zealand Ministry of Education, 25 August 2026](https://ncea.education.govt.nz/whats-new/support-ncea-level-1-and-co-requisite-implementation)). The report decision is older than the page and should not be presented as new. What is current is the implementation bundle: assessment integrity is being handled alongside readiness, resources and transition time rather than as a detector purchase.

## Sceptic's corner

The tempting verdict on Gemini’s simulations is that generative AI has finally escaped the chat box and become a learning environment. A student can manipulate an orbit instead of reading about one; surely that is active learning. It may be.

Interactivity is a property of the artefact, not of the thinking around it. A learner can drag a gravity slider with no prediction, explanation or transfer, just as easily as they can scroll through prose. Worse, a generated simulation can hide its assumptions more effectively than a generated paragraph. The equations, approximations and boundary conditions sit behind movement that looks physically coherent. Google’s current release says the elements are made for the user’s question and are on by default where Gemini is enabled; it does not say how teachers can inspect the model, version it, cite it or distinguish a numerical simulation from a persuasive animation ([Google Workspace Updates, 24 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/generate-interactive-simulations-and-models-in-the-Gemini-app.html)).

The grading study supplies the better standard. Its authors did not accept a convincing total score; they looked at question-part disagreement, changed the evidence-location instructions and retained examiner control where partial credit stayed difficult ([Pathak and colleagues, 20 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20521)). Apply the same discipline to generated models. Ask what claim the simulation makes, where the evidence sits, which condition would falsify it, and whether the student can explain the relationship once the animation is gone. The staffroom line: moving parts are not shown working. The learning begins when the student can say why they moved.
