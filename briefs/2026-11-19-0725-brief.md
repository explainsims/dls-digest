## Top story

**A large phone-pouch study found a small learning gain.** Harvard's Center for Education Policy Research [announced the findings on 17 November](https://cepr.harvard.edu/cell-phone-press-release). The researchers followed about 3.3 million U.S. students at roughly 4,600 schools between 2021–22 and 2025–26, using fall-to-spring NWEA MAP Growth scores and comparing schools that introduced Yondr pouches with similar schools. The main estimate, pooling maths and reading, was 0.0087 standard deviations of extra annual growth, or about 0.01. The authors translate the magnitude into the equivalent of one to two weeks of instruction in a year. They calculated that comparison from typical test-score growth. The study measured test scores and pouch adoption, so the weeks figure tells readers the scale of the effect, in familiar units. [Research report, including Table 2](https://cepr.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum9881/files/2026-11/Phone-Free%20Schools%20and%20Achievement%20Growth_1.pdf).

The study's stronger comparison is at school level. The researchers looked for differences between adopting and comparison schools before the rules changed and found no clear difference in their earlier growth trends. Schools still chose whether to adopt. The estimate is less precise when looking at either subject on its own, and the authors had relatively few schools with a long history of using pouches. Their separate analysis of statewide restrictions produced mixed results and the groups were already on different trajectories, which makes those estimates harder to interpret.

For international schools, the distinction worth carrying into a phone discussion is between the written rule and what happens between classes. A school can have a strict policy on paper and still leave teachers negotiating it six times a day. A school with an effective routine might already have much of the benefit available locally. Start with the outcome the school has reason to care about, such as interruptions during reading or the time lost getting a lesson underway. Then ask what a pouch would change about that routine and who would be doing the extra work.

## Other stories

- **Gemini Notebook turns source material into interactive sites.** Google's [18 November Workspace update](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/11/transform-your-notes-and-research-into-dynamic-tools-with-Gemini-Notebook.html) says the web rollout started on 16 November, can take up to 15 days across both Workspace release tracks and is limited to users aged 18 and older. Administrators can control access to the Notebook service, with no separate control for generating sites. The [sharing instructions](https://support.google.com/gemininotebook/answer/18945748?hl=en-5) give the public-link route to consumer-account owners and editors, and say link viewers need access to the full notebook. Google documents a separate download route. On shared sites, each user can see their own inputs, including when the user is the owner, so a checklist completed by several people would yield separate views. A teacher could generate an explorable history timeline from public sources and check whether the dates, labels and controls survive the move from notebook to site. For Ed Tech Coaches, seeing it work in the author's browser answers only the first question. Before promising a class resource, check who can actually open it and what else that access would give them.

- **Scotland gives teachers time to work through its draft curriculum.** In a [17 November letter to the Scottish Parliament](https://www.parliament.scot/-/media/files/committees/session-7/education-and-gaelic-committee/correspondence/2026/cabsececgtoconvener_17nov26.pdf), Education Secretary Màiri McAllan said two extra in-service days had been agreed for 2026–27 to support the curriculum revision. Local authorities must seek an exemption to arrange the closures, and the letter acknowledges the cost to families. A sample of practitioners is testing draft materials, with an advanced draft scheduled for January 2027, final materials in spring and implementation in August 2028. That sequence gives teachers something concrete to do with the time. Put actual lessons and pieces of pupil work alongside the draft and see where the proposed sequence diverges from what a department currently teaches and assesses. An international school changing its curriculum or assessment reporting can borrow the sequence even if it cannot borrow the two days. It is far cheaper to hear that three teams interpret a standard differently while the document is open for revision than after families have seen three incompatible reports.

- **A science museum puts careers, families and AI in the same programme.** The New York Hall of Science's [17 November announcement](https://nysci.org/press-releases/new-york-hall-of-science-launches-exploring-ai-together-connecting-young-people-families-and-communities-to-the-future-of-artificial-intelligence) scheduled a 50-student, three-hour mentored hackathon with Playlab for the 18 November public launch of Exploring AI Together. An evening programme was designed for 14- to 24-year-olds and their educators and caregivers. The museum's family advisory committee was involved before the launch, with an October session about how AI affects decisions in fields such as housing and transport. Its research team plans to evaluate the initiative. The useful design choice is that adult participants get questions of their own. In a school careers event, pupils might work out what information a system would need to suggest an opportunity, while a parent or visiting professional asks how it would recognise missing evidence or handle an exception. Both can compare the suggestion with a real listing or policy. A successful session would leave the family with a way to interrogate the next recommendation they encounter, even after the prototype is forgotten.

- **America's national assessment makes the hardware contingency explicit.** A [17 November U.S. Department of Education notice](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-11-17/pdf/2026-23391.pdf) seeks comment on the proposed data collection for the 2028 National Assessment of Educational Progress through 19 January 2027. The plan includes multistage testing studies in reading and maths at grades 4 and 8. It envisages testing on school devices, with Chromebooks provided to public or private schools whose devices fall below the minimum specification. The assessment reports results for populations and groups, with no individual student or school scores. For schools planning their own device-based assessment, the fallback is the instructive part. A backup device that merely opens the test can still alter a pupil's experience if the keyboard, text size or navigation differs. Rehearsing an ordinary device change before test day makes the handoff visible. Try a sample task all the way through on the spare with the accommodations that a pupil normally uses, then check what happens to work already entered. The aim is for a hardware problem to stay a hardware problem.

- **An Erasmus+ funding call asks school partnerships to show what travels.** The European Commission's [18 November call announcement](https://erasmus-plus.ec.europa.eu/whats-new/news/over-eur53-billion-for-wider-opportunities-under-erasmus-2027-call) gives a 2027 budget of over €5.3 billion across education, training, youth and sport. The budget also supports the new package's priorities on basic skills, teachers and digital tools including AI. The [school development partnerships scheme](https://erasmus-plus.ec.europa.eu/programme-guide/part-b/key-action-2/partnerships-for-school-development) is one specific part, with €400,000 per selected 36-month project. Its criteria ask for a baseline and measurable learning targets in a basic skill, plus plans for other schools to use successful approaches. A school outside the programme can still take something from that spec. In a cross-school reading project, for example, look for a response both partners can assess and agree what counts as improvement. Then have the receiving school try the materials without a visiting specialist to explain them. That handover says more about whether a practice can travel than a polished account written by the team that invented it.

## On the radar

- **An inclusion pilot will make some forms of disclosure optional.** In a [16 November preview](https://ww2.montgomeryschoolsmd.org/press/index.aspx?id=13605&pagetype=showrelease), Montgomery County Public Schools in Maryland scheduled a 19 November unveiling for a voluntary initiative supporting students with disabilities that may be hard for others to see. Selected groups are due to start in early 2027, with educational resources, self-advocacy opportunities and optional identification tools. The details to watch are where the identification would be used, who would see it and how a student who chooses to keep a disability private can get the same support. A good test of the eventual design is what happens when a student asks for help without using the tool.

- **A school partnership needs a task that works across timetables.** The British Council's [19 November teacher session](https://www.britishcouncil.org/school-resources/international-education-week/bring-world-your-classroom), with Leeds City of Languages, puts collaborative school projects on the International Education Week programme. The promising signal would be a worked example in which pupils respond to one another's ideas and teachers can reuse the sequence. A live call can be memorable, but pupils at either end also need time to prepare and reply, especially across time zones or languages. A question recorded by one class, answered with local evidence by another, and followed up after both have had time to listen is a small enough exchange to repeat.

## Sceptic's corner

The Harvard release's historical claim deserves more scrutiny than its estimate of pouch effects. It suggests that phones in school may explain about half the annual slowdown in U.S. achievement growth after 2013. The arithmetic compares the size of a recent estimate for schools adopting lockable pouches with the size of a longer national decline. That comparison makes a small number intelligible. Establishing how much of a national trend phones caused would take evidence about that earlier period, including how phone use changed and what else changed alongside it. [Harvard release](https://cepr.harvard.edu/cell-phone-press-release).

The [earlier U.S. pouch study](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35132/w35132.pdf), revised in September, found average test score effects close to zero, with modest positive estimates for high school, especially in maths. The [new report's discussion](https://cepr.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum9881/files/2026-11/Phone-Free%20Schools%20and%20Achievement%20Growth_1.pdf) says the uncertainty intervals overlap and points out that one study measured score levels while the other measured within-year growth. Both studies leave room for small academic benefits. The precision suggested by an annual promise of recovered weeks goes further than either can deliver for a particular school.

A school that wants fewer phone interruptions already has an outcome it can inspect. It can ask whether teachers spend less time intervening, whether students settle into sustained work and whether pupils with approved access can use it without being singled out. Those questions can return different answers, and each has a different remedy. Protect the quiet needed for a lesson and judge the policy first on whether it makes that quiet easier to sustain.
