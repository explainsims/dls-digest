## Top story

**The evidence grew; the verdict stayed conditional.** Stanford's SCALE Initiative [published its updated AI-in-education review on 10 November](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/evidence-base-review/evidence-base-ai-k12-november-review), expanding its causal evidence base from 20 papers to 52. The 32 additions comprise 28 examining student-facing tools and four examining educator-facing tools. Five new student-facing studies took place in U.S. K–12 settings, where the earlier review had none. These are newly reviewed findings, mainly from papers published between October 2025 and June 2026, rather than 32 experiments conducted this week.

The distinction that survives the larger sample is between using AI to support learning and using it to produce the work. The former looks more promising, with mixed results; the latter can weaken later independent performance. Pleasant learning experiences also frequently fail to translate into stronger learning outcomes. The researchers still cannot establish whether gains persist over longer periods. Their repository relies heavily on preprints, and causal findings in one setting do not automatically travel to another.

For international secondary schools, that makes the most useful evaluation question surprisingly small: what can the student do when the assistance comes away? Imagine two classes using the same assistant to prepare explanations of an unfamiliar graph. Both submit cleaner explanations. A week later, give each student a different graph and ask them to defend an interpretation without the assistant. The second task tests the claim that matters to a teacher; the polished first product tests something else.

That need not become a miniature university trial every time someone tries a tool. A department can name one intended learning gain, retain a modest comparison with its usual practice and arrange a delayed check before expanding. The report supplies a reason to make those choices explicit. Fifty-two papers are a stronger starting point than twenty. They still do not make the product category the intervention: the task, guidance and conditions of use remain part of what is being tested.

## Other stories

- **OpenAI's new education report puts a number on the guidance gap.** In its [10 November account of EMEA partnerships](https://openai.com/index/closing-ai-guidance-gap-education/), OpenAI cites ThinkYoung findings that 79% of surveyed students used AI for homework or assignments, while 47% said they had been shown how. [ThinkYoung's research page](https://www.thinkyoung.eu/aieducation) describes surveys of 5,000 students aged 13–19 and 600 teachers, plus student and parent focus groups. These are reported experiences, and the percentages should not be treated as an estimate for students in another school or as evidence of learning gains. The practical question is what a student thinks instruction permits after attending the introduction. Show a fictional homework task, an acceptable request for help and a request that hands over the thinking. Ask the pupil to explain the difference and where to seek clarification. A school can have a polished presentation about responsible AI and still leave that choice obscure. Training attendance tells us who was in the room; a worked example tells us whether the boundary travelled home.

- **Claude is becoming a place where the shared working file stays.** Anthropic's [10 November launch](https://claude.com/resources/articles/sheets-whiteboards-data) introduces Whiteboards and Sheets in beta on paid plans and makes the former Dashboards generally available as Claude Data. Sheets can create tabs, formulas and dropdowns, with export to Excel or CSV. Data adds certified queries and live sharing; viewers can see dashboard results without having the connectors behind them. On Team plans all three are enabled by default. On Enterprise, Whiteboards and Sheets start off, while Data switches on by default on 19 November unless administrators change the setting. For a school evaluating the platform, the interesting test is a handover. Build a harmless departmental tracker, let a second teacher edit it and export it, then check what remains legible: formulas, changes, ownership and the definition behind any displayed number. Generating a spreadsheet is a useful convenience. Leaving the next colleague a file they can understand is the working standard, especially when the person who prompted it is absent.

- **A maths programme teaches AI by making the model visible.** A [10 November Concord Consortium profile of researcher Jie Chao](https://concord.org/blog/ai-in-education-teaching-students-what-it-means-to-be-human/) describes a six-hour supplement for Algebra I or Integrated Math 1. Students use algebra and statistics to create, test and refine mathematical models of language. Its spring 2025 pilot involved more than 80 Florida Virtual School students in Grades 6–11. A separate 2025–26 randomised pilot with 80 Algebra I students reported greater gains in AI self-efficacy for the treatment group. That is confidence in understanding and creating AI, rather than a demonstrated increase in mathematics achievement. The organisation is describing its own programme, so the distinction belongs beside the result. For secondary teachers, the curricular idea is stronger than another session on prompt tips: let pupils see what a model does with an example, alter the example and explain the change. A student who can identify which features shifted a prediction has something more durable than familiarity with a particular chatbot's menu. The profile also points to six free modules connecting AI with subjects including English and history.

- **Google's reusable skills arrive as copies, with a maintenance consequence.** Its [9 November Workspace release](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/11/skills-in-workspace.html) starts the rollout of custom instructions across Studio, Gmail, Docs, Slides, Drive and Chat. The education eligibility listed is the Google AI Pro for Education add-on, for users aged 18 and over; it is not a blanket student rollout. Rapid Release starts on 9 November, Scheduled Release on 30 November, each taking up to 15 days. Sharing from Studio gives recipients independent copies, so later edits to the original do not update copies they saved. Referenced Drive files need their own permissions. English prompts and instructions are supported; Sheets and Meet are outside the initial surfaces. For an international school, a departmental resource-formatting skill could therefore be useful and quietly diverge into six versions. Put the owner, revision date and source policy in the instructions themselves, then give colleagues a way to recognise a retired copy. Sharing a rule is easy. Knowing which version produced a resource three months later is the more interesting administrative problem.

- **Workspace automation can wait, which makes old assumptions worth checking.** Google's [9 November Studio update](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/11/pause-and-schedule-automated-workflows-with-the-new-Wait-until-step-in-Workspace-Studio.html) adds a pause inside a flow, including branches and loops, for a specified date or duration up to 14 days. The Activity view records the intended resume time and time zone. Rollout was scheduled over one to three days for both release tracks, including Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus. There is no separate admin control for the step. This is useful plumbing for a reminder sequence that previously needed separate schedules. It also creates an obvious school test: what happens if the request is resolved while the automation sleeps? In a synthetic example, mark a form complete after the pause starts and see whether the resumed sequence checks current status before sending its reminder. A delay preserves the flow's place in time. It does not, by itself, establish that the reason for the next action still exists. The most helpful reminder is sometimes the one that gets cancelled.

## On the radar

- **A Google integration deadline arrives today.** An [official developer notice dated 12 October](https://developers.google.com/workspace/release-notes?hl=en) says Workspace MCP servers start rejecting requests on 11 November from OAuth clients that have not been marked for an AI-powered agent. Clients left unmarked by 12 November will require replacement to use MCP. This concerns agent integrations, including the listed Gmail, Calendar, Chat, Docs and Drive servers; ordinary REST-only applications are unaffected. The current deadline, rather than the original announcement, earns the slot. A supplier using MCP should be able to say which client changed and show a successful authorised call after enforcement, rather than merely confirm that someone saw the notice.

- **California's summit supplies a useful accountability question.** The [education department's 9 November release](https://www.cde.ca.gov/nr/ne/yr26/yr26rel46.asp) describes an AI summit spanning literacy, mathematics, workforce preparation, safety and privacy, with educators, students and district leaders involved. It calls for evidence of improved student outcomes and keeping educators central to instructional decisions. Those are commitments in an event statement, rather than results from an intervention. Watch for the implementation detail that would make them inspectable: who decides an experiment is unsuccessful, what evidence they use and whether the district can stop without losing the underlying service. An ambitious summit becomes useful to another school when those choices acquire names, dates and examples.

## Sceptic's corner

Satisfaction is an especially slippery scoreboard because both sides can make it mean what they need. If students like an AI tutor, advocates can call that engagement. If they dislike it, advocates can call that productive struggle. The argument then becomes impossible to lose and rather difficult to evaluate.

There is a useful uncomfortable detail in [OpenAI's new EMEA report, in its Estonia discussion](https://assets.ctfassets.net/kftzwdyauwt9/3w97q66WFq2swJk1WvfN5C/a9bc88b2b5fbdd7e4abf029585c6ef2f/responsible-ai-in-education.pdf). It reports a student Net Promoter Score of −27.5 and satisfaction of 3.07 out of five for the ITI tutor, with 182 respondents. Feedback included excessive questioning, slow exchanges and requests for clearer explanations. OpenAI explicitly separates those experiences from proof of whether learning improved. Publishing an awkward result is useful; treating the awkwardness itself as proof that the pedagogy worked would undo the benefit.

A student who wants the answer may resent a good question. A student who already understands the first step may also resent a bad sequence of six unnecessary ones. The teacher needs enough evidence to tell those situations apart. Inspect a few exchanges against the task's learning goal, ask students where the conversation stalled and check what they can subsequently explain independently. Keep the usability finding and the learning finding side by side.

The question is not whether a tutor can sustain dialogue. It is whether this dialogue earns the time it takes from this learner. Endless questions can be a teaching method. They can also be a loading screen with manners.
