## Top story

**Google has been shopping chatbot-safety bill language to US state legislators, and the carve-outs in it fit Gemini.** An NPR investigation published on 18 September found chatbot bills with similar or verbatim language introduced in at least 10 states this year, with lawmakers, lawyers and advocates describing a lobbying effort led by Google to write its own model bill ([NPR, 18 September 2026](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/18/nx-s1-5968878/ai-chatbots-safety-regulation-google)). Hawaii state Rep. Trish La Chica said "The framework had been provided by Google early on during the beginning of the session," and that its definition exempted chatbots embedded within another application or website — which she read as covering Gemini. She declined to sponsor it, and said the bill was vetted to remove those exemptions before it became Act 248. Colorado's Chatbot Safety Act, signed in May, carves out 12 categories, including chatbots housed within search engines; Marjorie Connolly of the Tech Oversight Project summed it up: "Parents are being asked to accept a chatbot law that exempts chatbots." Where Colorado does impose duties, companies must "institute technically feasible measures" for known minors — and, as attorney Laura Marquez-Garrett put it, "it's up to the companies to say what's technically feasible." Google told NPR it "is committed to supporting thoughtful, effective AI legislation that protects consumers while fostering innovation."

None of this binds an international school outside the United States. It matters because vendors build one global product and set child-safety defaults to the regulatory floor, and NPR has just shown who is pouring that floor. The "feature within another software application" exemption, found in bills in at least eight states, describes exactly how Gemini reaches students in Google for Education schools: inside Docs, Gmail and Drive, not as a standalone chatbot. The protection such a school actually holds is its Workspace for Education agreement and its admin-console settings, not a statute. The line to keep ready: when a vendor says a product complies with child-safety law, ask which law — and which exemption it sits in.

## Other stories

- **A two-year randomised trial of Khanmigo found students mostly ignored the tutor, and it added nothing beyond Khan Academy itself.** Jill Barshay's Proof Points column on 21 September surfaced an August NBER working paper by Philip Oreopoulos and Nina Low of the University of Toronto, which randomised low-achieving students in 18 Tennessee middle schools over two years, 2024 to 2026 ([Hechinger Report, 21 September 2026](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-khanmigo-math-ai-tutor/)). Assignment raised maths achievement by 1.3 national percentile ranks per term, gains that "resemble those from Khan Academy practice without AI assistance." The mechanism is the finding: 96 percent of students tried Khanmigo, but the median student messaged it on only a third of practice days, and in only 17 percent of sessions in which they had made a mistake ([NBER, August 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35620)). The authors' verdict: "We observe how students actually used the AI tutor, and the answer is: not much." Sal Khan's response is a redesign — Khanmigo now pops up automatically after a wrong answer, and he plans to give students "credit" for bot-assisted redos. The lesson for any school rolling out Gemini's guided-learning or study-notebook features: access is not the intervention. The intervention is whatever makes a stuck student actually ask, and a pilot that reports seats provisioned rather than help-seeking after errors is measuring the wrong thing.

- **Stanford used generative AI to swap a Hispanic male student out of a promotional photo for a generated Black woman, in breach of its own policy.** The Stanford Daily reported on 23 September that Residential and Dining Enterprises altered a 2024 Lunar New Year dinner photo for a housing banner — changing students' clothing into Stanford merchandise, slimming two students' faces and replacing student Billy Ramirez entirely with a fictitious Black woman ([San Francisco Standard, 22 September 2026](https://sfstandard.com/2026/09/22/stanford-student-ai-photo-replaces-latino-man-black-woman/); [Stanford Daily, 23 September 2026](https://stanforddaily.com/2026/09/23/university-confirms-ai-edited-photo/)). Public relations director Charlene Gage said "Stanford's AI policy is clear: The use of AI in producing or altering images of Stanford people, events, research, facilities, or achievements is strictly prohibited," adding that "Both the alteration and lack of disclosure in this case violate that policy" ([Inside Higher Ed, 23 September 2026](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/09/23/ai-edited-stanford-ad-violated-campus-policy-official-says)). Ramirez said being edited out left him feeling "silenced and erased from a representation that was supposed to include me." The instructive detail is that Stanford had exactly the right rule and it still happened, in a dining-services office nobody would list as a communications risk. Image editing is now a built-in feature of the tools every school office already uses; for a school whose website, yearbook and admissions material are made of children's faces, that rule needs an owner, a review step and a consent line.

- **OpenAI halved its mid-tier prices with GPT-6 Sol and Luna, and says the cuts are permanent.** Released on 22 September, GPT-6 Sol costs $2/$10 per million input/output tokens against GPT-5.6 Sol's $4/$20, and Luna $0.10/$0.50; an OpenAI spokesperson confirmed to VentureBeat that the rates are permanent rather than promotional ([VentureBeat, 22 September 2026](https://venturebeat.com/technology/openai-releases-gpt-6-sol-and-luna-models-slashing-api-costs-50-or-more)). OpenAI also published unusually specific behaviour numbers: on an internal coding-deception test built to elicit dishonesty, Sol's rate fell to 1.3% from 10.4%. The ChatGPT rollout names Plus, Pro, Business and Enterprise; no Edu tier is mentioned. For a Google school, the more consequential line sits in the same piece: Gemini 3.8 Flash's $0.75/$3.75 rate is introductory "through Dec. 31, before increasing to $1.50/$7.50 on Jan. 1, 2027." For schools whose home-built apps run on Flash-class models, any move to 3.8 Flash should be budgeted at the January price, not today's, while OpenAI locks in a halving. VentureBeat's caveat that labs get different results when benchmarks are run "under different configurations, effort settings and harnesses" is the reason to price a school's small apps per completed task on its own workloads, not per token on a vendor chart.

- **Two American voices weighed in on Australia's online-safety overhaul in the same week, pulling the same way.** On 18 September OpenAI published an Australian Youth Safety Blueprint — six pillars from AI literacy and age-appropriate safeguards to privacy-protective age assurance, crisis support and parental controls — pitched as a contribution to Australian policy ([OpenAI, 18 September 2026](https://openai.com/index/australian-youth-safety-blueprint/)). The full document engages the draft Online Safety Amendment (Digital Duty of Care) Bill 2026, says "In schools, for example, adoption should be teacher-led," and asks for "outcomes-focused obligations" rather than "prescribing fixed technical measures" ([OpenAI blueprint, September 2026](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/7e70543d-a803-44b7-8754-ca00b3f2ff0c/australian-youth-safety-blueprint.pdf)). On 22 September the US government filed its response to the consultation on that bill, objecting to "prescriptive product design mandates or expanded litigation risks that unduly burden American companies" ([US Embassy Canberra, 22 September 2026](https://au.usembassy.gov/u-s-government-response-to-the-australian-consultation-on-the-online-safety-amendment-digital-duty-of-care-bill-2026/)). Read beside the top story, the pattern is consistent: the industry is for safety principles and against anyone else specifying the mechanism. Australia has become the region's bellwether on children online, so where Canberra lands is likely to shape the teen defaults vendors ship across Asia. The teacher-led line is worth quoting — with the author named.

- **Provosts use AI every week; their institutions mostly have no plan for it.** Inside Higher Ed's annual survey of chief academic officers, released on 23 September with Hanover Research (376 provosts, 4.7-point margin of error), found roughly seven in 10 use AI in their own work at least weekly, but only one in 10 report a centralised AI strategy ([Inside Higher Ed, 23 September 2026](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/governance/executive-leadership/2026/09/23/ai-use-funding-cuts-how-provosts-navigate-2026)). Seventy-one percent use it to summarise documents; only 12 percent said institutional AI investment had produced department workflow optimisation, 8 percent reported "institutionwide operational transformation," and one in five agreed their institution has a coherent vision for how AI will change what and how it teaches. Paul LeBlanc, former president of Southern New Hampshire University, put the gap simply: "When you bolt AI onto existing workflows, there is limited impact." Note the survey was supported by Honorlock, a proctoring vendor, and Watermark. It is a higher-education sample, but the shape transfers to any school leadership team: personal productivity is where adoption stops by default. The distance between using AI to triage an inbox and knowing what it changes about teaching is precisely the gap Ed Tech Coaches exist to close, and it is a teaching-and-learning question, not a tools question.

## Sceptic's corner

This week's claim comes with a number built for a slide: AI tutors match expert human tutors at "918× lower cost." It is from StudentBench, a 23 September arXiv preprint whose data and code sit under Handshake AI Research: 2,383 participants, one hour of AI or human tutoring on GRE questions, and a pooled AI-minus-human difference inside pre-set equivalence bounds ([arXiv, 23 September 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28470)).

Test it. Participants were adults (median age 21) recruited through Handshake and paid $50 per session regardless of performance. Learning was measured once, immediately after the hour. The headline cost ratio belongs to one open-weight model, Gemma 4 31B, set against a $75-an-hour human reference. And the authors flag the largest hole themselves: with no condition in which students simply worked practice problems alone, the study "does not separately measure the added benefit of interacting with an AI tutor over doing practice problems alone."

Now set it beside Khanmigo. In Tennessee nobody paid students to engage, and mostly they didn't: the median student messaged the tutor on a third of practice days. StudentBench measured what a tutor can do for a paid, motivated adult who has already sat down for an hour. The Toronto study measured what happens when a middle-schooler who is behind in maths gets to choose. Those are different questions, and schools only ever get to ask the second.

The staffroom line: AI can now tutor a willing learner about as well as a person can; the unsolved problem is the unwilling one, and that is still the teacher's job.
