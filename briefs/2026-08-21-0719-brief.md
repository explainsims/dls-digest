## Top story

**Google has put a control plane around Workspace agents.** [New Workspace Studio safeguards](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/new-enterprise-security-controls-for-Workspace-Studio-enable-expanded-collaboration-use-cases.html), announced on 17 August and updated as end-user features began rolling out on 20 August, give administrators much more than a master on/off switch. Studio flows now run under least-privilege agent identities with unique audit identifiers; audit events can carry flow context; and a new access dashboard can suspend flows or revoke their OAuth scopes. Administrators can disable individual steps, block webhooks, prevent Gemini from processing particular data, and place human confirmation before a flow shares information externally. Data-loss-prevention rules can also inspect supported Studio actions.

That is the important move: an agent is becoming a governable identity with bounded permissions, observable actions and interruptible execution. For international schools using Google Workspace, this makes a tightly scoped operational or teaching-workflow pilot more defensible than an automation whose effective authority is hidden inside its creator's account. It also supplies a useful procurement test for every other agent platform: can the school identify the actor, limit its tools and data, require approval at the consequential step, and stop it centrally?

But the update contains a migration trap. Google says several controls initially apply only to newly created flows, with support for existing flows promised later. A dashboard may therefore look reassuring while older automations remain outside the newest boundary. Availability is also uneven: Studio reaches Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus, while some DLP protections depend on the edition and action. The prudent response is not broad enablement. Schools should first classify existing flows by creation date, owner, scopes, external writes and approval points; then pilot one low-consequence workflow created under the new regime. Controls make an experiment auditable. They do not make the experiment educationally worthwhile.

## Other stories

- **An exam score may mean something different when the examinee is a model.** A [16 August preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15630) compared the latent structure of human and model responses on high-school chemistry and university-entrance quantitative-reasoning assessments. Across six multimodal LLMs, exploratory factor analysis and resampling found systematic differences between the constructs inferred from model responses and those inferred from human responses. This is a narrow case study, not proof that every benchmark is invalid. It does, however, puncture a common shortcut: a model matching a student score does not establish that it possesses the same knowledge structure or reasoning competence. For school evaluation, task scores should be paired with error patterns, transfer tasks and evidence about how the answer was produced. A useful protocol would retain the original rubric while separately coding strategy, failure mode and performance after the problem is altered. Otherwise a familiar test scale can lend human meaning to a machine result that the instrument was never designed to support.

- **A lecture assistant can retrieve the teacher instead of impersonating one.** Researchers tested [LLMs that select relevant segments from instructor-recorded video](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16131), rather than generating an unrestricted answer. Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT-5.4 Pro and Qwen3.5-397B were compared with lecturer-selected timestamps and judged for relevance, sufficiency, redundancy and extraneous material; the proprietary models reached near-parity with the lecturer despite limited exact timestamp overlap. A pilot in a roughly 900-student introductory C-programming course drew strong use, especially for foundational concepts. The attractive architecture is retrieval with provenance: the learner sees the instructor's explanation in context, and the teacher retains the knowledge boundary. It also gives reviewers a visible evidence object—the selected clip—rather than a fluent answer whose support must be reconstructed. The evidence is still early. Engagement is not learning, an automated judge supplied much of the evaluation, and the paper reports no unaided outcome. A school pilot should therefore test whether students can solve a related task after the clip, not merely whether they clicked it.

- **Just-in-time coaching produced artefacts, but only preliminary learning evidence.** [LearnAI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19164) embedded brief AI-literacy exposure across 18 courses in five disciplines, then offered one-to-one support from trained undergraduate tutors. Its five stages—problem framing, tool-task mapping, iterative co-prompting, deployment and verification, then ethical reflection—helped 35 clients co-create 36 portfolio sites and more than 20 small applications over two semesters. That sequence is more useful for professional learning than a catalogue of tools because it begins with the work and keeps verification inside the workflow. The model's other strength is timing: support arrives while someone has an authentic task and a reason to judge the result. Yet the evaluation is modest: interviews covered five clients and two tutors, and the paired pre/post sample was seven. Treat this as a promising service design, not an efficacy claim. For an Ed Tech Coach experiment, preserve the staged script but collect a before-and-after artefact, a short independent transfer task and the time required from both coach and participant.

- **Slides can now become editable recordings through Vids.** From 20 August, Google began rolling out [presentation recording from Google Slides into Google Vids](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/record-presentations-in-google-slides-with-Google-Vids.html). The workflow records from the deck, then opens the result in Vids for edits such as transcript correction or generated voiceover before it is shared by link. Rapid Release domains may see it over as many as 15 days; Scheduled Release begins 7 September. The older recording route under Slides' View menu remains available. This removes some friction from teacher-created explanations and student presentations, particularly when a speaker needs to repair a segment without re-recording the entire piece. Because the output becomes a Vids project, the workflow also changes ownership, collaboration and sharing questions that a simple recording may not raise. It does not remove the instructional work: a polished recording can still be too long, inaccessible or cognitively overloaded. Test the handoff, captions, permissions and playback from a learner account before recommending it as a standard workflow.

- **Gemini notebooks can become shareable templates—with a fork problem.** Google's [17 August notebook-copy feature](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/make-copy-of-notebook-in-gemini-notebook.html) duplicates sources, generated Studio artefacts such as audio or video overviews, study guides, flashcards, quizzes and slide decks, plus prompts and custom chat configuration. Drive sources carry over only when the copier already has permission; personal chat history and notes do not. Copies do not stay synchronised with the original. The sharing-level **Allow copies** setting is on by default and has no administrator control. This could make a carefully bounded research notebook reusable across classes without exposing another person's conversation history. Distribution convenience therefore needs a maintenance convention: recipients must know whether they own a snapshot, a starting point or an endorsed resource. It also creates provenance and version drift: a copied notebook may look official long after its source, instructions or permissions have changed. Any template needs an owner, version date, update route and a clear statement of which sources the recipient can actually inspect.

## On the radar

- **Introductory programming assessments remain highly model-solvable.** A [17 August study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16318) ran five current systems—ChatGPT-5.2, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Microsoft 365 Copilot—against authentic object-oriented programming tests and exams. All exceeded the historical student average and often earned full marks on longer tasks, while still producing non-compiling code and struggling with interfaces, abstract classes, inheritance and graphics-heavy questions. Those failures may be more diagnostically useful than the headline scores because they expose where inspection and execution still matter. This is a redesign signal, not evidence that students no longer need OOP: assess explanation, debugging, live modification and unaided transfer alongside code output.

- **Gemini adoption is becoming visible in Admin reports.** Google's [20 August reporting update](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/view-google-chat-usage-metrics-in-Gemini-reports-dashboard.html) adds organisation- and user-level Gemini-in-Chat activity, including summarisation and generation, to the Gemini reports dashboard. Google also warns that some interactions have been reclassified as active use, so trend lines can move because the definition changed. For Education, availability is limited to Google AI Pro for Education. Pair any count with stated purpose, actual outcome and an explicit metric-definition note. Useful for licence and support questions; unsuitable as a proxy for learning, teaching quality or employee performance.

- **Chat governance now has a middle setting.** A [20 August Workspace update](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/granular-space-creation-restrictions-now-available-in-Google-Chat.html) lets administrators allow all creation, block new spaces while retaining direct and group messages, or block all new conversations. The policy can be targeted by domain, organisational unit or group and is available across Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus. That middle option matters: international schools can limit unmanaged collaborative spaces without disabling ordinary communication. A staged change by group offers a safer test than an immediate domain-wide restriction. Before changing it, map legitimate learner and educator space-creation patterns so a governance fix does not quietly break coursework or clubs.

## Sceptic's corner

The week's tempting conclusion is that better controls make agents ready for school. They make agents *possible to govern*; that is a smaller claim.

Google's Studio update is unusually concrete, but its own migration note shows why a control list can mislead. If protections initially cover new flows while old ones persist, two automations performing the same task can sit under different rules. Human confirmation can become a reflex click. DLP can be configured too narrowly. An audit trail can prove that an agent acted without proving that the action helped anyone. The new Gemini-in-Chat dashboard illustrates the measurement version of the same error: observable activity is administratively useful, but more prompts, summaries or active users do not establish time saved, better decisions or stronger learning.

Nor do today's research papers close that gap. The lecture-retrieval pilot reports engagement, not unaided performance; LearnAI reports attractive artefacts from a tiny paired sample; benchmark work warns that even familiar scores may not carry familiar meaning. Schools should resist converting governability into endorsement.

A minimum viable agent is therefore organisational, not technical: a named owner; a bounded data and action scope; explicit approval before consequential output; an audit route someone will actually inspect; a transfer or learning measure beyond usage; and a retirement rule when the owner leaves or the platform changes. If a pilot cannot answer those six questions, the right response to a richer control panel is not faster adoption. It is a better-shaped pilot.
