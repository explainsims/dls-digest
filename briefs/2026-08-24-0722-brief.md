## Top story

**Passing the governance test did not stop the breach.** Hong Kong’s Privacy Commissioner closed its investigation of the May Canvas incident on 20 August with a finding that the four affected institutions had taken all practicable steps under the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. City University, the Hong Kong Academy for Performing Arts, the Hong Kong Institute of Construction and HKUST had conducted pre-deployment security reviews, used contractual safeguards and monitored third-party assessments. The regulator found no contravention by them. The same report confirms that 146,969 CityU identities, 4,584 HKAPA identities and 2,333 HKIC identities were affected — at least 153,886 people before HKUST’s still-pending count is added ([Hong Kong PCPD, 20 August 2026](https://www.pcpd.org.hk/english/news_events/media_statements/press_20260820.html)).

The mechanism is more useful than the verdict. ShinyHunters submitted malicious code through a Free-for-Teacher support request, exploited a cross-site-scripting flaw, captured an authorisation token, elevated access and exfiltrated data on 29 April. After access was blocked, the attacker used another vulnerability on 7 May to re-enter Canvas and deface institutional login pages. Instructure says it fixed the flaws, discontinued Free-for-Teacher, engaged CrowdStrike and retrieved the stolen data after reaching an agreement with the attacker. The institutions’ internal systems were not affected.

For international schools, this is not a referendum on one LMS. It is a sharper test of the controls around every hosted learning platform. The PCPD’s remedy list is practical: minimise stored personal data, define access and retention, require rapid incident notification, review logs and third-party controls continuously, and enable multifactor authentication. The uncomfortable lesson is that due diligence, contracts and monitoring can make an institution legally diligent without making its supplier technically unbreachable. Procurement evidence is necessary. Data minimisation is what limits the invoice when the evidence fails.

## Other stories

- **South Korea will give paid generative-AI access and training to 120,000 disadvantaged and rural high-school students.** The Education Ministry and Korea Student Aid Foundation announced the new AI On-ieum programme on 19 August, with school applications closing on 28 August. Priority goes first to students receiving education benefits or covered by regional education-welfare programmes; rural high schools may then apply for school-wide provision, giving every enrolled student access if selected. The package combines subscriptions to multiple commercial generative-AI services with a staged education programme, and students must complete preliminary training that includes AI ethics before using the services ([Republic of Korea policy briefing, 19 August 2026](https://www.korea.kr/news/customizedNewsView.do?keyType=KW02&newsId=148970284)). This is an equity intervention with a refreshingly concrete denominator, but access is still the input, not the result. The announcement names neither vendors nor selection measures, cost, duration or learning outcomes. For international schools, the travelling question is narrower: when AI capability differs by family subscription, has the school created an instructional entitlement or merely permitted a consumer advantage?

- **The US Education Department has told schools to demand independent evidence, not just responsible design.** Its six-page Dear Colleague letter of 20 August distinguishes instructional technology from recreational screen use and asks schools to judge tools by learning outcomes, cost, usability, accessibility, interoperability, implementation support and educator experience. Providers should publish independent evaluations where feasible, disclose limitations and support implementation; districts should bring evidence into procurement and renewal, then review outcomes rather than treating adoption or usage as success. The letter explicitly names randomised controlled trials, third-party evidence, educator and parent feedback, implementation reviews and local outcome data as different contributions to a decision ([US Department of Education, 20 August 2026](https://www.ed.gov/media/document/guidance-education-technology-and-screentime-schools-114386.pdf)). The sensible part travels beyond the US: ask what problem a product solves, for whom, when, for how long, and with what learning evidence. The caveat is that an evidence hierarchy is not an implementation plan; a credible trial elsewhere does not prove a tool survives local timetables, language profiles or teacher workload.

- **AI literacy is moving from prompt tips to deliberate encounters with failure.** An Associated Press report published on 21 August describes Charleston County School District’s two-stage approach: first policy and consultation with teachers, students and parents, then training for teachers and middle- and high-school students. The district serves about 50,000 students. Utah, meanwhile, has trained more than 7,000 teachers — almost a third of its public-school instructors — through a state AI specialist post, with an eight-person team delivering more than 150 sessions supported by a US$500,000 healthcare-provider grant. The classroom grain is the useful bit: teachers demonstrate a generated world map full of misspellings and invented labels, then teach verification, privacy and bias through visible defects ([Associated Press, 21 August 2026](https://apnews.com/article/ai-literacy-schools-education-4fb9f2c0240993499870f4f204bf41c1)). That is better literacy than a tour of buttons, provided the lesson moves beyond laughing at an easy hallucination. Students also need to recognise the harder failure: an answer that is fluent, plausible and only slightly wrong.

- **A new child-facing advertising benchmark found disclosure missing from 45.5% of its YouTube sample.** Utrecht, Maastricht and Radboud researchers submitted ChildSafeAds on 19 August: 3,360 videos from 939 channels, beginning with sponsor segments independently marked by SponsorBlock users and paired with transcripts, channel data and linked sales pages. Systems must identify the offer, assign a product category and flag legal risks across four cumulative evidence levels, from transcript alone to the external page. GPT-5.4 generated labels after the organisers iterated on taxonomy and prompts; GPT-5.6 Luna independently labelled the development set ([Bertaglia et al., arXiv, submitted 19 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19165)). It is a shared-task description, not a completed evaluation: participating-system results are promised in a later version, and crowdsourced sponsor markings shape the candidate pool. Still, the 45.5% disclosure figure is a useful media-literacy warning. Platform labels cannot be the sole teaching signal when nearly half the sampled commercial segments failed to use them properly.

- **Google is replacing Gemini’s Chat side panel with a command surface, and the history will not follow it.** Announced on 19 August for rollout beginning 26 August, Ask Gemini in Google Chat will find files, summarise conversations and capture action items through Workspace Intelligence. The existing side panel disappears for affected English-language accounts, Gems remain available in other Workspace apps, and prior side-panel conversation history will not migrate. Administrators can export that history; users may download it where organisational policy allows. The feature is on by default when both Gemini in Workspace for Chat and Workspace Intelligence are enabled, and promotional higher limits run only until 1 October before usage limits apply ([Google Workspace Updates, 19 August 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/)). This is a migration story disguised as a feature launch. Before colleagues discover the new shortcut, international schools need the dull answers: whether both prerequisites are enabled, whether old history matters, and what the post-promotion ceiling actually is.

## On the radar

- **OpenAI cut GPT-5.6 Sol’s API and credit price by more than 20% for three months.** The 21 August update does not yet rewrite the displayed base table, which still lists Sol at US$5 per million input tokens and US$30 per million output tokens ([OpenAI, updated 21 August 2026](https://openai.com/index/gpt-5-6/)). Temporary discounts are useful for experiments and dangerous for annual budgets: record both the promotional rate and the reversion date before a prototype quietly becomes infrastructure. Compare the post-discount cost with cheaper tiers, and keep the workload’s educational value separate from the prestige of the model name.

- **A science platform has launched an embedded tutor that refuses to supply answers.** Penda Learning’s 19 August release says Cosmos offers preset routes for hints, explanations, summaries and step-by-step coaching, stores timestamped chat history, lets teachers inspect usage, can be disabled by student or assignment and switches off during assessments ([Penda Learning, 19 August 2026](https://www.pendalearning.com/news/penda-learning-releases-cosmos)). Those are better product decisions than a blank chat box. The evidence claim remains vendor-authored; no learning evaluation of Cosmos appears in the release. Also absent are the base model, retention terms, training-data treatment and error rates across learner groups — the questions that turn a promising interface into a reviewable product.

- **A school-board campaign has turned AI policy into an electoral pledge.** About half of Chicago’s board candidates had signed a parent-group proposal by 18 August calling for a three-year generative-AI moratorium, a chief privacy officer, screen-time limits and tighter biometric-data rules. Chicago Public Schools disputes the group’s description of district deployments, while one candidate declined because a moratorium could impede AI literacy ([Axios Chicago, 18 August 2026](https://www.axios.com/local/chicago/2026/08/18/cps-board-candidates-asked-to-sign-ai-pledge-)). Watch the structure: tool-specific questions are becoming governance-platform questions voters can understand. A three-year ban is a blunt instrument; the demand beneath it — a public inventory, privacy ownership and age-banded limits — is harder for a district to dismiss.

## Sceptic's corner

The easy reading of Hong Kong’s Canvas finding is that the institutions did everything right and were simply unlucky. The regulator found pre-assessments, contracts and continuous monitoring; it found no breach of the ordinance by the four institutions. That is meaningful. It is also a compliance conclusion, not a reliability certificate.

The disclosed controls mostly describe what an institution can inspect before purchase and require on paper afterwards. None would necessarily expose a support-form cross-site-scripting vulnerability before an attacker did. Nor does retrieving stolen data erase the copies, credentials or downstream phishing risk. The report’s strongest recommendations therefore move from assurance to exposure: transfer only necessary data, set retention periods, constrain access, use multifactor authentication and monitor logs ([Hong Kong PCPD, 20 August 2026](https://www.pcpd.org.hk/english/news_events/media_statements/press_20260820.html)).

There is a second trap. The US evidence letter may tempt schools to build a handsome procurement rubric and call the work mature. Independent trials, privacy terms and security assessments are all useful, but they answer different questions. A product may improve learning in a trial, satisfy privacy law and still fail operationally through a supplier flaw. Conversely, a safe platform may add no learning at all. The mature position is not to demand one perfect badge. It is to keep four ledgers separate — learning effect, implementation burden, data exposure and technical assurance — and renew only when each still earns its place. That is less elegant than a maturity badge, but considerably more honest about what a school actually knows.
