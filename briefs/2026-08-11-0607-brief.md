## Top story

**New South Wales is trying to make authorship visible again.** Education Minister Prue Car has reportedly asked the NSW Education Standards Authority for an interim moratorium on unsupervised take-home assessment tasks in the Higher School Certificate. The proposal is subject to NESA advice and is expected to begin next term for the incoming Year 12 cohort; major projects in areas such as art and technology may need exceptions. No confirming NESA notice was visible at filing, so this is a reported proposal, not yet a settled rule ([Sydney Morning Herald, 9 August](https://www.smh.com.au/national/nsw/take-home-assessments-to-be-banned-in-radical-hsc-overhaul-20260806-p60m44.html)).

The pressure behind it is concrete. The report says NSW recorded 1,270 malpractice cases in HSC school-based tasks in 2025, with most involving take-home work. It also cites [Learning First’s May survey of almost 3,400 NSW teachers](https://learningfirst.com/research/aiuseinschools), in which, among teachers reporting student AI use, 80% of Years 7–10 teachers and 73% of Years 11–12 teachers had seen it used to complete assessment tasks. These are teacher reports, not a measured prevalence rate across all pupils, but they explain the urgency. Final examinations account for half of the HSC mark and school-based tasks the other half, so removing unsupervised assessment would alter a substantial part of the credential rather than add another plagiarism warning. NESA’s current guidance already requires submitted work to be a student’s own or appropriately acknowledged and says a suspected student may have to produce notes, plans, progressive drafts or explain the work orally ([NESA, current 2026 guidance](https://www.nsw.gov.au/education-and-training/nesa/hsc/rules-and-procedures/2026-guide/before-you-start-your-hsc)).

The mechanism matters more than the headline. NSW is shifting from trying to detect an invisible tool after submission to controlling where evidence is produced. That strengthens confidence in authorship, but it also risks shrinking assessment toward timed writing and tests unless schools protect long-form research, design, performance and accessibility through supervised stages, retained process evidence and viva voce checks.

For international schools, the proposal is useful as a comparator rather than a template. A blanket take-home ban would flatten important differences between disciplines and programmes. The sharper design question is this: which claim about learning does each task make, and what observable evidence would let a teacher defend that claim when AI assistance is ordinary?

## Other stories

- **A capable agent now fits on one well-equipped local machine.** Meta released [Muse Glimmer](https://research.meta.ai/blog/introducing-muse-glimmer-open-agentic-model) on 10 August: a 30-billion-parameter, open-weights model under Apache 2.0, trained for local agents, function calling, coding, image-and-text input and failure recovery. Meta says roughly four-bit quantisation reduces the language model from more than 55 GB at full precision to under 20 GB, leaving room for its working memory, image encoder and speculative-decoding component inside a 24 GB or 32 GB hardware envelope. Its DFlash companion proposes blocks of tokens for the main model to verify in parallel; Meta reports decode-speed gains of 3.1 times on an RTX 5090, 1.8 times on an M5 Max and 1.5 times on an M4 Max. It is trained across more than 100 languages and was assessed under Meta’s Advanced AI Scaling Framework, but the published comparisons and speed tests remain vendor evaluations, not school-task evidence. For Ed Tech Coaches, the release creates a credible route to prototype locally without sending every prompt to a model provider. That changes the privacy and recurring-cost conversation; it does not end it. Model files, tool permissions, logs, patches, hardware access and evaluation become the school’s responsibility. Local is a deployment property, not a safety certificate.

- **Google has put rubric drafting inside the assignment screen.** From 5 August, teachers over 18 can ask Gemini to generate a Classroom-ready rubric from the assignment context, review and edit it, then attach it without moving through a separate document. Existing rubric files can still be converted, and rubrics made in the Gemini tab can still be exported to Docs or Sheets. Google says the feature is available across Education Fundamentals, Standard and Plus, with a full rollout taking one to three days, and is on by default wherever Gemini in Classroom is enabled ([Google Workspace Updates, 5 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/streamlining-rubric-generation-in-Google-Classroom-with-Gemini.html)). This is useful workflow compression, but the word *relevant* is doing a lot of work. A fluent rubric can still reward what is easy to phrase rather than what the task is meant to reveal. Comparing one generated draft against an existing strong rubric would make a useful, tightly bounded PD calibration exercise. Teachers should supply the learning intention and inspect every criterion, level distinction and weighting before the rubric becomes the assessment contract.

- **Google Meet notes can now preserve the slide as well as the speech.** The 3 August rollout lets Gemini automatically insert screenshots of presented content into the document created by Take notes for me. Administrators can allow screenshots whenever AI notes run or restrict them to meetings that are also recorded; presenters receive an alert and can disable capture from the notes panel. The feature is available to Google AI Pro for Education, with a gradual rollout of up to 15 days ([Google Workspace Updates, 3 August](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/visual-screenshots-now-included-in-Google-Meet-meeting-notes.html)). The gain is real: a transcript that says *this graph* without the graph is weak evidence. The governance change is just as real. A fleeting slide containing student information, an unreleased plan or a colleague’s draft can now become a persistent, shareable object. Schools should treat the screenshot setting as a retention decision, not merely a note-quality preference.

- **Washington is splitting child-AI safety into concrete control layers.** On 5 August, the US Senate Commerce Committee put the Youth AI Privacy Act, the CHATBOT Act and the Children’s Artificial Intelligence Toy Safety Act on one [markup agenda](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/meetings/executive-session-24-08-05-2026/). The committee page records the agenda, not the disposition, so none should be reported as law. Their mechanisms differ sharply. The [Youth AI Privacy Act](https://www.markey.senate.gov/news/press-releases/markey-introduces-legislation-to-protect-children-from-privacy-and-safety-risks-posed-by-ai-chatbots) would limit memory used to personalise replies to minors, prohibit training on their personal data, ban advertising and profiling, and restrict engagement features. The [CHATBOT Act](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/press/rep/release/cruz-schatz-curtis-schiff-introduce-new-bill-giving-parents-control-over-kids-ai-chatbot-use/) centres family accounts, parental consent and access to conversations. The [toy proposal](https://blakemoore.house.gov/media/press-releases/congressman-blake-moore-introduces-bill-to-ban-artificial-intelligence-chatbots-in-childrens-toys) takes the hardest line: banning manufacture, import, sale or distribution of children’s toys containing AI chatbots. This is useful because *child safety* too often becomes a fuzzy procurement phrase. International schools can turn the bills into questions for approved-tool reviews now: what is remembered, what trains the model, what drives return use, and who can inspect a conversation? The tensions matter too. Parent monitoring creates its own privacy risk, and a ban designed for an always-listening toy does not transfer automatically to a teacher-directed tool.

## On the radar

- Google Drive now supports [timestamped comments on videos](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/provide-more-clear-contextual-feedback-with-timestamped-comments-in-Google-Drive-videos.html): a comment can be anchored to a moment, appears as a marker on the player timeline and retains replies, mentions, editing and resolution. The 3 August release is on for all Workspace customers with no admin switch. This could remove the parallel timecode document from student film, recorded-presentation and staff-training review. Because creation is a web feature while mobile shows file-level comments, test the student handoff before standardising the workflow. Watch whether assessment feedback remains exportable and reviewable after a course closes.

- Colorado State University ran an [AI-contracts workshop](https://tilt.colostate.edu/events/cultivating-student-growth-in-the-age-of-ai/) on 10 August, framing the agreement as a way to keep AI use directed toward student growth rather than replacement of learning. The page offers no published artefact, participant evidence or outcome yet. If a usable template appears, the interesting parts will be task-specific boundaries, what evidence students retain, how consent and accessibility work, what happens when the tool changes mid-course, and whether the contract creates a genuine learning conversation rather than another policy students click past. A contract should be piloted with one real task, not pasted across a course.

- The inaugural [Cambridge AI in Education Summit](https://aisummit.cambridge.org/summit2026/) reached its abstract-acceptance date on 10 August for an October programme organised around human intelligence, connection, assessment evidence, judgment, responsibility, access and equity. Those themes are unusually well balanced; the programme will show whether they become competing arguments or seven polite tracks. Watch for school-based studies with measures of learning and teacher workload, not only principles or product demonstrations. The useful programme signal will be sessions that publish methods or artefacts a school can test, not merely a compelling abstract.

## Sceptic's corner

The easy response to the NSW proposal is relief: if the work is done under supervision, the authorship problem disappears. It does not. It becomes smaller, more visible and more expensive.

Supervised conditions can establish that a student produced a particular performance at a particular time. They are much weaker at capturing sustained inquiry, revision, collaboration, design decisions and the kind of long-form intellectual work that improves because a learner can leave it, return and think again. If the answer to AI is to convert every consequential task into timed individual production, schools may protect the credential while narrowing the curriculum the credential is supposed to represent. Students requiring assistive technology, flexible pacing or distance access also make *in the room* a poor proxy for fairness.

The alternative is not a detector. It is an evidence stack: observed classroom checkpoints, version history that is actually reviewed, source notes, short oral defence, an unannounced transfer task, and a final product whose permitted AI role is explicit. Different disciplines need different combinations. A visual-arts body of work should not be authenticated like a mathematics response; neither should a collaborative investigation be forced into the shape of an examination.

The NSW move is useful because it admits that warnings and post-hoc suspicion are no longer enough. Its value will depend on whether the temporary moratorium creates time to redesign evidence, or quietly becomes the redesign.
